# 05 — Mock Interview: Data Engineering Manager (M5)

> **Lesson 5 of 5 — Practice** · ~20 min

A full mock at the EM M5 bar (Data Engineering Manager).
Sam has been a Tech Lead for 2 years and is now making the
transition to people management.

The EM bar is *qualitatively* different from the IC bar. The
interviewer is no longer looking for *technical leadership* —
they're looking for **people leadership**, **hiring judgment**,
**performance management**, **career development**, and
**team-level operational excellence**. The stories shift
from "I built" to "my team built, and I enabled it."

---

## 1. The setup

- **Candidate:** Sam, 12 years experience, currently Tech
  Lead on a data platform team at a public SaaS company.
  2 years of tech-lead experience, 0 years of formal
  people management.
- **Interviewer:** Pat, Data Engineering Manager at a
  large tech company, 6 years of management experience.
- **Round:** 55 minutes, 6 questions, behavioral only.
- **Bar:** M5 (Manager). The interviewer will be looking
  for *people* signals, not technical signals.

---

## 2. The transcript

### Q1: "Tell me about yourself."

> *"I'm a data engineer with 12 years of experience. The
> last 2 years I've been a tech lead on a 6-person data
> platform team, which is where I discovered that the
> work I love most is *enabling* a team to do their
> best work — not doing the technical work myself. The
> project I'm proudest of as a tech lead is the
> migration of our team from per-team batch pipelines
> to a shared streaming platform, which was successful
> because the team owned the work, not because I did
> it.*
>
> *Three things I'd want you to know about me. First,
> I think a lot about how to create the conditions for
> a team to do great work — the operational
> environment, the technical environment, the
> psychological safety. Second, I've gotten good at
> calibrating performance — I've had to have 3
> performance conversations in the last year, two
> positive and one constructive, and the framework I
> developed for them has become a template my
> colleagues use. Third, I care a lot about career
> development — I spend 1:1 time on it explicitly,
> not as a side effect of project work.*
>
> *I'm talking to you about this role because I want
> to do the people-management work full-time, and
> your team is doing the kind of data platform work
> I want to support."*

**Analysis:** 75 seconds. The headline is honest
(tech lead, not yet a manager). The signature work
*attributes success to the team*, not the candidate
— a strong manager signal. The 3 signals are all
*people* signals. The "why here" is specific to the
*people work*, not the technical work. 4/4.

### Q2: "Tell me about a time you had to develop someone
on your team."

> *"One of the engineers on my team — let's call her
> Aisha — was a strong individual contributor who
> wanted to grow into a tech-lead role. She had the
> technical skills but no experience in stakeholder
> management or project planning.*
>
> *I worked with her over 6 months on a deliberate
> development plan. The plan had 3 components: (1)
> she would own the technical design for a new
> project (with me reviewing, not co-owning); (2)
> she would present the design to the 3 cross-team
> stakeholders, with me sitting in the back of the
> room for backup; (3) she would lead the project's
> weekly status updates, with me shadowing the
> first 2 and then stepping away.*
>
> *What I learned as her manager: the first
> stakeholder presentation was rough. Aisha was
> technically crisp but the PM打断ed her twice and
> she lost her thread. We debriefed after. She
> prepared more for the next one — wrote out the
> 3 questions she expected, rehearsed her answers
> with me, and brought printed handouts. The second
> presentation went well. The third was excellent.*
>
> *Within 9 months, Aisha was independently running
> the design and stakeholder management for her
> project. She's now a tech lead on a different
> team, which is what she wanted. The development
> plan I used with her is the template I now use
> for any engineer who wants to grow into a tech-
> lead role."*

**Analysis:** EM-caliber. The development is
*deliberate* (3 components, 6 months). The
*specific* failures and recoveries are named (the
rough first presentation). The candidate is
*honest about their own role* (shadowing, not
co-owning). The outcome is *measurable and
promotable* (Aisha is now a tech lead). The
template propagated. 4/4.

### Q3: "Tell me about a time you had to give someone
difficult feedback."

> *"I had a senior engineer on my team — let's call
> him Brian — whose code review responsiveness had
> been declining for 3 months. He was averaging 4
> days per review, which was blocking 3 other
> engineers on the team. I had avoided the
> conversation for 6 weeks because I knew Brian
> had a reputation for being defensive.*
>
> *What I did: I scheduled a 1:1, framed it as 'I
> want to share an observation, not a criticism,
> and I want to understand if I'm reading this
> right.' I brought 3 specific data points (PR
> timestamps, days outstanding). I asked if there
> was something I was missing.*
>
> *He told me he had been dealing with a family
> situation that was consuming his attention
> outside work hours. He hadn't realized it was
> visible to the team. We agreed on a 2-week plan:
> he would batch his reviews into 2 windows per
> day (morning and afternoon), which would let
> him respond within 4 hours during his focused
> windows and let the team know when to expect
> feedback. We also agreed that I would help
> triage anything urgent.*
>
> *The reviews improved within 2 weeks. Median
> review time dropped to 18 hours. Brian came
> back to me a month later and said the
> conversation was the moment he realized he
> needed to be more transparent with me about
> what was going on. We've been more open since."*

**Analysis:** EM-caliber. The avoidance is
*named* (the candidate had been avoiding for 6
weeks — honest self-awareness). The conversation
is *tactical* (1:1, specific framing, specific
data). The candidate *listens* and discovers the
real cause. The fix is *specific* (batch reviews,
2 windows). The outcome is *measurable* (18
hours median) and *cultural* (Brian now
transparently surfaces issues). 4/4.

### Q4: "Tell me about a hiring decision you were
involved in that you got wrong."

> *"I was on a hiring loop for a senior data
> engineer 6 months ago. The candidate was
> technically excellent — they aced the coding
> round, the system design was reasonable, and
> their behavioral answers were polished.*
>
> *I was the closer, and I voted hire. I should
> not have.*
>
> *What I missed: I didn't probe their *teamwork*
> deeply enough. In the behavioral round, their
> stories were all 'I built' and 'I led' — no
> 'I was wrong and learned' or 'I disagreed and
> synthesized.' I rationalized it as 'senior
> people have senior stories, junior stories
> would feel artificial.' That was wrong. The
> signals I should have weighted more heavily
> were: (1) the absence of any failure story with
> specific growth, (2) the absence of any conflict
> story with a charitable framing of the other
> person, (3) the absence of any 'I learned from
> someone more junior' moment.*
>
> *We hired them. Within 3 months it was clear
> they were the source of 2 of the 3 team conflicts
> on the team. Within 6 months we parted ways.
> The cost to the team was significant — 2 of the
> affected engineers asked to transfer to other
> teams.*
>
> *What I changed: I now weight 'absence of growth
> signals' as a stronger negative than I used to.
> The specific heuristic: if a senior candidate
> can't tell a clean failure story with specific
> growth, that is a *hiring red flag*, not a
> 'they're just not used to behavioral interviews.'
> I've used this heuristic on 4 subsequent loops
> and it's caught 1 of the 4 candidates who would
> have been similar misses."*

**Analysis:** EM-caliber. The candidate *owned*
the miss explicitly. The reasoning behind the
miss is *named* (rationalized the signals
away). The cost to the team is *specific*
(2 transfers). The new heuristic is *named* and
*measured* (1 of 4 subsequent candidates caught).
4/4.

### Q5: "How do you handle a team member who is
consistently underperforming?"

> *"I'd handle it in 4 phases, with the goal of
> either getting them to performance or out of
> the role, with respect and transparency, in a
> defined timeframe.*
>
> *Phase 1: clarify expectations. I'd have a
> 1:1 where I describe specifically what I'm
> seeing (data, not adjectives) and ask for
> their perspective. The conversation is
> collaborative — they may have context I'm
> missing. Outcome: a shared understanding of
> the gap and a 30-day plan to close it.*
>
> *Phase 2: 30-day check-in. If the gap is
> closing, continue. If not, escalate to a
> Performance Improvement Plan (PIP). The PIP
> has specific, measurable goals, a 60-day
> timeline, and weekly check-ins. It's not
> a surprise — I tell them in Phase 1 that
> the next step is a PIP if the gap doesn't
> close.*
>
> *Phase 3: PIP execution. Weekly check-ins,
> transparent about whether the trajectory is
> toward passing or failing. If passing,
> continue to monitor. If failing, transition
> to separation.*
>
> *Phase 4: separation. Done with respect,
> done with severance appropriate to tenure,
> done with clear documentation.*
>
> *The thing I want to emphasize: this is not
> a punitive process. It's a clarity process.
> The underperformer often *wants* the clarity
> and either rises to it or uses it to realize
> the role isn't the right fit. Either is a
> good outcome. The worst outcome is *drift* —
> months of underperformance that everyone
> pretends isn't happening."*

**Analysis:** EM-caliber. The answer is a
*framework* with 4 named phases. The
*transparency* is emphasized (no surprises).
The *worst outcome* is named (drift). The
candidate frames the process as a *clarity
process*, not a *punitive* one. 4/4.

### Q6: "What questions do you have for me?"

> *"Three things. First, what's the biggest
> people-leverage thing you've done as a
> manager — not a technical thing, a people
> thing? Second, what does 'good' look like
> for a manager on your team in the first 6
> months — what behaviors would I be
> demonstrating if I were ramping well? And
> third, what's the most common people-
> management mistake you've seen new
> managers make on this team, and what would
> you want me to do differently?"*

**Analysis:** All three are EM-caliber questions
that signal *managerial humility* (asking about
mistakes) and *managerial curiosity* (asking
about the role's first 6 months). The first
question is *exceptional* — it asks the
interviewer to talk about their own best work,
which is a strong rapport-building move. 4/4.

## 3. Overall assessment

**6 answers, all 4/4. Net: 24/24.**

This is a **strong hire at the M5 bar**. The
candidate:

- Attributes team success to the team, not to
  themselves
- Tells *honest* people-management stories,
  including a hiring miss
- Names a *framework* (4-phase underperformance
  process)
- Asks *managerial* reverse-interview questions
  that signal EM-level humility

The hiring committee would put this in the
"strong M5, ready to start" bucket. The
candidate would get an offer.

## 4. The IC-to-EM shift

| IC framing | EM framing |
|---|---|
| "I built the system" | "My team built the system, and I enabled it" |
| "I made the call" | "I created the conditions for the team to make the call" |
| "I was the one who..." | "I was the one who... the team..." (always include the team) |
| "I learned X" | "I developed Aisha through a 6-month plan and she's now a tech lead" |
| "I disagreed with X" | "I had to give Brian difficult feedback about his review responsiveness" |
| "I'd want to know about the team's technical work" | "I'd want to know about your biggest people-leverage moment" |

The shift is from *I* to *we*. From *technical
outcomes* to *people outcomes*. From *learning* to
*developing others*. From *disagreement* to *giving
feedback*.

If you're transitioning from IC to EM, your story
bank needs to be re-told through this lens. The
underlying events are usually the same. The framing
is different. See
`02_theory/05_calibration.md` for the calibration
implications.

---

## Try it

If you're interviewing for an EM role, take your
top 3 IC stories and reframe them as *team*
stories. The "I" becomes "the team." The "I
learned" becomes "I helped X learn." The "I
delivered" becomes "the team delivered."

If you can't reframe a story this way without
losing the truth of it, the story may have been
*too individual* to count as EM material. Find a
different one.
