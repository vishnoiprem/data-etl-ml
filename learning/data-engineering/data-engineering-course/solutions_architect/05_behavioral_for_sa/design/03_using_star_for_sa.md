# 03 — Using the STAR Framework (refresher for SA)

> **Lesson 3 of 11 — Behavioral for SAs** · ~15 min

The STAR framework, calibrated for SA interviews. The
customer is the protagonist. Customer outcomes are
quantified. Reflection is mandatory. This is the
*spine* of every behavioral answer in the SA loop.

---

## 1. The STAR framework

| Step | What to do | Duration | SA-specific adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S — Situation** | Set the scene. What team, what customer, what was at stake? | 10-15 sec | Name the customer (or anonymize with specificity). |
| **T — Task** | What was your specific responsibility? What outcome were you accountable for? | 5-10 sec | Your responsibility to the customer, not the team. |
| **A — Action** | Walk through what you did. Be specific: tools, decisions, communications. | 50-70 sec | Customer-facing actions: discovery, demos, whiteboarding, customer comms. |
| **R — Result** | Quantify. Customer outcome, deal size, NPS change, cost savings. | 15-20 sec | Customer outcome is the lead, not your personal outcome. |
| **Reflection** | What did you learn? What did you change? | 5-10 sec | Mandatory in SA interviews. |

Total: 90-120 seconds.

The 5 steps add up to 90-120 seconds. The Action step
is 50-70% of the time. Most candidates under-spend on
Action and over-spend on Situation.

---

## 2. The SA-specific adaptation

The 4 differences between the general STAR framework
and the SA-flavored version:

### Difference 1: Customer as protagonist

In the Situation step, name the customer. In the Task
step, frame the task as *your responsibility to the
customer*. In the Action step, the customer is the
audience for each action. In the Result step, the
customer outcome is the lead.

A general STAR answer (DE-flavored):

> *Situation: "We were migrating our data pipeline to
> the cloud."*
> *Task: "I was responsible for the migration."*
> *Action: "I led the migration, working with the
> platform team."*
> *Result: "We finished on time, under budget."*

A SA-flavored STAR answer:

> *Situation: "A Fortune 500 retail customer was
> migrating from on-prem Hadoop to a cloud data lake
> in 6 months. Their analytics team was blocked on
> the data ingestion step."*
> *Task: "I was the technical owner for the customer's
> cloud data lake architecture. I was accountable for
> the customer's analytics team being unblocked within
> 8 weeks."*
> *Action: "I led 3 customer-facing workshops to
> understand their workflow, designed a tailored
> architecture, and ran a 2-week POC that validated
> the design. I worked with the customer's platform
> team to set up the cloud environment and with our
> product team to enable a feature the customer
> needed."*
> *Result: "The customer's analytics team was unblocked
> in 7 weeks (one week ahead of plan). They went live
> 6 months later, reducing their infrastructure cost
> by 40% and accelerating their analytics delivery by
> 3x. The architecture we designed became the standard
> for 3 subsequent retail customers."*

The SA-flavored version names the customer, frames the
task as accountability to the customer, has customer-
facing actions, and quantifies the customer outcome.

### Difference 2: Quantified customer outcome

The Result step in a SA-flavored STAR answer has *both*
your outcome and the customer's outcome. The customer's
outcome is the lead.

A bad Result:

> *"It went well, the customer was happy."*

A good Result:

> *"The customer's analytics team was unblocked in 7
> weeks (one week ahead of plan), reducing their
> infrastructure cost by 40% and accelerating their
> analytics delivery by 3x. The architecture became
> the standard for 3 subsequent retail customers."*

The good Result has 3 specific numbers, a customer-
facing outcome, and a business impact (the standard
for 3 subsequent customers).

### Difference 3: Customer-facing actions

The Action step is 50-70% of the time, and the actions
should be *customer-facing*:

- Discovery calls
- Demos
- Whiteboarding sessions
- Customer workshops
- Customer email / Slack communications
- Customer escalations
- Co-development with the customer

A bad Action step (internal-only):

> *"I designed the architecture, wrote the code, and
> shipped the feature."*

A good Action step (customer-facing):

> *"I led 3 customer-facing workshops, designed the
> tailored architecture, ran a 2-week POC with the
> customer's data, and co-developed the integration
> with the customer's platform team."*

The good Action step names the *customer-facing* actions
specifically, not just the technical work.

### Difference 4: Mandatory reflection

The Reflection step is mandatory in SA interviews.
Without it, the story reads as "I did the work" without
"I grew from the work."

A bad story (no reflection):

> *"The deal closed, the customer was happy, end of
> story."*

A good story (with reflection):

> *"The transferable lesson: in any customer migration,
> the first 2 weeks should be 100% discovery, not
> architecture. We almost lost the deal because we
> tried to jump to a design before understanding the
> customer's workflow. I now insist on 2 weeks of
> pure discovery for any migration engagement."*

The reflection names the lesson and the change. It
signals "I'm a learning person, not just a doing
person."

## 3. The 4-step STAR process

The 4-step process for writing any STAR story:

### Step 1: Write the raw story

Write down everything you remember about the project,
in any order. Don't filter; just write.

### Step 2: Identify the customer

Name the customer (or anonymize: "a Fortune 500
financial-services customer, anonymized due to NDA").
The customer is the protagonist.

### Step 3: Reframe each section in SA-flavored language

Apply the 4 differences (customer as protagonist,
quantified customer outcome, customer-facing actions,
mandatory reflection).

### Step 4: Time yourself out loud

Tell the story. Time it. Adjust until it's 90-120
seconds.

---

## 4. The 5 common STAR mistakes (in SA interviews)

### Mistake 1: Too much situation

You spend 30-45 seconds setting the scene. By the time
you get to the action, you have 30-45 seconds left.
The story is lopsided.

**Fix:** Cap the Situation at 15 seconds. If you can't
compress it, the situation is too detailed.

### Mistake 2: Not enough action

You spend 20 seconds on the situation, 5 on the task,
15 on the result. The action — the core of the story —
is 5 seconds. The interviewer can't tell what you
actually did.

**Fix:** Cap the action at 50-70 seconds. Use the
saved time on action detail, not situation detail.

### Mistake 3: Vague result

The result is "it went well" or "the customer was
happy" or "we hit our goals." The interviewer reads
this as "I don't have a model of impact."

**Fix:** Quantify. Numbers, timeframes, customer-
specific outcomes.

### Mistake 4: No reflection

The result is the last sentence. There's no "what I
learned" or "what I changed." The interviewer reads
this as "I don't grow from experience."

**Fix:** End with a 5-10 second reflection. The lesson,
the change, the transferable insight.

### Mistake 5: Customer is generic

The customer is "a customer" or "the customer" or
"our customers." The interviewer reads this as "I
haven't worked closely with specific customers."

**Fix:** Name the customer (or anonymize specifically:
"a Fortune 500 healthcare customer, anonymized due
to NDA"). Generic references read as evasive.

## 5. A worked example: STAR story, 90 seconds

The story: A customer escalation in a pre-sales
engagement.

> **Situation (12 sec):** "A Fortune 500 financial-
> services customer — anonymized due to NDA — was
> evaluating our streaming product for a real-time
> fraud-detection pipeline. The deal was $2M/year, 3-
> year commitment, with 5 expansion opportunities
> identified."
>
> **Task (8 sec):** "I was the technical SA on the
> deal. I was accountable for the customer's
> evaluation going smoothly and for the architecture
> we proposed standing up to their CTO's review."
>
> **Action (55 sec):** "Three weeks into the
> evaluation, the customer's CTO pushed back hard on
> the architecture. They wanted a real-time feature
> store, and our proposed architecture had a 2-minute
> staleness on the feature store, which they said was
> unacceptable.
>
> I scheduled a 90-minute working session with the
> CTO and their senior engineers. I came prepared
> with 3 alternative architectures, each with a
> tradeoff analysis: cost, latency, operational
> burden. I also came with a recommendation — the
> middle option, which gave them sub-second staleness
> at a 30% higher cost — and a clear rationale for
> why I thought it was the right answer.
>
> The session went well. The CTO asked hard questions
> on each option. I answered what I could, and
> committed to follow up on what I couldn't by EOD
> the next day. The follow-up was a 4-page doc
> addressing the 3 hardest questions, with
> benchmarks from a similar financial-services
> customer."
>
> **Result (15 sec):** "The deal closed 2 months
> later at $2M/year. The customer went live 4 months
> after that. The architecture we designed became
> the reference for 4 subsequent financial-services
> customers."
>
> **Reflection (10 sec):** "The transferable lesson:
> when a senior stakeholder pushes back on an
> architecture, the move is to bring 3 options, not
> 1, and to be ready to defend each. The
> alternatives let the customer feel ownership of the
> final choice, which is what closes the deal. I now
> bring 3 options to every senior-stakeholder
> architecture review."

Total: 100 seconds. Clean, customer-flavored, quantified,
reflective. A 4/4 answer.

## Try it

Pick one story from your story bank (Lesson 02). Write
it as a STAR answer using the 4-step process. Time
yourself out loud. Adjust until it's 90-120 seconds.

Then run the "5 common STAR mistakes" checklist on your
answer. If you hit 0-1 mistakes, the story is in good
shape. If you hit 2-3, refine. If you hit 4-5, the
story needs more work.

Repeat for 3-5 stories in your bank. By the 5th, the
4-step STAR process will be in muscle memory.
