# 10 — Calibrations: The Org-Level Conversation

> **Lesson 10 of 21 — Performance Management** · ~20 min

How calibrations work, how to advocate for your team in a cal
session, and how to handle a "down-level" outcome. The
calibration is where the 4 boxes from Lesson 07 get distributed
across the org, and where the EM's advocacy is most tested.

---

## 1. What calibrations are

A calibration is a 60-90 minute meeting where a group of EMs
(sometimes 3-5, sometimes 10+) sit in a room and distribute the
4 boxes (Lesson 07) across everyone in the org or sub-org. Each
EM presents their direct reports, the group discusses, and the
ratings get adjusted to match the org-level distribution.

The structure:

1. **Pre-work.** Each EM submits perf reviews (Lesson 07) for
   all their directs 1-2 weeks before the cal session. The
   senior EM or director reads them in advance.
2. **The session.** Each EM presents 1-2 directs at a time
   (the strongest and weakest are usually first). The group
   discusses, asks questions, and the rating is finalized.
3. **Post-work.** The EMs go back to their directs and deliver
   the final ratings (Lesson 07).

The calibration session is where the org-level signal gets
generated. Without it, every EM is grading on their own curve,
and "Exceeds" means different things in different teams. With
it, the org has a consistent view of who the top performers
are (for promo and comp) and who the underperformers are (for
PIPs and performance plans).

The distribution that the senior EM or director is trying to
hit:

```
Exceeds:        10-15% of the org
Meets:          65-75%
Partially Meets: 10-15%
Doesn't Meet:   <5%
```

The hard constraint is the **ratio of Exceeds to Doesn't Meet**.
Most cal sessions force a 3-5:1 ratio, and the senior EM will
literally say "if you have 3 Exceeds, you can't have more than
1 Doesn't Meet." This is the moment where the EM's advocacy is
tested — if you want your 3 Exceeds to land, you have to
accept your 1 Doesn't Meet.

## 2. How to advocate for your team in a cal session

The cal session is a debate, not a verdict. The EM's job is to
make the case for their directs. The 5 moves that work:

### Move 1: Lead with the strongest piece of evidence

When presenting a direct for "Exceeds," open with the single
most compelling piece of evidence. Not "they did good work
this cycle" — "they owned the migration of 3 critical pipelines
with 0 downstream breakage, and the validation framework they
built is now used by 3 other teams."

The senior move: **name the rubric row, then name the
evidence**. "This is the People Growth row — they grew 2 E4s
who both got promoted this cycle. Here are the 2 specific
engineers and what each grew into."

### Move 2: Anticipate the counter-argument

If you know the committee is going to push back on a rating
("we think this is Meets, not Exceeds"), name the counter-
argument first. "I know the case for Meets is [the project
slipped timeline, the cross-functional feedback was mixed].
Here's why I think it's still Exceeds: [the specific
discriminator, with evidence]."

Pre-empting the counter-argument makes you look more credible
than being defensive about it.

### Move 3: Use peer comparisons

The committee can't tell if your direct is "Exceeds" without
knowing the cohort. The senior move is to name the comparison
explicitly: "In my 8 years of working with E5s, this is in
the top quartile on technical depth, top quartile on cross-
functional impact, and top decile on mentorship."

If you can't make the comparison credibly, the committee will
assume the rating is inflated.

### Move 4: Make the case in the room, not in the doc

The pre-work docs are read. The session is debated. The
senior move is to use the session for the live case — bring
the quotes, bring the metrics, bring the project names. The
EM who reads from the doc loses to the EM who tells a story.

### Move 5: Accept the outcome in the room

If the committee down-levels your direct, the senior move is
to accept the decision in the room and take it back to your
direct with a constructive framing. Fighting the decision in
the room — or sulking after — corrodes the cal process for
everyone.

The script for a down-level outcome:

> *"Okay, I hear the committee's feedback. I'll take it back
> to [direct] as Meets-with-growth-plan. The specific growth
> areas we'll work on are [X, Y]. We'll re-examine at the
> mid-cycle."*

---

## 3. How to handle a "down-level" outcome for a specific direct

Sometimes the committee down-levels a direct you advocated
hard for. The 3-step pattern:

1. **Process your own reaction first.** It's natural to feel
   defensive. Give yourself 24 hours before talking to the
   direct. The conversation you have with the engineer
   shouldn't carry your raw emotion.
2. **Find the signal in the feedback.** The committee saw
   something you didn't, or the case you made wasn't strong
   enough. Either way, the feedback is information. Ask the
   senior EM: "What specifically would have made this a
   strong Exceeds case?" The answer is your coaching agenda
   for the next cycle.
3. **Deliver to the direct with a growth plan.** "You got
   Meets this cycle. Here's the committee's feedback: [the
   specific gap]. Here's what we're going to do about it:
   [the specific plan, with timeline]. The next cycle is a
   different conversation if we close the gap."

The mistake new EMs make: passing the committee's feedback
verbatim to the direct ("the committee said you weren't
strategic enough"). The engineer hears "your manager didn't
fight for me" and the trust is broken. The senior move is to
own the message: "I advocated for Exceeds, the committee
thought Meets was more accurate, here's what we do next."

---

## 4. The "I have to fight for someone" tension

Sometimes the cal session is genuinely hard. You have a
direct who's a 7 out of 10 — solid, growing, not top
quartile — and the committee is pushing for "Partially Meets"
because your distribution has too many "Meets." The EM's
dilemma: fight for the rating, or accept the down-level to
protect the team's other Exceeds.

The senior move: **fight for the rating if you genuinely
believe the committee is wrong**. Don't fight to inflate
everyone — the committee will stop trusting you. But if
you have specific evidence that the direct is meeting the
"Meets" bar, say so, and let the committee decide.

The other senior move: **don't trade one direct's rating
for another's**. The cal session isn't a zero-sum
negotiation. If your team has 3 strong Exceeds and 1
borderline Doesn't Meet, you can have 3 Exceeds and 0
Doesn't Meet if the borderline person is a Meets. Don't
sacrifice a Meets to keep an Exceeds.

## 5. A worked example: the cal session transcript

A 4-person team. Sam is advocating for 2 Exceeds (Priya
and Aarav), 1 Meets (Jordan), and 1 Partially Meets (the
junior who joined 3 months ago and is still ramping).

> **Sam:** "First up, Priya, E5, Exceeds. The single most
> compelling piece of evidence: she reframed the dashboard
> quality issue from a series of bugs to a systemic
> problem, proposed a 6-month project, negotiated the
> timeline with the PM, and shipped it. The dashboard
> error rate went from 8% to 0.4%, and the top customer
> renewed at $4.2M. The validation framework is now used
> by 3 other teams. I know the counter-argument is that
> the project took 2 weeks longer than the renegotiated
> timeline. I think this is still Exceeds because the
> project wouldn't have existed without her — the previous
> 18 months of bug-fixing didn't fix the systemic issue.
> And the cross-functional impact is unusually high for an
> E5."
>
> **Senior EM:** "What's the E5 cohort look like for this
> bar?"
>
> **Sam:** "I've worked with 8 E5s in my career. Priya's
> in the top 2 on cross-functional impact, top 3 on
> technical depth, and top 1 on mentorship. She's
> explicitly credited by 2 of her mentees in their own
> promo packets."
>
> **Senior EM:** "I can see the case. What do others think?
> ... Okay, Exceeds stands. Next."

**What makes this land:** Sam names the strongest evidence
first, anticipates the counter-argument, makes a peer
comparison, and is ready to defend the case in the room.
The senior move is the specific numbers (8% to 0.4%, $4.2M,
3 other teams, 2 mentees' promo packets) — they make the
case concrete.

## 6. Canonical questions this lesson answers

From `docs/reference/em_interview_canonical_questions.md`:

- Behavioral #33: *"Tell me about a time when you had to
  advocate for your team."*
- Behavioral #52: *"Tell me about a time when you had to
  evaluate someone's performance."*
- Behavioral #76: *"Tell me about a time when you had to
  make an unpopular decision."*
- Behavioral #112: *"Tell me about a time when you had to
  influence a decision that you disagreed with."*

---

## Try it

If you have a real cal session coming up, use the 5 moves
above. If you don't, write a 90-second answer for the
question "Tell me about a time you had to advocate for
your team in a room of peers." Use a real situation, even
if it's not from a formal cal — a budget meeting, a
project prioritization, a hiring loop debrief all have the
same shape.

If you're an IC interviewing for an EM role, the equivalent
exercise: write a 90-second answer for "Tell me about a
time you had to influence a decision where you didn't have
authority." The advocacy muscle is the same.

---

## Action item

Before your next cal session, write 1 paragraph for each of
your directs that you'd say in the room, not just in the
doc. The paragraph should lead with the strongest evidence,
anticipate the counter-argument, and make the peer
comparison. The paragraph is what you'll actually say when
the senior EM asks "tell us about [direct]."
