# 01 — Welcome to our EM Course!

> **Lesson 1 of 6 — EM Introduction** · ~10 min

How to get the most out of this track, what the next 6 lessons
actually do, and the one question this whole track is designed
to help you answer.

---

## 1. What this track is

This is a **prose-only** guide. There is no service to spin up,
no system to design, no coding problem to grind. The "running
the test" is *you, thinking honestly about whether you want to
spend the next 10 years of your career as a manager of engineers
or as an engineer of systems.* That is the entire feedback loop.

The 6 lessons are organized around a single decision:

> **"Should I become an engineering manager?"**

By Sunday night, you should have a clear answer — yes, no, or
"not yet, and here's the evidence I need first." All three
answers are good outcomes. The bad outcome is the one most
engineers end up with: they become a manager by default,
because their manager suggested it and they didn't have a
framework to evaluate the offer.

This track gives you the framework.

---

## 2. Who this track is for

Calibrated for senior+ engineers who are *being asked* to
consider the management path. The "ask" comes in one of three
forms:

1. **Your manager says you'd be a good manager.** You've been
   shipping, mentoring, and running effective 1:1s, and someone
   above you has noticed. The natural next step is the manager
   ladder — but the ask is rarely explicit. You need a framework
   to evaluate whether they're right.
2. **You're restless in your IC role.** You've been a senior
   engineer for 2-3 years. The work that used to light you up
   — designing systems, debugging hard problems, writing
   elegant code — now feels repetitive. You wonder if management
   is the next challenge, or if it's just the next thing that
   pays more.
3. **You're interviewing and got an EM offer.** You applied for
   a senior IC role. The company came back with "we'd like to
   consider you for the EM track instead." You have 7-10 days
   to decide whether to take it. This track won't make the
   decision, but it will give you the vocabulary to ask the
   right questions.

If you have **less than 3 years of full-time experience**, this
track will still help, but the trade-offs are different. The
management track at E3/L4 is rarely a great idea — you're
giving up the years you'd otherwise spend building deep
technical judgment for a "manager" title that comes with 1-2
reports and a lot of process.

If you have **15+ years and are already Staff+**, Lesson 06's
"4 career archetypes" framework is probably the most valuable
part of the track for you. The IC-vs-manager question past L7
is genuinely hard, and the framework helps.

## 4. The #1 mistake candidates make

The single biggest mistake is **taking the management offer
without ever seriously considering the IC ladder they were
already on**. The path of least resistance at senior+ is to
become a manager. Your manager suggests it. Your skip-level
suggests it. The comp is similar (or higher). The title is
more senior. There's a manager-track req open next quarter.

But the IC track at senior+ is *also* a real path. Staff
Engineer, Principal Engineer, Senior Staff — these aren't
"manager-lite" titles. They're a different career, with a
different impact model, a different interview loop, and (at
some companies) a higher comp band than the EM track. If you
take the manager offer without ever considering the IC path,
you may spend the next 5 years in a role you didn't want,
doing work you're not great at, and you won't be able to
easily reverse it.

The minimum viable decision loop:

```
1. Read Lessons 01-02                             (45 min)
2. Take Lesson 03's self-check with a pen         (20 min)
3. Talk to 2 EMs you trust                        (30 min)
4. Talk to 1 senior IC you trust who *didn't*     (30 min)
   go the manager route
5. Decide: yes, no, or "I need 6 more months"     (5 min)
```

That's 2 hours. The mistake is making the decision in 2
minutes because the offer was on the table. The cost of the
mistake compounds for 5-10 years.

## 5. The mental model in 60 seconds

Read this once. Reread it after Lesson 03.

> **Management is a trade, not a promotion.** You are
> trading *your own output* (the systems you build) for
> *the output of 5-12 people* (the systems they build, with
> your help). The leverage is higher. The skill set is
> different. The reversibility is low. The best managers
> I know made the trade because they had evidence — from
> running 1:1s, mentoring interns, leading projects — that
> their leverage was already higher as a manager than as
> an IC. The worst managers I know made the trade because
> it was the next rung on the ladder.

If after 6 lessons you can defend that paragraph in your own
words — and you've decided yes, no, or "not yet" with
specific evidence — this track has done its job.

---

## 6. A note on authenticity

There's a real risk in this track: you read about manager
compensation, see the title ladder, talk to two EMs who
love their jobs, and convince yourself you want it. The
mimetic desire is real. "I want to be a manager" is often
"I want what my manager has," and the wanting is not the
same as the fit.

The honest version of the decision looks like this: you've
been doing 60% management work for the last 12 months
(mentoring, running 1:1s, scoping team projects, writing
review packets) and you found that work more energizing
than the 40% IC work (writing code, designing systems,
debugging). If that's true, management is for you. If the
40% is still your favorite part of the week, stay on the
IC ladder and aim for Staff.

Don't decide based on what sounds good in a Medium post.
Decide based on what you've *already* been doing.

## Try it

Before Lesson 02, set a 10-minute timer and answer these
questions in writing. Be honest. No one is going to read
this but you.

1. **What's the work I've been doing in the last 6 months
   that I most want to keep doing?** (Code? Design? Mentoring?
   Writing? Talking to customers? Running meetings?)
2. **What's the work I've been doing that I most want to
   stop doing?** (On-call? Status meetings? Code review?
   Design reviews? 1:1s? Sprint planning?)
3. **If I had to give a 90-second answer to "why do you
   want to be a manager," what would I say right now?**
   (Don't revise. First draft.)
4. **Who are the 2 EMs I most respect, and what specifically
   do they do that I want to learn?** (Names and one specific
   behavior each.)

If you can answer #1-#3 cleanly, you have a starting point
for Lesson 03's self-check. If #4 makes you draw a blank,
that's worth knowing too — it's a signal that you don't
yet have a model of what good management looks like, and
you should fix that *before* deciding to do it.
