# 02 — What is Engineering Management?

> **Lesson 2 of 6 — EM Introduction** · ~15 min

The 3 flavors of engineering management, what each one actually
does on a Tuesday morning, the trade-off you'd be making, and
what the comp looks like at FAANG.

---

## 1. The 3 flavors

"Engineering manager" is one job title that hides at least three
different careers. The work, the leverage, and the comp at each
level are *not* the same.

| Flavor | Level (rough) | Reports | Primary output | Skip-level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **People Manager (EM)** | E4-E5 (Meta) / L5-L6 (Google) / M5 (most) | 4-8 ICs | A high-performing team | Director / Sr. EM |
| **Tech-Lead Manager (TLM)** | E5 (Meta) / L6 (Google) / M5-M6 | 4-8 ICs, often with a tech lead on the team | A team that ships — both people *and* code | Director / Sr. EM |
| **Engineering Director (ED)** | E6 (Meta) / L7 (Google) / M6-M7 | 3-5 EMs | A high-performing org (2-4 teams) | VP / Sr. Director |

These are not titles you'd interview for interchangeably. A
People Manager is hired for people judgment. A TLM is hired for
people judgment *and* technical credibility. An ED is hired for
org judgment — the ability to set direction across multiple
teams. The interview loops are different. The bar is different.
The day-in-the-life is different.

A few things worth internalizing before you read on:

- **Most people who say "I want to be an EM" mean the first
  one** — the People Manager at E5/L6/M5. That's the most
  common path and the one this track focuses on. If you're
  targeting TLM or ED, see Lesson 06's career-archetype
  section.
- **The flavors blur at smaller companies.** A "Director of
  Engineering" at a 40-person startup might be a People
  Manager in all but title. The level-mappings above are
  for FAANG-scale orgs (1,000+ engineers). At a 100-person
  company, "Director" and "Sr. EM" are often the same job.
- **"Engineering Manager" ≠ "Manager of Engineers."** At some
  companies, the EM title is the people-manager role. At
  others, it's the tech-lead-manager role. Always read the
  JD. The 5 bullets under "what you'll do" tell you which
  flavor you're interviewing for.

## 2. Day-in-the-life at each level

The best way to understand the difference between the flavors
is to look at what they actually *do* on a Tuesday morning. The
shapes of the calendars are very different.

### People Manager, E4 (Meta) / L5 (Google) / M5 (most)

> **You manage 4-6 ICs. You do not write code.**

- **8:30-9:00** — Email triage. Yesterday's code review pings,
  calendar holds, an HR ticket about a PTO request, a question
  from a report about an internal transfer process.
- **9:00-9:30** — 1:1 with a report. They're stuck on a project.
  You listen. You help them name the real problem (it's not
  the project; it's the relationship with their PM). You don't
  solve it for them.
- **9:30-10:00** — Prep for a hiring loop debrief. Three
  candidates interviewed yesterday. You read the rubrics, write
  your own notes, then go around the room with the panel.
- **10:00-11:30** — Two 30-minute 1:1s back-to-back. One is
  a high-performer who wants to talk about promo packet timing.
  One is a struggling report who is in their 4th month of
  onboarding and still doesn't feel productive.
- **11:30-12:30** — Skip-level prep. Your director asked for
  a "team health" update. You write 5 bullets: 1 win, 1
  concern, 1 ask, 2 names to call out. This takes an hour
  because the 1 concern is hard to write honestly.
- **12:30-1:30** — Lunch. You eat at your desk because you
  have a perf review packet to finalize by EOD.
- **1:30-3:00** — Perf review packet writing. You have 2
  packets to submit this cycle. You spend 90 minutes on
  each, because the writing is the work. The packet is
  what gets the person promoted (or not) — not your
  recommendation alone.
- **3:00-4:00** — A "difficult conversation" with a report
  who missed their last two OKRs. You came in with notes.
  You leave with a 30-day plan and an agreement to revisit.
- **4:00-5:00** — Status meetings. A weekly staff meeting
  with your peer managers. A biweekly cross-team sync with
  the PM lead. A monthly all-hands.

The 8-hour day had **zero code written**. The output is
people: a perf packet, a 1:1 conversation, a hiring decision,
a skip-level update. The leverage is real — your 4-6 reports
will each ship 5-10x more than you would have shipped as an
IC. But the *kind* of work is different.

### Tech-Lead Manager, E5 (Meta) / L6 (Google) / M5-M6

> **You manage 4-8 ICs *and* you still write code, design
> systems, and review PRs.**

- **8:30-9:00** — Email + Slack. A PR review from yesterday
  needs a re-review after the author addressed your comments.
  A report is asking you to weigh in on a tech design.
- **9:00-9:30** — 1:1 with a report. They're ramping up on a
  part of the system you own. You spend 15 minutes on the
  system itself and 15 minutes on their growth.
- **9:30-11:00** — Coding. You're the tech lead on a migration.
  You write 80-150 lines of code, review 3 PRs, and unblock
  a report who's stuck on a schema decision.
- **11:00-12:00** — Design review. A new service your team is
  building. You're the most senior person in the room. You
  ask 4 questions, write the follow-up doc, and sign off.
- **12:00-1:00** — Lunch + reading.
- **1:00-2:00** — Hiring debrief. Same as the PM above.
- **2:00-3:30** — Two 1:1s. Same content, different shapes —
  the TLM's 1:1s are more likely to involve technical
  questions because the reports are still learning the system
  from you.
- **3:30-5:00** — A cross-team architecture review. You're
  presenting the migration plan to a peer team that will be
  affected. Two hours of meetings.

The TLM's day is roughly **40% people work, 50% technical
work, 10% meetings**. The split is the trade-off. You're
still shipping code, but you're shipping it at half the rate
you used to. Most TLMs find the trade worth it for 2-3 years
and then either move fully to PM (people work) or back to
Staff (technical work) once the team is ramped.

### Engineering Director, E6 (Meta) / L7 (Google) / M6-M7

> **You manage 3-5 EMs. You almost never write code.**

- **8:30-9:30** — Reading. Yesterday's skip-level notes from
  the 5 EMs you manage. The notes are 5 pages long. You read
  them carefully, mark 3 items for follow-up.
- **9:30-10:30** — 1:1 with one of your EMs. They're
  struggling with a report who's on a PIP. You help them
  think through the conversation they'll have this week.
- **10:30-12:00** — Strategy work. You're writing the
  half-year org plan: which teams are growing, which are
  shrinking, where the new bets are. The doc takes 6 weeks
  of partial effort to land.
- **12:00-1:00** — Lunch with a peer director. The
  relationship work — the unofficial part of the job that
  takes 30% of your time and is the actual lever for most
  cross-team projects.
- **1:00-2:30** — Hiring loop. You're the hiring manager for
  a new EM. Two candidates. You run the loop, write the
  packet, decide.
- **2:30-4:00** — A "skip-level" with the reports of one of
  your EMs (you don't usually 1:1 with the ICs). 6 people
  in a room, 60 minutes. You ask 3 questions, listen for
  50 minutes, take notes.
- **4:00-5:00** — A leadership review with your VP. The org
  plan, the hiring plan, the attrition numbers, the
  perf-calibration preview.

The ED's day is **80% people and org work, 20% strategy and
external (cross-org) work, 0% code**. The leverage is even
higher (you're now multiplying the output of 3-5 EMs, who
each multiply the output of 4-8 ICs) but the work is *even
further* from the systems. Most EDs I know haven't written
production code in 5+ years.

## 3. The IC vs manager trade-off

The single most important table in this track.

| Dimension | IC (Senior → Staff) | Manager (E5 → Director) |
|---|---|---|
| **What you ship** | Systems, code, design docs | People, decisions, packets |
| **Leverage model** | Your own output (deep) | Your team's output (wide) |
| **Skill set** | Technical depth, judgment, craft | People judgment, communication, scope |
| **Reversibility** | High — you can switch teams, switch stacks, switch companies and stay IC | Low — going back is possible (see Lesson 06) but it's a re-pitch, not a re-entry |
| **Feedback loop** | Fast — code compiles or doesn't, system scales or doesn't | Slow — a hire takes 6 months to know if you got it right; a perf decision takes 12 |
| **Failure mode** | Technical debt, missed deadlines | People churn, low-trust team, attrition |
| **Best signal of success** | "You ship things that work" | "People you used to manage become managers themselves" |
| **What you give up** | Team leverage, scope, people work | Hands-on building, deep technical judgment, the joy of writing code |

The last row is the one nobody talks about. The "joy of writing
code" is real, and you give it up. Some managers I know say
they got it back at the Director level (where they're farther
from the code and have accepted that), and some say they miss
it for the rest of their careers.

The "people churn" failure mode is the other thing. A bad
manager doesn't ship a bad system — they ship a bad team. The
system gets rewritten. The team's trust, the reports' careers,
the hiring brand of the org — those take years to repair.

## 4. Compensation (FAANG, 2024-2026, rough)

Numbers change, and public comp data is noisy. These are
**rough ranges** for US-based, FAANG-scale companies, based on
publicly shared levels.fyi data and the offer-letter data I've
seen. Treat them as order-of-magnitude, not exact.

| Level | Title | Base | Stock (annual) | Bonus | Total (year 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L5 (Google) / E5 (Meta) / M5 (most)** | EM | $200-240k | $300-500k | 15-25% | **$550-850k** |
| **L6 (Google) / E6 (Meta) / M6** | Sr. EM / TLM | $240-300k | $500-900k | 20-30% | **$900k-1.4M** |
| **L7 (Google) / E7 (Meta) / M7** | Director | $300-380k | $800k-1.5M | 25-40% | **$1.4-2.4M** |
| **L8 (Google) / E8 (Meta) / M8** | Sr. Director | $380-450k | $1.5-3M | 30-50% | **$2.5-4M+** |

For comparison, the IC equivalent (Staff / Sr. Staff / Principal):

| Level | Title | Total (year 1) |
|---|---|---|
| **L6 (Google) / E6 (Meta) / IC6** | Staff | $700k-1.1M |
| **L7 (Google) / E7 (Meta) / IC7** | Sr. Staff | $1.2-2M |
| **L8 (Google) / E8 (Meta) / IC8** | Principal | $2-4M+ |

Two patterns worth noticing:

1. **At L5/L6, manager comp is competitive with Staff IC.**
   The draw isn't the money — it's the leverage and the path.
2. **At L7/L8, Director comp and Sr. Staff comp are roughly
   equivalent.** The two paths diverge in *kind* of work, not
   in dollars. If you're choosing at this level, choose on the
   work, not the comp.

**The comp ranges for non-FAANG are dramatically lower.** A
"Director of Engineering" at a 200-person startup might be
$220k base + 0.5-1% equity. The title is the same; the comp
is not. Always ask for the band, not the title.

## 5. A worked example: Maya, 6 years DE, Meta E5

**Maya**, 6 years data engineering, E5 at Meta, on the
analytics platform team. Her manager has just told her "we'd
like to consider you for the EM track."

- **Maya's current day:** 70% coding + design, 20% mentoring
  + cross-team, 10% meetings. She writes 200-400 lines of code
  a day. She runs 1 design review a week. She co-leads the
  team's data quality initiative.
- **Maya's day as an EM:** 60% people work (1:1s, packets,
  hiring), 20% cross-team, 20% meetings. Zero code.
- **Maya's day as a TLM:** 40% people work, 40% technical,
  20% meetings. ~100 lines of code a day, mostly in
  design-review mode.
- **Maya's day as a Director:** 80% people and org, 20%
  strategy. Zero code for 6+ months.

Maya is in the position most senior+ ICs are in. The trade
is real, and it's not reversible in a weekend. The next
lesson is the framework for deciding whether to take it.

## Try it

Pick a current manager (yours, or one you respect at a
previous job) and answer these in writing. The point isn't
to evaluate them — it's to evaluate the *role*.

1. **What does their Tuesday look like?** (Be specific. What
   meetings? What writing? What 1:1s?)
2. **What did they ship in the last 6 months that wasn't
   code?** (A hire? A promo? A re-org? A decision?)
3. **What do they most complain about?** (Calendar? Process?
   Hard conversations? Hiring?)
4. **What part of their job would I most enjoy?**
5. **What part of their job would I most hate?**

If you can't answer #4 and #5 with specific answers, you
don't yet have a model of what management looks like — and
you should get one (shadow a manager for a week, read
`Will Larson's An Elegant Puzzle`, or do the test drive in
Lesson 03) *before* taking the offer.
