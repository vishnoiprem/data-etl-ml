# Lesson 4: Influence and Stakeholder Management

> **Week 1 · Day 4 · 90 min**
> **Deliverable:** A 60-second influence ask, recorded.

## 🎯 Architect Level
Staff.

## 🧠 Concept

The CDO role is, by definition, a role of influence without authority. You don't own the business. You don't own the technology. You own the data and the AI portfolio. To move either, you need to influence the people who own the other pieces.

The four moves of executive influence, in order:

1. **Data** — show the stakeholder something they didn't see. *"Here's the number you didn't know you were missing."*
2. **Framing** — reframe their problem in a way that aligns with your ask. *"The way you're measuring X is leaving $Y on the table."*
3. **Trade-off** — name the alternative they're implicitly choosing. *"If we don't do this, the alternative is Z, which costs more."*
4. **Ask** — make a specific, time-boxed, low-regret ask. *"Approve a 30-day pilot. If the numbers don't move, we stop."*

You don't always need all four. Sometimes data alone is enough. Sometimes you go straight to the ask because you have the relationship. But the order matters: data first, ask last. Never ask first.

The most common failure is to **lead with the ask**. *"I need you to fund $5M for a data lakehouse."* The stakeholder's first reaction is "no." You have to first make them feel the problem, then show them the data they didn't have, then frame the trade-off, then ask.

## 🛠️ Build It

**Spec:** Pick a real situation where you influenced a senior stakeholder. Speak a 60-second version of the four moves. Record.

**Acceptance:**
- 50-65 seconds
- The Data move is something the stakeholder didn't see
- The Framing move names the trade-off
- The Ask is specific, time-boxed, and low-regret
- 0 filler words

**Starter:** Use Story 10 (Resolving commercial stakeholder conflict) or a specific instance from Story 1 (KPI standardization).

**Solution (60s, hypothetical Makro CFO):**
> *"Last year our CFO was blocking a $2M investment in semantic-layer tooling. He'd already approved the lakehouse and he didn't see why we needed more.*
>
> *Data: I pulled three months of board-meeting transcripts. In every meeting, two different BUs reported the same metric with different numbers. The CFO had spent at least forty minutes of board time in one quarter alone reconciling which number was right.*
>
> *Framing: I showed him that we were paying for the confusion in two ways — analyst time to reconcile, and slower decisions because nobody trusted the data. The semantic layer would consolidate the definitions once and own them going forward.*
>
> *Trade-off: I named the alternative — keep the existing fragmented metrics and accept that every board meeting will start with a reconciliation argument.*
>
> *Ask: I asked for approval to run a 30-day pilot on the top five metrics. If the pilots didn't reduce reconciliation time, we'd stop.*
>
> *He approved it. The pilots ran, reconciliation time dropped, and the next ask — broader rollout — was an easy yes."*

## 🏛️ Architect Notes

- **Trade-off:** Data first vs. ask first. Data first. Always.
- **Capacity / cost:** A 60-second influence pitch is 130-150 words. The Data move is 30 seconds. The Framing is 15. The Trade-off is 10. The Ask is 5. That's 60 — leaves 0 for filler.
- **When NOT to use:** Don't use this structure for routine operational asks. Save it for asks that meet resistance.
- **Production checklist:**
  - [ ] The Data move is something the stakeholder didn't see
  - [ ] The Framing names the cost of inaction
  - [ ] The Trade-off is the alternative they implicitly chose
  - [ ] The Ask is specific, time-boxed, and low-regret
  - [ ] Total time under 1:05
