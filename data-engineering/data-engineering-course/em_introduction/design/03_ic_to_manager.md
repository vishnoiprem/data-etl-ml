# 03 — Transitioning from IC to Manager

> **Lesson 3 of 6 — EM Introduction** · ~15 min

The 4 signs you're ready to manage, the 3 signs you're not, the
"test drive" approach, and a script for pitching the move to
your current manager. This is the lesson that decides whether
the rest of the track is worth your time.

---

## 1. The 4 signs you're ready to manage

These are not aspirations. They're not "I think I'd be good
at this." They're *evidence from the last 6-12 months* that
you've been doing management work and you've been good at it.

| # | Sign | The evidence (one specific example) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **You mentor naturally, and your mentees grow.** | You ran an informal mentorship with a junior engineer. They've since been promoted, taken on more scope, or moved to a better team — and the credit lands on you. |
| 2 | **You run effective 1:1s.** | You have 2-3 people you meet with weekly or biweekly who *seek you out* for advice, not just status. They leave the 1:1 with more energy, not less. |
| 3 | **You scope team-sized projects, not just your own work.** | You've owned a project that required coordinating 3-5 other people across teams. The project shipped, and the credit was distributed, not hoarded. |
| 4 | **You feel limited by your own time, not your own skill.** | You find yourself saying "I could ship this faster if I had 3 more people" — not "I could ship this faster if I were smarter." The bottleneck is leverage, not depth. |

If 3 of 4 are true, you're likely ready. If all 4 are true,
you're *definitely* ready, and the question is "what took you
so long."

The hardest of the four to fake is #2. 1:1s are the work.
Most engineers are *terrible* at 1:1s — they default to status
updates, they talk too much, they don't listen, they don't
follow up. If your 1:1s are already the part of your week
that reports look forward to, the rest of management will come
easily. If they're a chore, that's a signal worth taking
seriously.

The easiest to fake is #3. Everyone has shipped a project
that touched multiple teams. The signal isn't the project —
it's whether *you* were the bottleneck or the unblocker. If
you were the bottleneck, you may not be ready. If you were
the unblocker (the person who removed 5 different blockers
for 5 different people), you're ready.

---

## 2. The 3 signs you're NOT ready

These are the signs I most often see candidates ignoring.
They're "I would still do this job even if I weren't getting
paid" signals, and they're the ones your manager — who wants
to keep you on the IC ladder, often — will not raise.

| # | Sign | The evidence (one specific example) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **You love coding, specifically the writing-it-yourself part.** | You find your happiest week is the one where you shipped a non-trivial feature alone, end-to-end, in 3 days. The 1:1s and meetings felt like an interruption. |
| 2 | **You want to stay deep technically.** | Your 5-year plan is "Principal Engineer at a top company," and you have a specific technical area you want to be known for. The management path is a side-grade, not a promotion. |
| 3 | **You don't enjoy 1:1s.** | You have them because you have to, not because you look forward to them. The conversation is on your calendar, not in your head. You leave them feeling drained, not energized. |

If 2 of 3 are true, stay on the IC ladder. The honest answer
is that you don't want management — you want *the things
management gets you* (more scope, more comp, more title),
and there's a different way to get each of those on the IC
ladder.

The cruelest sign is #2. "I want to stay deep technically"
is a sentence I hear from senior engineers who are being
pushed to manage, and it's the sentence I take most
seriously. If you have a real, specific technical area you
want to be known for — distributed systems, ML infra, data
quality, query optimization, anything — staying on the IC
ladder and going for Staff/Principal is the better path. The
comp at L7/L8 is roughly the same, and you get to keep the
work you love.

The most overlooked sign is #3. "I don't enjoy 1:1s" is
sometimes "I don't enjoy 1:1s *yet*" (in which case practice
fixes it) and sometimes "I don't enjoy 1:1s *because I find
people work draining*" (in which case nothing fixes it). The
test is whether you've had 1:1s that you *did* enjoy. If
you've had even 3-4, the muscle is there. If you can't
remember a single one, the signal is real.

## 3. The test drive

If you're between the 4 and the 3 — i.e., you have evidence
in both directions — the answer is to **test drive** before
committing. The cleanest version is to manage an intern for
a summer. The messier but more realistic version is to take
on 2-3 management-shaped projects inside your current role.

### The intern test drive

Ask your manager: "I'd like to be the primary mentor for our
next intern." The scope is small (1 person, 12 weeks) and
the risk is low (the intern's work is bounded). The signal
is real:

- **If you find the mentoring energizing**, the rest of
  management will too. 1:1s with an intern are a higher-energy
  subset of management 1:1s.
- **If you find it draining** — the calendar holds, the
  progress-check-ins, the soft-skills conversations — the
  signal is real and worth taking seriously.

The intern test drive is also the most reversible experiment.
You can stop at the end of the summer with no career
consequences, and the intern still got a great summer.

### The project test drive

If no intern is coming, ask your manager for one of these:

1. **Lead an existing engineer's onboarding.** Pick the most
   junior person on the team and be their primary point of
   contact for their first 90 days. Run their 1:1s. Block
   their blockers. Set their OKRs.
2. **Own a hiring loop.** Volunteer to be the hiring manager
   for your team's next req. You'll write the JD, screen
   resumes, run the loop, and write the offer. The
   decision-making and the writing are the management work.
3. **Run a cross-team project.** Pick a project that needs
   3-5 people from different teams. Be the coordinator. The
   work is the cross-team sync, the RFC, the status update,
   the unblocking — not the code.

Each of these is a 1-3 month commitment. After one of them,
you'll have *real* evidence to evaluate. After three, you
won't be test-driving anymore — you'll be doing management
work, and the question will be "why aren't I getting paid for
this."

### The honest signal

The test drive only works if you do the work for real. If
you "test drive" by half-assing the intern mentorship, the
signal you get is "I don't like half-assing an intern
mentorship," which is true but useless. Do the work the way
you'd do it as a manager, and the signal will be honest.

## 4. The pitch to your manager (script)

If after the test drive you decide you want to make the move,
the next conversation is with your current manager. The
pitch has 4 parts, in order:

1. **The evidence.** "Over the last 12 months, here's the
   management work I've been doing: [list 3-4 specific
   things — the intern, the onboarding, the cross-team
   project, the hiring loop]. I want to do more of it."
2. **The "why now."** "I'd like to make the move in the
   next 6-9 months, not because I'm bored on the IC side but
   because the management work is what I'm best at and where
   my leverage is highest."
3. **The ask.** "I'd like your help in two ways: (1) your
   nomination when an EM req opens, and (2) a 6-month
   development plan to get me ready — specifically, more
   1:1 practice, a perf-cycle observation, and a hiring
   loop."
4. **The fallback.** "If the timing doesn't work, I want to
   stay on the IC ladder and aim for Staff. I'd rather get
   this right than rush it."

A few things to avoid:

- **Don't pitch it as a "promotion."** It's not. The title
  change is downstream. The pitch is "I want to do
  different work."
- **Don't pitch it as a "step back from coding."** It's
  partly that, but framing it as a step *back* signals
  you're not sure. The framing is "step *toward* the work
  I'm best at."
- **Don't pitch it without evidence.** "I want to be a
  manager" with no list of management work is a request
  your manager will struggle to act on. The 3-4 specific
  things in part 1 are the whole pitch.

A worked example of the full script, for Maya from
Lesson 02:

> *"I've been thinking about the next step, and I'd like to
> talk to you about the EM track. The reason is the work,
> not the title. Over the last 12 months, I've been doing
> more management-shaped work — I mentored Priya through
> her promotion to E5, I owned the data-quality initiative
> that touched 4 teams, and I ran last quarter's hiring
> loop for the senior DE req. That work is what I find
> most energizing, and the parts of my week I most want
> to keep are the 1:1s and the cross-team coordination,
> not the code.*
>
> *I'd like to make the move in the next 6-9 months, not
> because I'm bored on the IC side — I still love the
> design work — but because my leverage is higher as a
> manager than as an IC. Specifically, I can multiply
> Priya's output, Anish's output, and the new hire's
> output in a way I can't multiply my own.*
>
> *I'd like your help in two ways: first, your nomination
> when an EM req opens on the team. Second, a 6-month
> development plan to get me ready — more 1:1 practice,
> a perf-cycle observation, and ideally a hiring loop
> this quarter.*
>
> *If the timing doesn't work, I want to stay on the IC
> ladder and aim for Staff. I'd rather get this right than
> rush it. But I wanted to put it on your radar now so we
> can plan for it."*

This is a 90-second script. The words will change. The
4-part structure shouldn't.

---

## 5. When to *not* pitch

There are two situations where the pitch should wait.

1. **You've been in your current role for less than 18
   months.** Pitching the move before you've been a senior
   IC for a year and a half is a signal that you haven't
   earned the senior IC bar yet. Spend 6 more months
   shipping as an IC. The EM pitch will be stronger for it.
2. **Your current manager is the same person who'd
   *block* the move.** Some managers (especially high-IQ
   ICs who never wanted to manage) actively don't want
   their reports to manage. If your manager has talked
   down 2-3 other reports' EM pitches in the last year,
   your pitch will get the same treatment. The workaround
   is to talk to your skip-level first.

In both cases, the action is the same: gather more evidence
and have a different conversation.

---

## Try it

Take the 4 ready signs and the 3 not-ready signs. For each
one, write down one specific example from the last 6-12
months. Be honest. If you can't fill in the example, the
sign doesn't apply to you.

Then count:

- **3+ ready signs, 0-1 not-ready signs** → you're ready.
  Read Lesson 04 next.
- **2 ready, 2 not-ready** → test drive. Pick one of the
  three test drives in Section 3, ask your manager, and
  revisit in 90 days.
- **0-1 ready, 2+ not-ready** → stay IC. Skip to Lesson 06
  and read the career-archetype section.

There's no wrong answer. The wrong answer is making the move
*without* this assessment.
