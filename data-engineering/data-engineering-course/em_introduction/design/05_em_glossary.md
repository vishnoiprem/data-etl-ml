# 05 — Engineering Management Glossary

> **Lesson 5 of 6 — EM Introduction** · ~20 min

The vocabulary you need to read JDs, perf docs, skip-level
emails, and PIP packets without Googling every third word. 60+
terms, alphabetized, 1-3 sentence definitions, with see-also
links.

Bookmark this lesson. You won't memorize it in one read —
you'll come back to it whenever a term in a perf review or a
skip-level doesn't ring a bell.

---

## A

**1:1 (one-on-one)**
A recurring 30-60 minute meeting between a manager and a
direct report, usually weekly or biweekly. The 1:1 is the
*manager's* meeting — the report drives the agenda, the manager
listens. See also: skip-level.

**Attrition**
The rate at which people leave an org or team, usually
expressed as a percentage per quarter or per year. Voluntary
attrition (resignations) and involuntary attrition (PIPs,
terminations) are tracked separately. See also: regrettable
attrition, PIR.

**Autonomy (in the rubric)**
A promotion-rubric dimension: the ability to operate without
direction, set your own goals, and decide *how* to achieve
them. Senior EMs are expected to operate with full autonomy;
mid-level EMs operate with direction. See also: scope, bar,
rubric.

---

## B

**Bar (the bar)**
The hiring bar — the quality threshold above which a candidate
is hired and below which they're not. "Above the bar" / "below
the bar" is a binary at most companies. "The bar has gone up"
means the company's quality threshold has risen. See also:
bar raiser, rubric, leveling.

**Bar raiser**
A trained interviewer (Amazon, Meta) with independent veto
power on a hiring loop. The bar-raiser is *not* on the
hiring manager's side and is responsible for keeping the
hiring bar consistent across the company. See also: bar.

**Bonus**
The cash component of compensation that's variable based on
company + individual performance. Usually 15-30% of base for
EMs, paid annually. See also: comp band, RSU.

**Burnout**
A state of physical, mental, and emotional exhaustion caused
by sustained work stress. An EM's job includes recognizing
burnout in reports and intervening before it becomes a
performance issue. See also: 1:1, OOO.

---

## C

**Calibration**
The cross-team meeting where managers align on performance
ratings to ensure consistency. In a calibration meeting, 5-10
managers discuss each of their reports and assign ratings
(top-box, middle, low). The goal is to remove manager bias.
See also: performance review, leveling.

**Career ladder / eng ladder**
The defined levels an engineer (or EM) can be promoted
through, with the expectations at each level. At Google:
L3-L8. At Meta: E3-E8. At most US tech: IC1-IC6 or
M1-M7. See also: leveling, scope.

**Coaching (vs. mentoring)**
Coaching is asking questions to help the report find their
own answer. Mentoring is sharing your own experience to
guide theirs. Good EMs do both; the balance shifts toward
coaching as the report gets more senior. See also: 1:1,
mentorship.

**Comp band**
The salary range for a given level and discipline, usually
with a min, mid, and max. Companies publish these internally
to ensure pay equity. "You're at the top of the band"
means there's limited upside without a promotion. See also:
RSU, leveling.

**Compensation philosophy**
A company's overall approach to comp: market-leading (Stripe,
Netflix), market-matching (most), or below-market + equity-
heavy (early-stage startups). The philosophy determines
whether your offer is "high base, low equity" or the reverse.
See also: RSU, comp band.

**Cross-functional partnership**
Working with people outside your team (PM, Design, Data
Science, Sales) to ship outcomes. A core EM skill — the
ability to drive outcomes through influence, not org chart.
See also: TPM, SDM.

---

## D

**Director (eng director)**
A senior leader (L7/E6 at FAANG) who manages 3-5 EMs. The
Director's job is org-level: setting direction, allocating
resources, hiring EMs, handling escalations. See also:
skip-level, VP.

**Dotted-line report**
A person who works for you on a project but reports to
someone else in the org chart. Common for cross-team
projects and rotational programs. See also: matrixed org.

---

## E

**EM (engineering manager)**
A people manager whose reports are engineers. The "manager"
in "EM" is a manager of *people*, not a manager of
*projects*. See also: TLM, people manager, tech lead.

**EM loop**
The interview loop for an EM role — see Lesson 04 for the
6-round structure.

**Eng ladder (engineering ladder)**
See "career ladder."

**Exec round**
The final round in an EM loop, usually with a VP, Sr.
Director, or trained bar-raiser. Tests org judgment and
values alignment. See also: bar raiser.

---

## F

**Firing**
The involuntary termination of employment. The manager's
hardest job. Should follow a documented PIP and a clear
trajectory; never a surprise. See also: PIP, performance
review.

**Full-stack EM**
Informal term for an EM who codes, designs, and manages
roughly equally. The "TLM" role in Lesson 02. See also:
TLM.

---

## G

**Goals (OKRs, KPIs)**
Objectives and Key Results (Google) or similar goal-setting
frameworks. Set quarterly or half-yearly; the EM's job is to
align team goals to org goals. See also: scope, autonomy.

**Going forward (term)**
A phrase in performance reviews meaning "in the future, you
need to do X." Often a soft warning. If you hear it 2 cycles
in a row, you're on a PIP trajectory. See also: PIP, performance
review.

**Group / org / org chart**
The hierarchy: IC → EM → Director → VP → CTO. "Org" is the
collection of teams under a VP. "Group" is a sub-collection
under a Director. "Org chart" is the visual representation.
See also: skip-level, dotted-line.

---

## H

**Halo / horns effect**
The cognitive bias where one strong (halo) or weak (horns)
trait colors the perception of all other traits. The #1
calibration-meeting problem. See also: calibration, performance
review.

**Headcount**
The number of people allocated to a team. "I have 2
headcount" means I can hire 2 people. "I lost a headcount"
means someone left. See also: attrition, requisition.

**Hiring bar**
See "bar."

**Hiring loop**
The full interview cycle for a candidate: recruiter screen,
technical round(s), behavioral, debrief, decision. Usually
4-6 rounds. See also: EM loop, bar raiser.

---

## I

**IC (individual contributor)**
A person whose primary output is their own work, not the
work of others. The opposite of "manager." Senior ICs (Staff,
Sr. Staff, Principal) are the parallel track to the EM
ladder. See also: IC ladder, scope.

**IC ladder**
The career path for individual contributors who don't manage.
Same leveling as the manager ladder, with a different
rubric. The IC ladder at L7/L8 is "Principal Engineer" or
"Senior Staff." See also: career ladder, leveling.

**Involuntary attrition**
Attrition caused by the company (PIP, termination, RIF),
not the employee. Tracked separately from voluntary
attrition. See also: attrition, PIR, RIF.

---

## J

**Job description (JD)**
The public posting for a role. Read it carefully — the
"responsibilities" section is the real JD; the "requirements"
section is a wish list. See also: leveling, comp band.

---

## K

**Keep (promotion decision)**
The promotion decision when a person is not yet ready. "We
decided to keep them at L5 for another cycle" means they
didn't get promoted. See also: promotion packet, leveling.

---

## L

**L (level)**
A shorthand for a level on the eng ladder. L4, L5, L6, L7,
L8 at Google. See also: leveling, eng ladder.

**Layoff**
A reduction in force (RIF) — a company-initiated mass
termination, usually for financial reasons. Different from
a PIP. See also: RIF, PIP.

**Leveling**
The process of assigning a level to a person. Done at hire
and at promotion. "What level are we hiring for?" is the
question that determines the comp band and the rubric. See
also: career ladder, comp band, rubric.

**L10 / quarterly business review (QBR)**
A weekly or quarterly all-hands where the org reviews the
top 10 priorities, the metrics, and the issues. Invented at
EOS (Entrepreneurial Operating System); now standard at
most US tech. See also: skip-level, all-hands.

---

## M

**M (manager)**
The manager ladder shorthand. M3, M4, M5, M6, M7. At Meta,
the manager ladder is M1-M7; M5 is roughly equivalent to
E5/L6 at other companies. See also: leveling, eng ladder.

**Manager (vs. lead)**
A *manager* has people-management responsibility (hiring,
firing, reviews). A *lead* has technical or project
leadership without people-management responsibility. Many
"tech lead" roles are lead-but-not-manager. See also: TLM,
tech lead.

**Matrixed org**
An org where people have multiple reporting lines (e.g., a
solid-line manager and a dotted-line project lead). Common
in larger companies and during cross-team projects. See
also: dotted-line.

**Mentorship**
A longer-term relationship where a more senior person helps
a more junior person grow. Different from coaching: mentorship
is sharing your own experience, not asking questions. See
also: coaching, 1:1.

**Micromanagement**
Managing too closely — checking in too often, overriding
decisions, not delegating. The #1 complaint junior EMs get
in their first year. See also: autonomy, scope.

---

## N

**New-hire packet**
The written summary a manager prepares for a new hire's
first 90 days, including goals, onboarding plan, and
check-in cadence. The packet is the manager's first
"hiring" decision. See also: onboarding.

---

## O

**Onboarding**
The 30-60-90 day plan for a new hire. The EM's job is to
own the onboarding plan, the first-week check-ins, and the
30-day "is this working" conversation. See also: new-hire
packet.

**OOO (out of office)**
The status (calendar + Slack) when someone is on vacation
or otherwise unavailable. A good manager models taking
real OOO time and respects reports' OOO time. See also:
burnout.

**Org (organization)**
A collection of teams under a VP. "I run the data org" means
the VP of Data runs the org, and the directors and EMs under
them are in the org. See also: group, org chart.

---

## P

**People manager**
The flavor of EM whose primary job is managing people —
hiring, 1:1s, reviews, growth, attrition. The "PM" in
"People Manager" is not the same as a "Product Manager."
See also: EM, TLM.

**Performance improvement plan (PIP)**
A documented plan to help an underperforming employee improve
over 30-60-90 days. Most PIPs don't end in improvement; the
purpose is often to create a paper trail before termination.
See also: going forward, firing, PIR.

**Performance review (perf)**
The twice-yearly (or annual) review cycle where every
employee is rated, written up, and calibrated. The manager's
deliverable is a "perf packet" for each report. See also:
calibration, promotion packet.

**Perf packet**
The written document a manager produces for each report
during the perf cycle: 1-2 pages summarizing impact,
strengths, growth areas, and a rating. The packet is the
primary input to calibration. See also: promotion packet,
performance review.

**PIR (post-incident review / post-implementation review)**
Two meanings. (1) *Post-incident review* — the blameless
writeup after an outage, what went wrong, what to fix. (2)
*Post-implementation review* — the 30-90 day review after
someone leaves (voluntary or involuntary) to understand
why. See also: postmortem, attrition.

**Postmortem**
A blameless writeup after an incident (outage, bug, near-
miss). The format is: timeline, contributing factors, what
went well, what to fix. The EM's job is to make postmortems
blameless in practice, not just in name. See also: PIR.

**Promotion packet**
The written document that proposes a person for promotion
to the next level. The packet includes: the person's
impact, scope, rubric evidence, and 3-5 peer / stakeholder
endorsements. Reviewed in a promotion committee. See also:
leveling, perf packet, calibration.

---

## R

**Regrettable attrition**
Attrition where the company *wants* to keep the person who
left. Tracked separately from non-regrettable attrition
(people the company was already planning to let go). See
also: attrition.

**Requisition (req)**
An approved headcount slot for a hire. "I have an open req"
means you can hire. "My req was pulled" means the headcount
was removed. See also: headcount.

**RIF (reduction in force)**
A company-initiated mass layoff. "We did a 10% RIF" means
10% of the company was let go, usually for financial
reasons. See also: layoff, PIP.

**RSG (refresh grant)**
A periodic grant of additional RSUs to existing employees,
usually annual or biannual, to keep total comp competitive
as the stock price moves. See also: RSU, comp band.

**RSU (restricted stock unit)**
A grant of company stock that vests over time (typically
4 years with a 1-year cliff). The largest component of EM
comp at most US tech companies. See also: bonus, RSG.

**Rubric**
The scoring framework for a level. "The L5 rubric" lists
the 5-8 dimensions (scope, impact, judgment, collaboration,
communication) and what each looks like at L5. The rubric
is what every interview and promotion decision is measured
against. See also: leveling, bar.

---

## S

**Scope**
A promotion-rubric dimension: the size and complexity of
the work. "She has L6 scope" means her work is at L6 size.
"Scope creep" is when a project's scope grows beyond what
was originally scoped. See also: autonomy, rubric.

**Skip-level**
The meeting between an IC and their manager's manager.
"Skip-level" is also the relationship itself. A good
manager doesn't try to control what happens in skip-levels
— the relationship is between the IC and the skip-level
directly. See also: 1:1, org chart.

**SDM (senior director of engineering)**
A senior leader (L7/E7 at FAANG) who manages multiple
directors. Equivalent to a "Sr. Director" or "VP" at
smaller companies. See also: Director, VP.

**Senior EM (Sr. EM)**
An EM at the L6/E6 level, usually managing 6-10 ICs and
operating with full autonomy. The next step up the EM
ladder from EM. See also: leveling, EM.

**STAR (situation, task, action, result)**
The format for behavioral interview answers. Situation
(set the scene), Task (what was your job), Action (what
did you do), Result (what was the outcome). The most
common behavioral framework, especially at FAANG. See
also: behavioral round, EM loop.

---

## T

**TLM (tech-lead manager)**
A flavor of EM who manages 4-8 ICs *and* still writes code
and reviews designs. The "hybrid" role. Common at smaller
companies and at FAANG teams that need senior technical
leadership. See also: EM, tech lead, full-stack EM.

**Tech lead**
A technical leadership role (not a management role) where
the person sets the technical direction for a team without
people-management responsibility. Often the precursor to a
TLM role. See also: TLM, manager vs. lead.

**TPM (technical program manager)**
A role that owns cross-team program execution. Not an EM,
not a PM — closer to a "program manager" with technical
depth. The TPM's job is to unblock, coordinate, and track.
See also: PM, cross-functional partnership.

**Turnover**
Another word for attrition. Often used interchangeably.
"Annual turnover" = annual attrition rate. See also:
attrition.

---

## U

**Underperformer**
An employee who is consistently missing expectations. The
EM's job is to identify underperformance early, give
honest feedback, and either coach to improvement or move
to PIP. See also: PIP, going forward.

**Up-leveling**
The act of raising the quality bar for a team's output.
"Up-leveling the team" means raising the average level of
the people on it. The cleanest way is to hire above the
current median; the messiest is to manage out the bottom.
See also: bar, calibration.

---

## V

**VP (vice president of engineering)**
A senior leader (L8/E8 at FAANG) who manages multiple
directors or SDMs. The VP's job is org strategy, exec
alignment, and people decisions at the top. See also:
Director, SDM.

**Voluntary attrition**
Attrition caused by the employee (resignation), not the
company. Tracked separately from involuntary attrition.
See also: attrition, regrettable attrition.

---

## W

**Working agreement**
A document the team writes together (often in an offsite)
that defines how they'll work: meeting norms, code review
expectations, on-call expectations, communication norms.
A good EM revisits the working agreement every 6-12 months.
See also: 1:1, team health.

---

## Z

**Zero-interest promotion**
A promotion that's not blocked by any specific concern but
also not actively championed. "We gave them a zero-interest
promotion" means nobody opposed it but nobody fought for
it either. The opposite of a "strong yes" promotion. See
also: promotion packet, calibration.

---

## Cross-reference index (terms by theme)

If you're reading a perf doc, here's where to look first:

- **People:** 1:1, mentorship, coaching, skip-level, working
  agreement.
- **Hiring:** headcount, requisition, hiring loop, bar, bar
  raiser, leveling.
- **Comp:** comp band, RSU, RSG, bonus.
- **Perf:** performance review, perf packet, calibration,
  promotion packet, PIP, going forward.
- **Attrition:** attrition, regrettable attrition, voluntary
  vs. involuntary, PIR, RIF.
- **Levels:** eng ladder, IC ladder, manager ladder, scope,
  autonomy, rubric, leveling.
- **Org design:** skip-level, dotted-line, matrixed org, group,
  org, VP, Director, SDM.
- **Cross-functional:** TPM, PM, cross-functional partnership.

## Try it

Pick a real JD for an EM role (from levels.fyi, your
network, or a job board). For each of the 5 terms below,
find the JD's equivalent phrasing:

1. The JD says "you'll partner with PM and Design." → Which
   glossary term captures this? (Cross-functional partnership.)
2. The JD says "you'll manage a team of 5-8 engineers."
   → Which flavor of EM is this? (People manager, not TLM.)
3. The JD says "L6 / E6 / M6." → What's the compensation
   implication? (See the comp table in Lesson 02.)
4. The JD says "you'll run performance reviews and
   calibration." → Which two glossary terms apply? (Performance
   review + calibration.)
5. The JD says "you'll partner with TPM on cross-team
   programs." → What's a TPM? (See "TPM" above.)

If you got all 5, the glossary is doing its job. If you
missed 2+, read those entries once more and bookmark the
cross-reference index for the next time you read a JD.
