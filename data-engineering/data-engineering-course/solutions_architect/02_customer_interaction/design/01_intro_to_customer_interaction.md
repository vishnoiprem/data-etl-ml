# 01 — Intro to Customer Interaction Interviews

> **Lesson 1 of 12 — Customer Interaction** · ~10 min

The 4 customer-interaction round types, the rubric for each,
and what "passing" actually looks like. The 30,000-foot view
of the round most engineering candidates under-prepare for.

---

## 1. The 4 round types

Customer-interaction interviews at the SA level come in 4
shapes. The names vary by company; the structure is the
same.

| Round | Format | Duration | What it tests |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | 1:1 roleplay, you run a discovery call | 30-45 min | Can you listen, qualify the deal, surface the customer's real pain? |
| **Demo** | 1:1 roleplay, you demo a product or solution | 30-45 min | Can you structure a demo, handle breakage, drive the customer to a next step? |
| **Objection handling** | 1:1 roleplay, the customer pushes back | 20-30 min | Can you handle common objections with grace, specificity, and without becoming defensive? |
| **Whiteboard / Architecture** | 1:1 roleplay, you draw a solution and defend it | 45-60 min | Can you design under pressure, defend your choices, and adapt to new constraints? |

At AWS, all 4 are tested separately. At Snowflake and
Databricks, the discovery and demo are often combined into a
single "customer scenario" round. At Salesforce, the
discovery and objection are often combined.

The 4 are tested in 1-3 actual interview rounds, depending
on the company.

---

## 2. The rubric (per round)

Each round has a different rubric, but the *signals* are
similar:

### Discovery rubric

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Active listening** | Did the candidate ask clarifying questions, or did they jump to a solution? |
| **Pain-point discovery** | Did the candidate identify the *underlying* pain, or just the surface ask? |
| **Qualification** | Did the candidate identify the decision-maker, decision-process, timeline, and budget? |
| **Customer's customer** | Did the candidate center the *end-user* of the customer's product? |
| **Closing the call** | Did the candidate summarize, set expectations, and propose a clear next step? |

The 5 signals map to the 5 phases of a discovery call:
listen, dig, qualify, reframe, close.

### Demo rubric

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Customer-tailored narrative** | Did the candidate tailor the demo to the customer's pain, or did they run a generic demo? |
| **Pacing** | Did the candidate pause for questions, or did they race through? |
| **Breakage handling** | When the demo broke (and it will), did the candidate recover gracefully? |
| **Driving to a next step** | Did the demo end with a clear "here's what we should do next"? |
| **Honesty about limits** | Did the candidate say "this doesn't do X" when it doesn't, or did they over-claim? |

The 5 signals map to the 5 phases of a demo: frame,
narrate, show, break, close.

### Objection-handling rubric

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Acknowledge first** | Did the candidate acknowledge the objection before responding? |
| **Specificity** | Was the response specific, or was it "we're better than the alternative"? |
| **Customer framing** | Did the response use the customer's vocabulary, or the SA's jargon? |
| **No defensiveness** | Did the candidate stay calm, or did they get defensive? |
| **Re-engagement** | Did the candidate pull the customer back into the conversation, or did they end the call? |

The 5 signals are tested in 3-5 objections per round.

### Whiteboard / Architecture rubric

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Defensible design** | Is the architecture actually defensible, or is it handwavy? |
| **Tradeoff articulation** | Did the candidate name the tradeoffs (cost, latency, ops, scalability)? |
| **Listening under pressure** | Did the candidate absorb new constraints from the "customer" without losing the thread? |
| **Quantification** | Did the candidate use numbers (e.g., "this will give us 50ms p99 latency at $X/month") or adjectives? |
| **Customer-tailored** | Did the candidate center the customer's constraints, or did they reach for a generic best-practice architecture? |

The 5 signals are tested across the 60-minute design
exercise.

## 3. The common failure modes

Each round has its own common failure mode. If you recognize
any of these in your own behavior, that's the gap to close
first.

### Discovery failure modes

- **"Solutioneering"** — pitching a solution before listening
  to the problem. Most common failure mode by far.
- **"Checklist discovery"** — running through 15 questions
  without adapting to what the customer says. Feels robotic.
- **"False listening"** — saying "I understand" 3 times in
  a row without any specifics. Customer can tell.
- **"No next step"** — ending the call with "let me know if
  you have questions" instead of a specific follow-up.
- **"Selling to the wrong person"** — pitching a $5M
  enterprise solution to a mid-market customer, or vice
  versa.

### Demo failure modes

- **"Feature tour"** — running through every feature in
  order instead of following the customer's narrative.
- **"No breaks for questions"** — racing through 15 minutes
  of demo without pausing.
- **"Freezing on breakage"** — the demo breaks, the
  candidate stops, the customer disengages.
- **"Over-claiming"** — promising the demo does something
  it doesn't.
- **"Generic close"** — ending with "any questions?" instead
  of a specific next step.

### Objection failure modes

- **"Defensiveness"** — getting visibly annoyed by the
  objection, raising voice, interrupting.
- **"Generic rebuttal"** — saying "we're better than the
  competition" without specifics.
- **"Arguing the objection"** — treating the objection as
  a debate to win instead of a concern to address.
- **"Caving"** — giving in immediately, dropping the
  price or conceding the point without exploring the
  underlying concern.
- **"Stalling"** — "let me check with my team" as a
  defensive delay, without a real follow-up.

### Whiteboard / Architecture failure modes

- **"Generic architecture"** — drawing the standard
  reference architecture, not tailored to the customer.
- **"No tradeoffs"** — designing without naming what
  you're giving up (cost, latency, complexity, ops burden).
- **"No listening"** — continuing the design without
  absorbing the customer's mid-design constraints.
- **"Adjective-laden"** — "this is scalable and resilient"
  without specific numbers.
- **"No defense"** — folding on the first challenge,
  redesigning from scratch instead of defending the choice.

If you recognize yourself in 1-2 of these, the relevant
lessons in this module are your prep. If you recognize
yourself in 3+, your prep is broader; do the full module
plus a few mocks with a friend.

## 4. The 4 prep modes

Customer-interaction rounds require 4 different kinds of
prep, and most candidates do only the first.

| Prep mode | What it is | Time investment |
|---|---|---|
| **Reading the framework** | Reading lessons like this one | 4-6 hours |
| **Studying mocks** | Watching or reading transcript mocks | 2-3 hours |
| **Out-loud practice** | Running the round yourself, recorded, alone | 4-8 hours |
| **With-a-friend practice** | Running the round with a friend playing the customer | 4-6 hours |

The first two are necessary but not sufficient. The
candidate who only reads and studies fails the round because
the round tests *behavior*, not *knowledge*. The behavior
is built in prep modes 3 and 4.

For each lesson that includes a mock (06, 07, 08, 09), do
the mock *out loud* with a friend. If you don't have a
friend available, do it alone with a recording. Listening
back to yourself is the most uncomfortable and most
effective prep you'll do.

---

## 5. What "passing" actually looks like

A "passing" customer-interaction round has 4 characteristics:

1. **The customer feels heard.** At the end of the call,
   the customer (or the interviewer playing the customer)
   says something like "you really understood our problem"
   or "I feel like you get what we're trying to do."
2. **The candidate qualifies the deal.** The candidate
   identified the decision-maker, the decision-process, the
   timeline, and (often) the budget — or knew to ask about
   each.
3. **The next step is specific.** Not "let me know if you
   want to follow up." But "I'll send a 1-page technical
   brief by Thursday, and we'll set up a 60-minute
   whiteboarding session for next Tuesday."
4. **The candidate's product knowledge is invisible.** The
   best customer-interaction rounds don't *show* product
   knowledge — they *use* product knowledge to serve the
   customer. If the candidate is visibly showcasing their
   knowledge, the round failed.

If you hit all 4, the round is in the bag. Move on.

## Try it

For each of the 4 rounds (discovery, demo, objection,
whiteboard), answer in writing:

1. **What is the round testing?**
2. **What is my biggest failure mode for this round?**
3. **What is my prep plan for this round?**

If the answers to #2 are vague, you don't yet have a
model of the round. Do the relevant lessons (02-04) before
the mocks (06-09). If the answers to #3 don't include
"practice out loud with a friend," your prep is incomplete;
rebuild it.

The most common prep mistake is reading all 12 lessons
and not doing any mocks. Reading is *necessary*; it's not
*sufficient*. The behavior is built in the mocks.
