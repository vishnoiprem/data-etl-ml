# 09 — Mock Objection Handling: "Your Pricing is Too High"

> **Lesson 9 of 12 — Customer Interaction** · ~10 min

A 3-minute mock objection-handling transcript with
analysis. The objection is the most common one in SA
interviews: "Your pricing is too high."

This is the most-practiced lesson in the module. Read it
twice, then run the 30-second and 2-minute responses out
loud with a friend.

---

## 1. The setup

- **Candidate:** Sam, 8 years data engineering. Interviewing
  for Senior SA.
- **Interviewer:** Jordan. Plays the role of "CFO" at a
  mid-market customer who's evaluating the vendor's data
  pipeline product.
- **Round:** 15 minutes total — 5 minutes of setup, 10
  minutes of objection-handling.
- **Objection:** "Your pricing is 2x what we expected. We
  can't justify this to the board."

The CFO has a real concern. Sam has 10 minutes to address
it.

---

## 2. The transcript

> **Sam:** "I appreciate you sharing that concern directly.
> Pricing is exactly the kind of thing that should be on
> the table. Let me ask a clarifying question first —
> when you say 2x what you expected, are you comparing us
> to a specific competitor's pricing, or is this a
> question about whether the value is worth the spend at
> all?"
>
> **Jordan:** "It's both, honestly. We were budgeting
> around $200k, and your pricing came in at $400k. And
> we've talked to [competitor], and they're $150k."
>
> **Sam:** "Got it. Two specific things I want to address.
> First, on the absolute price — our pricing is per-
> workload, not per-seat. So as your usage grows, the
> per-workload cost goes down. If you're at 50 workloads
> today and grow to 200 over 18 months, our effective
> per-workload cost drops by 60%. That's a different
> shape than [competitor]'s per-user pricing, which
> scales linearly with your team size.
>
> Second, on the comparison — the $150k figure from
> [competitor] is their list price. We've done side-by-
> side comparisons with 3 customers in your industry, and
> our TCO over 3 years was 30% lower, primarily because
> [specific reason — e.g., we don't charge for data
> egress, our managed services include 24/7 support
> whereas [competitor] charges extra for it, etc.].
>
> Third, on value — and this is the part that matters most.
> Our customers typically see a 3-5x ROI in the first year
> because [specific outcome — e.g., $X savings, Y%
> faster time-to-market]. If the value is 3-5x the cost,
> then the question isn't whether $400k is too much —
> it's whether you're capturing the full value.
>
> Two things I'd suggest. First, let me put together a
> side-by-side comparison with [competitor] for your
> specific workload pattern, so the $150k vs. $400k
> comparison is apples-to-apples. Second, let me put
> together a 12-month ROI model for your specific use
> case, so the value question is concrete rather than
> hand-wavy.
>
> Could we spend 60 minutes next week going through both?
> I'd suggest we do this with your finance lead and your
> technical lead, so we can answer questions on both
> sides."
>
> **Jordan:** "That's helpful. Let me think about it and
> get back to you."
>
> **Sam:** "Of course. And if it's helpful, I can share
> the ROI model template in advance, so your finance
> lead has time to review before the meeting. That way,
> the 60 minutes is a working session, not a discovery."

---

## 3. The analysis

### What Sam did well

- **Acknowledged the objection first** ("I appreciate you
  sharing that concern directly. Pricing is exactly the
  kind of thing that should be on the table."). This is
  the move that keeps the conversation alive.
- **Asked a clarifying question** before responding. The
  question "is this a comparison or a value question?"
  reframes the objection from "your price is too high" to
  "I need a clearer picture of why this is worth $400k."
- **Distinguished the 3 sub-concerns** — absolute price,
  comparison to competitor, and value. Most SAs answer
  the surface "your price is too high" without
  distinguishing. Sam distinguished and addressed each.
- **Used specific numbers** — 60% drop in effective per-
  workload cost, 30% lower TCO over 3 years, 3-5x ROI in
  year 1. The numbers are credible (specific, plausible,
  not generic "we're cost-effective").
- **Proposed a concrete next step** — 60-minute working
  session with two specific deliverables (TCO comparison
  and ROI model). The CFO knows exactly what happens
  next.
- **Offered to share the ROI template in advance.** This
  signals respect for the CFO's time and positions the
  next session as a working session, not a pitch.

### What Sam could have done better

- **Could have asked about the board context.** "When
  you say 'we can't justify this to the board,' what's
  the board's specific concern? Is it the absolute
  number, the lack of differentiation from cheaper
  options, or the missing ROI case?" Anchoring on the
  board's *specific* concern would deepen the response.
- **Could have anchored on the customer's customer.**
  The CFO isn't the end-user; the data engineers and the
  product managers are. A senior SA move would be to
  remind the CFO: "the question for the board isn't
  just the $200k delta — it's the cost of the
  alternative, which is your team spending 30% of their
  time on pipeline plumbing instead of product work."

### The rubric score

| Signal | Score |
|---|---|
| **Acknowledge first** | 4/4 — explicit acknowledgment before responding |
| **Specificity** | 4/4 — concrete numbers, specific actions |
| **Customer framing** | 3.5/4 — used customer vocabulary, but could have used more |
| **No defensiveness** | 4/4 — calm, no arguing |
| **Re-engagement** | 4/4 — specific next step with two deliverables |

**Overall: 3.9/4.** A high-score objection-handling round.

## 4. The 5-step objection pattern

The same 5-step pattern from Lesson 04, applied here:

1. **Acknowledge.** "I appreciate you sharing that
   directly."
2. **Validate.** "Pricing is exactly the kind of thing
   that should be on the table."
3. **Reframe.** "When you say 2x, are you comparing us
   to [competitor] or evaluating value?"
4. **Respond.** Three specific sub-responses (absolute
   price, comparison, value).
5. **Check.** "Could we spend 60 minutes next week going
   through both?"

The 5-step pattern is the spine of every objection
response. Practice it 10 times with different objections,
and you'll have it in muscle memory.

## 5. The 4 most common follow-up objections

After the initial "your pricing is too high," the customer
often pushes back 2-3 more times. Sam handled the first
pushback well; here are 3 follow-up objections and how to
handle them:

### Follow-up 1: "Can you give us a discount?"

> "We do have some flexibility on multi-year commitments.
> A 3-year commitment typically unlocks [specific
> percentage] off list. What I'd suggest is we get the
> pricing comparison and ROI model in place first, so
> the discount conversation has the right context. Does
> that work?"

### Follow-up 2: "Our budget is firm at $200k."

> "Understood. The question is whether the value
> justifies re-opening the budget conversation. Our
> customers typically see [specific ROI] in year 1.
> If we can demonstrate that the [specific outcome]
> is worth $400k, then the budget conversation can be
> reframed. Could we spend 30 minutes on the ROI model
> before the budget conversation?"

### Follow-up 3: "Competitor X said they'd match your
features for $150k."

> "It's worth pressure-testing that claim. The 3 areas
> where our customers find the difference is real are
> [specific areas]. I can pull together a 1-page
> comparison on those 3 areas. The question is whether
> those differences are material to your use case. If
> they are, the $250k delta is justified. If they're
> not, then [competitor] is the right answer."

Each follow-up follows the same 5-step pattern:
acknowledge, validate, reframe, respond, check. The
content differs; the pattern doesn't.

## Try it

Pick the 3 objections from Lesson 04 that you'd most
likely face in your specialty. For each:

1. **Write a 30-second response using the 5-step pattern.**
2. **Write a 2-minute response using the 5-step pattern.**
3. **Write a follow-up response (in case the customer
   pushes back again).**
4. **Run all 3 out loud with a friend.**

Time each response. A 30-second response should be 25-35
seconds. A 2-minute response should be 1:45-2:15.

The investment is 60-90 minutes for 3 objections. The
return is the entire objection-handling round of the
interview. Done well, the objection-handling round is
a free win — most candidates under-prepare for it.
