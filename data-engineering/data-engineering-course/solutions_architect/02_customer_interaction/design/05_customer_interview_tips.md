# 05 — Customer Interaction Interview Tips

> **Lesson 5 of 12 — Customer Interaction** · ~12 min

10 practical tips for staying calm, listening, and
structuring the customer-interaction rounds. The meta-
patterns that apply across discovery, demo, objection
handling, and whiteboarding.

---

## 1. The 10 tips

### Tip 1: The first 30 seconds set the tone

The first 30 seconds of the round are the most important.
If you land them well, the rest of the round goes
smoothly. If you don't, the rest of the round is
recovery.

What to do in the first 30 seconds:

- Acknowledge the customer (or the interviewer playing the
  customer) warmly.
- State the agenda clearly: "I'd love to use our 30
  minutes to understand how your team handles analytics
  today, what you're trying to improve, and what your
  decision-process looks like. Is that OK?"
- Ask permission to take notes (signals respect for the
  customer's time).

What *not* to do in the first 30 seconds:

- Launch into your pitch.
- Apologize for the cold start ("sorry, I just joined the
  team").
- Over-claim ("I've been doing this for 20 years").

### Tip 2: Take visible notes

In an in-person call, take notes on paper or a laptop. In
a video call, take notes in a way the customer can see
(some tools support this). The visible notes signal
listening and respect.

Don't take notes so obsessively that you stop engaging.
The 70/30 rule: 70% of your attention on the customer,
30% on your notes. The notes are a memory aid, not a
transcript.

### Tip 3: Pause after the customer speaks

The most underused move in customer interactions. After
the customer finishes a thought, pause for 1-2 seconds
before responding.

Why this works:

- It signals thoughtfulness.
- It gives the customer space to add more.
- It prevents the SA from jumping to a solution
  prematurely.
- It feels slower, but it's actually faster — fewer
  misunderstandings, less rework.

The 1-2 second pause feels awkward to the SA. The
customer doesn't notice it. The interview rubric loves
it.

### Tip 4: Use the customer's vocabulary

When the customer says "ingestion," you say "ingestion,"
not "the data-plane component." When the customer says
"our data team," you say "your data team," not "the
analytics organization."

This is harder than it sounds. Most SAs have internal
vocabulary that leaks into customer calls. The fix is
*active listening* — every time the customer uses a
word, mentally note it, and use it back.

### Tip 5: Ask the second question

The customer says X. The junior SA answers X. The senior
SA asks "tell me more about X" or "what's behind X?" —
the question the customer *should* be asking.

This is the move that signals senior SA on every call.
It also surfaces the underlying pain, which is where the
real deal value lives.

### Tip 6: Summarize before you answer

Before responding to a complex question, restate the
question in your own words: "Let me make sure I heard
you correctly — you're asking whether we can handle
[X, Y, Z]. Is that right?"

This does 3 things:

- It confirms your understanding (saves you from
  answering the wrong question).
- It signals listening to the customer.
- It buys you 5-10 seconds of thinking time.

### Tip 7: Have a "backup" for every demo

Every demo will break. Have a backup:

- A recorded version of the same demo.
- A different browser.
- A different machine.
- A slide that walks through the same content.
- A whiteboarding session on the same topic.

The backup doesn't need to be perfect. It just needs to
be *available* so the recovery is fast.

### Tip 8: When you don't know, say so

The junior SA fakes it. The senior SA says "I don't
know, but here's how I'd find out."

Why this works:

- It's honest. Customers can tell when you're faking.
- It builds trust.
- It defers to a follow-up where you can have the right
  answer.
- It's the move that the bar-raiser round specifically
  tests.

A worked example:

> **Customer:** "Does your product support [obscure
> feature]?"
>
> **Junior SA:** "Yes, absolutely." (Lies. Doesn't
> know.)
>
> **Senior SA:** "I'm not sure about that specific
> feature — let me check with the product team and get
> back to you by EOD. If we don't support it, I'll let
> you know what's the closest alternative."

The senior SA is the one the customer trusts at month 6.

### Tip 9: End with a specific next step

The single biggest failure mode in customer-interaction
rounds is the weak close. "Let me know if you have
questions" is a weak close. "I'll send a 1-page technical
brief by Thursday, and we'll set up a 60-minute
whiteboarding session for Tuesday at 2pm. Does that
work?" is a strong close.

The 3 components of a strong close:

1. **Summary** — restate the customer's pain and what
   you've agreed to.
2. **Next step** — specific deliverable, specific date.
3. **Backup** — alternative if the primary next step
   doesn't work.

### Tip 10: Treat the interviewer as a collaborator, not an adversary

The interview is not a hostile test. The interviewer is
trying to evaluate you, but they're also trying to help
you succeed. The senior SA moves:

- Ask the interviewer for clarification if you don't
  understand the scenario.
- Use the interviewer's hints (they often drop them
  when you're on the wrong track).
- Treat the round as a *collaboration* — "let's figure
  out the right answer together" reads as senior SA;
  "I have to defend my position" reads as defensive.

The bar-raiser round at AWS is the canonical example.
The interviewer will give you signals — nod, follow-up
questions, body language — that you're on the right
track. Use them.

## 2. The 3 things interviewers watch for

Across the 10 tips, there are 3 underlying signals
interviewers watch for:

1. **Customer focus.** Is the candidate centered on the
   customer's problem, or on their own solution pitch?
2. **Listening.** Is the candidate actually hearing what
   the customer says, or are they following a script?
3. **Trust-building.** Is the candidate behaving in a
   way that makes the customer want to continue the
   conversation?

The 3 signals compound. A candidate who hits all 3 will
get the offer. A candidate who hits 1-2 will get
downleveled. A candidate who hits 0 will fail.

The 10 tips are the *practices* that produce the 3
signals. They are not the signals themselves.

---

## 3. The "5 minutes before the round" routine

In the 5 minutes before each customer-interaction round,
do this:

1. **Re-read the scenario** (if provided in advance).
   Note the customer's likely pain, the likely objections,
   the qualification questions.
2. **Plan the first 30 seconds** (Tip 1). The first 30
   seconds are scripted; the rest is adaptive.
3. **Plan the close** (Tip 9). Have a specific next
   step in mind.
4. **Take 3 deep breaths.** This sounds silly; it works.
   Customer-interaction rounds are performance rounds, and
   your body needs to settle.

The 5 minutes are the difference between a candidate who
walks in cold and a candidate who walks in calibrated.
The difference shows in the first 30 seconds, which
shapes the rest of the round.

---

## 4. The "5 minutes after the round" routine

In the 5 minutes after each customer-interaction round,
do this:

1. **Write down 3 things that went well.** Be specific.
2. **Write down 3 things that could be better.** Be
   specific.
3. **Write down 1 thing to practice before the next
   round.** Singular focus.
4. **Re-set.** Take 3 deep breaths. The next round is
   separate.

The post-round routine is the highest-leverage 5 minutes
of your prep. It converts the experience into a lesson.
Without it, the round is a memory. With it, the round is
a stepping stone.

---

## Try it

For your next 3 customer-interaction mocks (with a
friend), commit to 1 of the 10 tips. Pick the tip that
maps to your biggest failure mode. Run the round with
the tip as your focus. After the round, write down how
the tip changed your behavior.

Rotate through 3 different tips over 3 rounds. By the end
of the 3 rounds, you'll have internalized 3 of the 10.
After 10 rounds, you'll have internalized all 10.

The 10 tips are the *practice*, not the goal. The goal
is the 3 signals: customer focus, listening, trust-
building. The 10 tips produce the 3 signals when
practiced consistently.
