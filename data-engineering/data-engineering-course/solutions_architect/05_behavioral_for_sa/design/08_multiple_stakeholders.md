# 08 — Tell Me about a Time You Worked Across Multiple Stakeholders

> **Lesson 8 of 11 — Behavioral for SAs** · ~12 min

The SA role is *inherently* cross-functional. The senior
SA move is to make cross-functional collaboration feel
*effortless* — even when it's hard. A worked STAR story.

---

## 1. The question

> *"Tell me about a time you worked across multiple
> stakeholders."*

This question tests 4 signals:

- **Stakeholder mapping.** Can you identify who matters
  in a complex engagement?
- **Communication.** Can you tailor your message to
  different stakeholders with different priorities?
- **Diplomacy.** Can you navigate conflicting priorities
  without losing trust?
- **Outcomes.** Did the cross-functional work land a
  result?

The senior SA move is to *name the stakeholders
specifically* (not "the team"), to *name their
different priorities* (not "they all wanted the same
thing"), and to *show how you tailored your
communication* (not "I just communicated well").

---

## 2. The worked STAR story (Alex, Sr. SA at Databricks)

> **Situation (12 sec):** "I was the SA on a $5M
> manufacturing deal — the customer, anonymized due
> to NDA, was a Fortune 500 manufacturer building a
> predictive maintenance platform on top of a
> data lakehouse. The deal had 5 stakeholders: the
> VP of Engineering (the economic buyer), the
> Director of Data (the technical buyer), 2 senior
> data engineers (the technical evaluators), and the
> CISO (the security approver)."
>
> **Task (10 sec):** "I was the technical owner of
> the engagement. I was accountable for all 5
> stakeholders aligning on the architecture and
> security posture, and for the deal closing on
> time."
>
> **Action (60 sec):** "The 5 stakeholders had 5
> different priorities. The VP of Engineering cared
> about cost and time-to-value. The Director of Data
> cared about the team's skills and operational
> burden. The senior data engineers cared about the
> performance and ergonomics of the platform. The
> CISO cared about the security posture and the data
> residency requirements.
>
> I designed the engagement around the 5 priorities.
> The VP of Engineering got a 30-minute cost-and-
> value overview call once a week. The Director of
> Data got a 60-minute technical deep-dive every
> 2 weeks, focused on operational burden and team
> skills. The senior data engineers got 2 hands-on
> working sessions per week, where they actually
> used the product. The CISO got a 90-minute security
> review with our security team, focused on the
> specific compliance requirements.
>
> The breakthroughs came at the seams. When the CISO
> raised a data-residency concern that would have
> killed the deal, I brought the Director of Data
> into a follow-up session to align on a hybrid
> architecture that addressed the CISO's concern
> without disrupting the team's workflow. The
> Director of Data became the CISO's advocate,
> because they understood the operational impact.
>
> The senior data engineers flagged an
> ergonomics issue during the hands-on sessions that
> I'd designed the architecture to work around. I
> re-architected within 48 hours to address their
> feedback, which earned their trust."
>
> **Result (15 sec):** "The deal closed at $5M, 3-
> year commitment. All 5 stakeholders signed off.
> The VP of Engineering told us it was the smoothest
> cross-functional evaluation they'd been through.
> The senior data engineers wrote a public-facing
> case study (with our anonymization protocol) and
> became reference customers."
>
> **Reflection (10 sec):** "The transferable lesson:
> in any multi-stakeholder deal, *each stakeholder
> needs their own engagement*. Trying to address
> everyone on one call usually fails because the
> priorities diverge. I now design multi-stakeholder
> engagements with a separate cadence for each
> stakeholder group, plus joint sessions at the
> seams where alignment is needed. The pattern
> works across deal sizes and customer industries."

---

## 3. The 4 signals the story hits

1. **Stakeholder mapping.** Alex named all 5
   stakeholders with their titles and priorities. Not
   "the customer team" or "the stakeholders" — 5
   *named* people with 5 *different* priorities.
2. **Communication.** Alex designed a different cadence
   for each stakeholder group (30-min cost call, 60-
   min technical deep-dive, 2 hands-on sessions, 90-
   min security review). The communication was
   *tailored*, not generic.
3. **Diplomacy.** Alex brought the Director of Data
   into the CISO conversation to bridge the priorities.
   Alex re-architected within 48 hours to address
   the engineers' feedback. Both are *bridge* moves,
   not *push* moves.
4. **Outcomes.** The deal closed at $5M. All 5
   stakeholders signed off. The customer became a
   reference. The outcomes are quantified.

The story is a 4/4.

---

## 4. The "5 stakeholders" framework

The senior SA pattern for multi-stakeholder deals.
The framework:

| # | Stakeholder | Their priority | The engagement cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Economic buyer** | Cost, time-to-value, ROI | 30-min business call once a week |
| 2 | **Technical buyer** | Team skills, operational burden | 60-min technical deep-dive every 2 weeks |
| 3 | **Technical evaluators** | Performance, ergonomics | 2 hands-on sessions per week |
| 4 | **Security approver** | Compliance, security posture | 90-min security review once |
| 5 | **End-user / user's manager** | Workflow fit | 1 site visit / shadowing session |

The 5 stakeholder categories are the *spine*. Not
every deal has all 5; some have 3-4. The framework
is what you adjust from.

The key insight: **different stakeholders need
different engagement cadences**. Putting them all
in one call usually fails because the priorities
diverge.

---

## 5. The "engagement design" move

The senior SA move is to *design the engagement*,
not just react to it. The design has 3 components:

1. **Per-stakeholder cadence.** Each stakeholder group
   has a separate engagement (call, working session,
   etc.) with their own agenda.
2. **Joint sessions at the seams.** When 2 stakeholders
   need to align, a joint session brings them together.
   The seams are the highest-leverage moments.
3. **A shared document.** A central 1-pager (or 5-page
   doc) that all stakeholders can reference. Updates
   after each session are emailed to all stakeholders
   (with appropriate scoping).

The 3 components are the *practice* of multi-stakeholder
engagement. The story is a side effect.

---

## 6. The "what's hard about it" reflection

The senior SA reflection on multi-stakeholder work is
not "I communicated well." It's "here's what's
specifically hard about it, and here's how I've
adapted."

A weak reflection:

> "I learned to communicate with different
> stakeholders."

A strong reflection:

> "The specifically hard part is that the cadences
> conflict. The VP of Engineering wants to close fast,
> but the CISO needs 3 weeks for security review. The
> Director of Data wants to validate the architecture,
> but the engineers want to validate the ergonomics.
> Trying to please everyone at once usually fails.
> I've learned to *sequence* the engagements: open
> with the engineers (fast feedback), layer in the
> Director of Data (technical alignment), then the
> VP of Engineering (cost alignment), then the CISO
> (security review). The sequencing gets everyone
> their moment without the conflicts."

The strong reflection *names what's hard* and *names
the adaptation*. The interviewer reads this as "this
candidate has a model of the work."

---

## 7. The 3 anti-patterns

### Anti-pattern 1: "I worked with a lot of people"

You tell the story as "I worked with many people on
a complex project." The stakeholders are vague. The
interviewer reads this as "I don't have a model of
the stakeholder map."

**Fix:** Name the stakeholders specifically. Their
titles, their priorities, their engagement cadence.

### Anti-pattern 2: "I communicated well"

You tell the story as "I made sure everyone was on
the same page." The communication is vague. The
interviewer reads this as "I don't tailor my
communication."

**Fix:** Show how you tailored. "The VP got X, the
Director got Y, the engineers got Z." The
tailoring is visible.

### Anti-pattern 3: "It all worked out"

You tell the story as "the deal closed, everyone was
happy." The conflicts are absent. The interviewer
reads this as "I'm glossing over the hard parts."

**Fix:** Name the conflicts. "The CISO had a concern
that would have killed the deal." "The engineers
flagged an issue." The conflicts are visible, and
your resolution is the lead.

---

## Try it

Identify a real multi-stakeholder engagement you
worked on. Use the 5-step process from Lesson 04.

Specifically, for this question:

- Name 3-5 stakeholders with their titles.
- Name each stakeholder's priority.
- Describe the engagement cadence for each.
- Describe at least 1 seam where 2 stakeholders
  needed to align.
- Quantify the outcome.

If you can't recall the specific stakeholders and
priorities, your story is too generic. The senior SA
move is to be specific; even if the names are
anonymized, the structure of the engagement should
be visible.

Re-tell out loud. Record. Listen back. The first
time, you'll hear the moments where the
stakeholders blur together or the engagement cadence
is vague. Reframe those. By the 3rd time, the story
will be clean.
