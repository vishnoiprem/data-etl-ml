# 01 — Behavioral Questions for Solutions Architects

> **Lesson 1 of 11 — Behavioral for SAs** · ~15 min

The 12 most-asked behavioral questions in SA interviews,
organized into 4 categories. The senior SA move: have 8-
10 stories ready that can answer 2-3 questions each.

---

## 1. The 12 questions, in 4 categories

### Category 1: Customer focus (4 questions)

These test whether the candidate centers the *customer*
in their work — not their team, not their manager, not
their product.

1. **"Tell me about a time you solved a complex problem
   for a customer."** (The classic. See Lesson 04.)
2. **"Tell me about a time you influenced a customer's
   technical direction."** (See Lesson 07.)
3. **"Tell me about a time you had to push back on a
   customer."** (When the customer was wrong, and you
   had to say so.)
4. **"Tell me about a time you lost a customer."** (Or
   a deal. See Lesson 06.)

### Category 2: Cross-functional collaboration (3 questions)

These test the candidate's ability to work across teams
— sales, product, engineering, support — and across
stakeholders — economic buyer, technical buyer, end
user.

5. **"Tell me about a time you worked across multiple
   stakeholders."** (See Lesson 08.)
6. **"Tell me about a time you disagreed with sales and
   how you handled it."** (See Lesson 05.)
7. **"Tell me about a time you had to align product and
   customer."** (When the customer's ask didn't match
   the product roadmap.)

### Category 3: Technical judgment (3 questions)

These test the candidate's *technical* decision-making
in the context of a customer engagement.

8. **"Tell me about a time you had to learn a new
   technology quickly for a customer."** (See Lesson 09.)
9. **"Tell me about a time you recommended a technical
   approach the customer disagreed with."** (And how you
   resolved the disagreement.)
10. **"Tell me about a time you made a technical mistake
    with a customer."** (And what you learned.)

### Category 4: Personal growth (2 questions)

These test self-awareness and growth — the meta-signals
of a senior candidate.

11. **"Tell me about your biggest pre-sales win."** (See
    Lesson 10.)
12. **"Tell me about a time you failed with a customer
    and what you learned."** (Different from "lost a
    customer" — this is about *your* failure, not the
    deal's outcome.)

---

## 2. The signal behind the question

Every behavioral question tests 1-2 underlying signals.
The 4 signals:

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Customer focus** | Does the candidate center the customer in their work? |
| **Cross-functional collaboration** | Can the candidate work across teams and stakeholders? |
| **Technical judgment** | Can the candidate make defensible technical decisions under customer pressure? |
| **Self-awareness** | Does the candidate have a model of their own strengths and growth areas? |

The 4 signals are the rubric. A 4/4 answer hits 1-2
signals explicitly. A 2.5/4 answer hits them implicitly
or not at all. A 1/4 answer misses them.

---

## 3. The 3 differences from general DE behavioral

The 3 differences between SA behavioral and general DE
behavioral:

| Dimension | General DE behavioral | SA behavioral |
|---|---|---|
| **Protagonist** | Your team, your manager, your project | **Your customer** (or external stakeholder) |
| **Outcome metric** | Engineering (latency, uptime, cost) | **Business** (deal won, customer outcome, revenue impact) |
| **Failure framing** | Process, technical, team | **Customer-impacting** (the customer suffered, and you recovered) |

The senior SA move is to *naturally* anchor on the
customer. The junior SA move is to anchor on the
internal team, the manager, or the project. The
interviewer is watching for the protagonist of the
story.

---

## 4. The 5 common failure modes

The 5 ways candidates fail the SA behavioral round:

### Failure mode 1: Internal-engineering stories

You tell a story about how you shipped a feature, led
a design review, or fixed a bug. The customer is not
mentioned. The interviewer reads this as "I haven't
done customer-facing work at the level the SA role
requires."

**Fix:** Reframe every story with a customer. If the
story is about shipping a feature, the customer is the
one who asked for the feature (or the one who benefited
from it). If the story is about a design review, the
customer is the one whose architecture was reviewed (or
the one who'll use the design).

### Failure mode 2: Vague outcomes

You say "the customer was happy" or "the deal went
well" without specific numbers. The interviewer can't
tell if the outcome was material.

**Fix:** Quantify. "The deal was $1.2M/year, 3-year
commitment, with 5 expansion opportunities identified."
"Customer NPS went from 32 to 67 in 6 months." "The
architecture we designed reduced their infrastructure
cost by 40%."

### Failure mode 3: No reflection

You tell a 90-second story, stop, and wait for the
next question. The interviewer reads this as "I don't
have a model of my own growth."

**Fix:** End every story with a 10-15 second reflection:
"the transferable lesson is X" or "the change I made
after that is Y" or "I now think about this kind of
problem differently because of Z."

### Failure mode 4: Blaming others

You tell a story where someone else (a teammate, a
manager, a customer) screwed up and you had to fix it.
The interviewer reads this as "I don't take ownership."

**Fix:** Even when others were involved, frame the
story around *your* actions. "I noticed the issue,
I took ownership of the fix, and I..." — the others
are mentioned as context, not as the protagonist.

### Failure mode 5: No customer

The most subtle failure mode. The story is *about* a
customer, but the customer is unnamed, generic ("a
Fortune 500 customer"), or absent. The interviewer
reads this as "I'm not used to working with specific
customers."

**Fix:** Name the customer (or anonymize specifically:
"a Fortune 500 financial-services customer, who I
can't name due to NDA"). Generic references ("a
customer we worked with") read as evasive.

## 5. The 8-10 story rule

A senior SA candidate has 8-10 STAR stories ready,
each 90-120 seconds long. The 8-10 stories cover the
4 categories in roughly equal weight:

| Category | Stories needed |
|---|---|
| **Customer focus** | 2-3 |
| **Cross-functional collaboration** | 2-3 |
| **Technical judgment** | 2-3 |
| **Personal growth** | 1-2 |
| **Total** | **8-10** |

The 8-10 stories are *not* 8-10 different experiences.
They are 6-8 different experiences, where some
experiences answer 2 questions. For example, "I solved
a complex problem for a customer" might also answer
"tell me about a time you learned a new technology
quickly" if the technology was new to the candidate.

---

## 6. The "tell me about yourself" question

Not on the list of 12, but asked in every loop. The
SA-flavored version:

> *"I'm a [current title] with [X] years experience.
> The work I'm proudest of is [customer-flavored
> achievement]. Three things I'd want you to know
> about me: [customer focus], [technical depth],
> [cross-functional collaboration]. I'm talking to
> [company] because [specific reason tied to the
> role]."*

The 60-second answer is the *frame* for the rest of
the loop. Set the customer-focus signal in the first
30 seconds, and the rest of the loop is easier.

---

## Try it

For each of the 4 categories, write down 2-3 stories
from your own experience. Don't worry about length;
write the full story with as much detail as you can
remember. The exercise is to get the stories *out of
your head and onto paper*.

Once you have 8-10 stories written, do Lesson 02's
worksheet to refine them into the 90-second version.

If you don't have 8-10 stories, the gap is *experience*,
not prep. Get more customer-facing experience (shadow
a current SA, take on a customer-facing project, do
an informational interview with a current SA) before
interviewing.
