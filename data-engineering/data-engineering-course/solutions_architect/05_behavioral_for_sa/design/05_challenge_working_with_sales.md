# 05 — Tell Me about a Challenge You Faced Working with Sales

> **Lesson 5 of 11 — Behavioral for SAs** · ~12 min

A common SA behavioral question: working with the sales
team (the AE, the sales engineer, the deal team). A
worked STAR story, with 2 variations.

---

## 1. The question

> *"Tell me about a challenge you faced working with
> sales."*

This question tests 3 signals:

- **Cross-functional collaboration.** Can you work
  across the sales/engineering boundary?
- **Customer focus.** Even when working with sales,
  do you center the customer?
- **Diplomacy.** Can you disagree with sales without
  blocking the deal?

The senior SA move is to acknowledge the friction
*between* sales and engineering as a *natural* tension
(not a blame story), and to position yourself as the
*bridge*.

---

## 2. The worked STAR story (Maya, Sr. SA at Salesforce)

> **Situation (12 sec):** "I was the SA on a $4M
> healthcare deal at my previous company. The deal
> was in late stage, with a verbal commitment from
> the customer's VP of IT and a target close date 6
> weeks out. I was brought in 8 weeks before close to
> do the final architecture review."
>
> **Task (8 sec):** "I was the technical owner of
> the customer's architecture sign-off. I was
> accountable for the customer's CTO approving the
> architecture and for the deal closing on time."
>
> **Action (55 sec):** "Three weeks into the
> engagement, after I'd done the architecture review,
> I found a major issue: the customer's HIPAA
> compliance posture didn't match our standard
> offering. The AE on the deal didn't want to flag
> this — he was worried it would jeopardize the
> verbal commitment.
>
> I had a hard conversation with the AE. I told him:
> if we close this deal without addressing the HIPAA
> gap, the customer's compliance team will catch it
> during implementation, the deal will blow up, and
> we'll lose the customer. Better to surface it now.
>
> I proposed: we go to the customer together, surface
> the gap as a finding from the architecture review,
> and offer 2-3 mitigation paths. The AE was nervous
> but agreed. We scheduled the call.
>
> The call went well — better than either of us
> expected. The customer's CTO appreciated the
> transparency, and we worked out a 90-day mitigation
> plan that the customer could absorb. The deal
> closed on time."
>
> **Result (15 sec):** "The deal closed at $4M, 3-
> year commitment. The customer's CTO specifically
> called out the architecture review as the moment
> they knew they could trust us. The 90-day
> mitigation was completed on schedule. The deal
> expanded 18 months later by $1.8M/year."
>
> **Reflection (10 sec):** "The transferable lesson:
> AEs and SAs have different definitions of 'good
> news' for a deal. The AE wants to close; the SA
> wants to close *sustainably*. When those diverge,
> the right move is to surface the issue *with* the
> AE, not behind their back, and to propose a
> customer-facing framing that preserves the
> relationship. I've now codified this — every
> major deal I work on, I do a 'compliance sanity
> check' with the AE in the first 2 weeks so we
> don't get surprised late."

---

## 3. The 3 signals the story hits

1. **Cross-functional collaboration.** Maya worked
   *with* the AE, not against him. The hard conversation
   was face-to-face, with a proposed solution.
2. **Customer focus.** The customer's HIPAA compliance
   was the lead. The CTO appreciated the transparency.
   The customer's expansion 18 months later is the
   proof.
3. **Diplomacy.** Maya didn't go over the AE's head.
   She didn't escalate to her own management. She
   resolved it with the AE directly.

The story is a 4/4.

---

## 4. The 2 variations for your own story

### Variation A: Focus on the customer-facing element

Same story, reframed around the customer:

> "...The customer's CTO was the deciding vote. I
> didn't want to put him in a position where he
> felt we were hiding something — that would have
> ended the trust. The right move was to surface
> the gap with a mitigation plan, so the
> conversation became 'we found this, here's how
> we handle it' instead of 'you didn't catch
> this'..."

This variation emphasizes the customer-trust angle.

### Variation B: Focus on the disagreement and resolution

Same story, reframed around the disagreement:

> "...The AE and I disagreed on whether to surface
> the HIPAA gap. He was worried about jeopardizing
> the deal; I was worried about the customer's
> outcome. I scheduled a 30-minute conversation with
> him, came prepared with the customer's
> perspective, and proposed a joint approach. We
> came out aligned, and the joint call to the
> customer was actually what closed the deal..."

This variation emphasizes the disagreement-and-
resolution angle.

---

## 5. The 4 differences from a generic "disagreement at
work" story

The SA-flavored version of "challenge with sales" has 4
differences from a generic disagreement story:

1. **The protagonist is the customer.** The customer is
   who both the SA and the AE serve. The disagreement
   is about *how* to serve the customer, not about
   internal politics.
2. **The resolution preserves both relationships.** Maya
   resolved the disagreement with the AE without going
   over his head. She preserved both the AE
   relationship and the customer relationship.
3. **The customer outcome is quantified.** The deal
   closed, the customer expanded 18 months later.
4. **The reflection is systemic.** Maya didn't just
   learn from the experience; she codified it (the
   "compliance sanity check" in the first 2 weeks).

The 4 differences are the *senior SA* signals. A junior
SA would tell this story as a blame-the-AE story ("the
AE was wrong, I had to fix it"). The senior SA tells
it as a customer-facing resolution.

---

## 6. The 3 anti-patterns for this question

### Anti-pattern 1: Blaming the AE

You tell the story as "the AE was wrong, I had to
fix it." The interviewer reads this as "I can't work
across teams."

**Fix:** Frame the AE as a partner with a different
perspective. Acknowledge their concerns; explain how
you resolved them.

### Anti-pattern 2: Internal politics

You tell the story as "my manager disagreed with the
sales VP, and I was caught in the middle." The
interviewer reads this as "I get caught in crossfire."

**Fix:** Resolve it with the AE directly. If escalation
is needed, frame it as "I asked my manager to help us
align" — not "I was pulled into a political fight."

### Anti-pattern 3: No customer

The story is about AE / SA dynamics, with the customer
as a distant context. The interviewer reads this as
"the customer isn't my main focus."

**Fix:** The customer is the lead. The SA-AE dynamic is
how you serve the customer better. The customer outcome
is the proof.

---

## Try it

Write your own "challenge working with sales" story
using the 5-step process from Lesson 04. Then write the
2 variations (focus on customer; focus on disagreement-
and-resolution).

Tell each version out loud. Time yourself. Adjust until
each is 90-120 seconds.

If you don't have a strong SA-sales challenge story, you
can also reframe a senior-engineering-cross-team
conflict story. The reframe: even if the original story
was about engineers, the *SA version* anchors on the
customer impact of the resolution. The customer becomes
the protagonist; the team dynamics are the context.

If you can't reframe any story this way, the gap is
*sales/engineering boundary experience*. Get more of
it (shadow a current SA, partner with the sales team
on a project, take on a customer-facing role) before
interviewing.
