# 18 — Working with Up-Level Managers and Skip-Levels

> **Lesson 18 of 21 — Cross-functional Collaboration** · ~20 min

Managing your manager, skip-level conversations, and the 3
conversations you have to have with your boss each quarter.
Managing up is the substrate for everything else in this
module — without a good relationship with your skip-level,
the PM partnership (Lesson 17), the stakeholder comms
(Lesson 20), and the influence work (Lesson 19) all get
harder.

---

## 1. The 3 conversations with your boss each quarter

Most EMs treat their skip-level relationship as reactive —
they talk to their boss when there's a problem, and that's
it. The senior move is to **proactively run 3 conversations
each quarter** with your skip-level. These are non-negotiable.

### Conversation 1: The "where are we going" conversation
(mid-quarter)

This is the strategic alignment conversation. The senior move
is to **bring your own view of the team's direction**, not
just to listen.

The script:

> *"I want to make sure we're aligned on where the team is
> going. Here's my view: [1-page summary of the quarterly
> roadmap (Lesson 11), with the strategic framing]. What's
> your view? Where do you see the team fitting into the
> broader org direction? Where am I missing context that
> would change my plan?"*

The senior move: **leave with specific commitments** —
"I'll incorporate [X] into the next sprint plan by [date]."

### Conversation 2: The "what's the biggest risk" conversation
(mid-to-late quarter)

This is the risk-flagging conversation. The senior move is
to **name the risk before the boss has to ask**.

The script:

> *"I want to flag the biggest risks I'm seeing right now.
> (1) [Risk 1: specific, with the impact and the mitigation
> I'm considering]. (2) [Risk 2]. I don't need you to fix any
> of these, but I want you to know they're on my radar and
> I'd love your input on the mitigations."*

The mistake: waiting for the boss to ask "what's the risk?"
The boss who has to ask is the boss who thinks the EM is
hiding things.

### Conversation 3: The "what do you need from me" conversation
(end of quarter)

This is the meta-conversation about the relationship. The
senior move is to **ask for feedback explicitly**.

The script:

> *"I want to check in on how we're working together. What's
> working well? What's not working? What do you need from me
> in the next quarter that I'm not currently providing? And
> what's the most useful thing I could stop doing?"*

The senior move: **act on the feedback in the next quarter**.
The boss who gives feedback and sees no change is the boss
who stops giving feedback.

## 2. The skip-level conversation (your boss's boss)

Skip-levels are tricky. They're 2 levels up the chain — your
boss's boss. The senior move is to **have a quarterly
skip-level**, with a specific purpose, and to keep your
boss informed about what was discussed.

The purpose of the skip-level:

- **For the skip-level:** they want to know what's
  happening on the ground, unfiltered.
- **For you:** you want the skip-level context that your
  boss may not be sharing.

The script for the quarterly skip-level:

> *"I want to check in on the team and the work, separate
> from the operational stuff I handle with [my boss].
> Three things I want to surface: (1) [team-level win
> from the quarter]. (2) [team-level risk or concern].
> (3) [a specific question I have about the org strategy
> that my boss may not have the full context on]."*

The senior move: **tell your boss what you're going to
discuss**. "I have a skip-level with [skip] next week.
Here's what I'm planning to surface: [the 3 items above].
Is there anything you'd want me to add?" The boss who knows
what's coming is the boss who doesn't feel bypassed.

## 3. The disagreement with your boss

Every EM disagrees with their boss at some point. The
senior move is to **disagree in writing, with a specific
alternative**, not in the meeting.

The pattern (from Lesson 02's worked example):

1. **Don't push back in the meeting.** The boss has
   already aligned with their boss; pushing back in the
   room looks like insubordination.
2. **After the meeting, send a 1-page follow-up.** The
   structure:
   - "I want to flag a concern about [decision]."
   - "The risks I'm seeing are [3 specific risks, with
     the impact]."
   - "Here's a specific alternative: [the alternative,
     with the tradeoff]."
   - "If we go with the original plan, here's what I'd
     want to do to mitigate: [the mitigations]."
3. **Ask for a follow-up.** "Can we get 15 minutes to
   discuss before [date]?"

The mistake: pushing back in the meeting in a way that
puts the boss on the defensive. The senior move is the
written follow-up, which lets the boss change their mind
without losing face.

## 4. The "managing up" cheat sheet

The 5 things every senior EM does for their boss:

1. **Bring solutions, not problems.** "We have a problem
   with X" is a problem. "We have a problem with X, here
   are 3 options, here's my recommendation" is a solution.
   The senior move is to **always bring at least one
   option** to every escalation.
2. **Pre-document the meeting.** Send the agenda and any
   pre-reads 24 hours in advance. The boss who reads in
   advance is the boss who makes better decisions in the
   meeting.
3. **Be predictable.** Same tone, same cadence, same
   level of detail every time. The boss who can predict
   how you'll react is the boss who trusts you.
4. **Disagree in private, support in public.** Disagree
   in the 1:1. Support the decision in front of the team.
   The senior move is to never undermine your boss in
   front of others.
5. **Manage their calendar.** Senior bosses are
   over-scheduled. The EM who offers "I can take that
   meeting for you" or "I'll write the doc and you can
   forward it" is the EM who gets more face time.

## 5. A worked example: the disagreement with my director

From Lesson 02's worked example — Sam disagreed with the
director on the reorg. The follow-up doc:

> **Subject:** Concern about the proposed reorg
>
> *I want to flag a concern about folding the platform
> team into the broader data org. The risks I'm seeing:*
>
> *(1) The 3 senior engineers who joined specifically
> for the platform work are at material retention risk —
> 2 of them have explicitly mentioned the integration
> in the last 6 months.*
>
> *(2) The peer EM's team has a feature-focused culture;
> the platform team's roadmap-focused culture would be
> subsumed.*
>
> *(3) The integration would consume 2-3 months of
> management cycles for the team, during which the
> streaming migration would slip.*
>
> *Here's a specific alternative: 2-track integration
> (light integration on tooling, separate roadmaps and
> on-call rotations). Estimated cost: 30% of the original
> integration effort, with materially less retention and
> execution risk.*
>
> *If we go with the original plan, the mitigations I'd
> propose: (1) explicit retention conversations with the
> 3 senior engineers before the announcement, (2) a
> 2-quarter freeze on roadmap changes to preserve the
> platform work.*
>
> *Can we get 15 minutes this week to discuss before the
> announcement?*

**What makes this land:** The risks are specific (named
engineers, specific cultural concerns, specific timeline
impact). The alternative is specific (2-track with the
estimated cost). The mitigations are explicit. The ask is
specific (15 minutes, this week, before the
announcement).
