# 02 — Creating Your EM Story Bank

> **Lesson 2 of 21 — Overview** · ~30 min

The end-to-end process for mining 12-15 EM-flavored stories from your
career, organized around the 5 categories from Lesson 01. Do this
once, well, and you'll have interview material for the next 5 years.

This is the highest-leverage lesson in the track. Block 3-4 hours.

---

## 1. Why 12-15 (not 10)

A 6-question People Management round covers 4-5 distinct categories
(see Lesson 01). With 12-15 stories you have:

- 2-3 stories per category (so you can pick the best fit, not the
  only fit)
- 3-5 "spare" stories for the inevitable question you didn't
  anticipate (e.g., the interviewer asks about your most painful
  layoff story and you don't have a layoff story — having a
  "downsizing" or "RIF" adjacent story in the bank saves you)
- Enough variety to span your management arc (your first 1:1, your
  first PIP, your first promo, your first reorg, your biggest
  retention save)

Most candidates stop at 5-7 stories. That's a coverage problem. The
EM move is to have *more than you need*.

For this track specifically, target 12-15 stories, weighted toward
the managing-down and crisis categories (the bulk of the work) and
lighter on managing-up and managing-self (which are 1–2 questions
each in most loops).

---

## 2. The mining process

Total time: 3-4 hours over 1-2 weeks. Do it in 3 passes.

### Pass 1: The people dump (60-90 min)

Set a 60-minute timer. Open a blank document. Title it: "PEOPLE I'VE
MANAGED." Don't stop writing for the full 60 minutes. For every
person who has reported to you (directly or indirectly), or with whom
you've had a meaningful mentoring / coaching relationship, write:

- Name (or pseudonym — "Sam, the senior engineer with 7 years of
  Kafka")
- Role and tenure when you worked with them
- 1-line description of who they were at the start
- 1-line description of what they grew into (or didn't)
- The 1-2 specific things you did that contributed
- The 1 outcome that anchors the relationship (promo, retention,
  scope expansion, or — honestly — a firing or a quit)

If you're an IC who's interviewing for an EM role, the equivalent
dump is: people you've mentored, people you've onboarded, peers
whose careers you've influenced, and (critically) the 2-3 people
you've handled the hardest disagreements with.

You should end this pass with 8-15 people. Most will be small
relationships. A few will be obvious "headline" relationships — the
engineer you grew from E4 to E6, the engineer you had to put on a
PIP, the engineer who quit and you wish you'd handled it differently.

### Pass 2: Categorize and tag (60-90 min)

Take the people dump. For each person, tag the strongest story
they'd anchor with:

- **Category** (managing down / managing up / managing sideways /
  managing self / crisis)
- **Specific question(s) it answers** (pick 2-3 from the canonical
  22 PM + 131 Behavioral in `docs/reference/em_interview_canonical_questions.md`)
- **Number** (yes / no — does it have a quantifiable outcome?)
- **Quality** (1-5 — gut feel for the story's strength)
- **Pain** (1-5 — how uncomfortable is the story to tell? High-pain
  stories are usually the highest-signal ones, because they
  demonstrate self-awareness)

The output is a 2-axis matrix, like this for a hypothetical M5
candidate:

```
                   Stories   Avg Quality   Best Question
Managing down        5         4.0         "Tell me about a time you had a difficult coaching conversation."
Managing up          2         3.5         "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."
Managing sideways    3         3.8         "Tell me about a time you influenced a peer team."
Managing self        2         4.5         "Tell me about a time you failed as a manager."
Crisis               1         3.0         "Tell me about a time you had to make an unpopular call."
```

The matrix tells you where you have coverage and where you have
gaps. The gaps are your prep priorities for the rest of this module.

For this candidate, the "crisis" row is light — 1 story, quality 3.0.
That's the prep priority: find a second crisis story, ideally one
with a higher pain score.

### Pass 3: Write the top 12-15 (90-120 min)

Pick the 12-15 stories that have:

- The highest quality + pain combination
- The best category coverage (fills gaps)
- At least one number (a promo, a retention save, a project outcome)
- The clearest decision you made (not "the team decided" — what did
  *you* decide?)

For each, write a 90-120 second spoken version. Use STAR (Situation /
Task / Action / Result) with the EM-specific overlay from Lesson 01:
the IC is the protagonist, you're the supporting character, the
"so what" lands on the person who grew.

The format I recommend for each story in your bank:

```
TITLE: [One-line name, e.g. "The 7-year Kafka expert"]
CATEGORY: [Managing down / Managing up / etc]
QUESTION(S) IT ANSWERS: [2-3 specific questions from the canonical list]
RUBRIC ROW: [People growth / Performance mgmt / etc]
THE PERSON: [2-3 sentences on who they were at the start]
THE STORY (90-120 sec spoken):
[Write it out as you'd say it, in first person, present tense
where possible. Lead with the person. End on what they became.]
THE NUMBER: [The 1-2 numbers that anchor the impact]
THE TAKEAWAY: [One sentence, the beat you end on]
FOLLOW-UP I'LL PROBE FIRST: [The follow-up I want them to ask]
```

The "follow-up I'll probe first" field is the EM move. Every story
has multiple angles, and you should be ready for the interviewer to
push on any of them. Pre-deciding which follow-up you want to talk
about lets you steer there.

For the "Kafka expert" example, the natural follow-up is "how did
you handle the engineer's identity work in the moment?" — and that's
exactly the question you want to be asked, because your answer is
your management philosophy made concrete.

## 3. Quality bar for each story

Every story in the final bank should pass these tests:

- [ ] The 5-question "so what" test (4+/5 — does it land on people
      outcomes, not project outcomes?)
- [ ] Names a specific person by role and characteristic (not "a
      junior engineer" — "the junior who'd been on the team 6 months
      and was afraid to ask 'dumb' questions")
- [ ] Describes who the person was at the start AND who they grew
      into (the before/after is the rubric)
- [ ] Speaks in 90-120 seconds when timed
- [ ] Has at least one specific number (promo level, retention
      outcome, project metric, headcount moved)
- [ ] Doesn't blame anyone (especially not the person whose story
      you're telling)
- [ ] Ends on a beat about the person, not about you
- [ ] Passes the "would I tell this in front of the person?" test
      (if no, rewrite or remove)

If a story doesn't pass all 8, it's not ready. Either rewrite it
or remove it.

The last test is the one most candidates skip. If you wouldn't tell
the story in front of the engineer it describes — if you'd be
embarrassed or they'd be hurt — then it doesn't belong in the
interview. Either find the angle that's respectful to them, or pick
a different story.

---

## 4. The story-bank doc

Build your bank in a single searchable document. Format doesn't
matter — Notion, Google Doc, spreadsheet, Markdown file. What
matters is:

- **Searchable** by category (so you can pull the right story fast)
- **Editable** in real time (so you can refine after every interview)
- **Portable** to your phone (so you can skim before an interview)
- **Versioned** (so you can see how a story has evolved over
  multiple interviews)

The senior move is to **reread your bank the morning of every
interview**. 5 minutes. Just skimming the titles and the takeaways.
The re-read primes the stories so they're at the front of your mind
when the question lands.

## 5. A worked example: 5 stories from the canonical 245

For concreteness, here's 5 EM-flavored stories for a hypothetical
M5 candidate (Sam, from the Module 04 mock interview). Each is
anchored to a specific question from
`docs/reference/em_interview_canonical_questions.md`.

### Story 1: The 7-year Kafka expert

**Canonical source:** People Management — *"Tell me about a time when
you had a disagreement with your manager."* + Behavioral — *"Tell
me about a time you had to coach someone through a career change."*

**Category:** Managing down
**Rubric row:** People growth

> *"When I took over the data platform team, my senior-most engineer
> had been the Kafka expert for 7 years. He was technically
> excellent and emotionally skeptical about the streaming migration
> I'd been hired to lead. He'd built his identity around the legacy
> batch pipeline, and he was worried the new system would make him
> irrelevant.*
>
> *My first 1:1 with him, I asked what he was most proud of from
> the last 3 years. He talked for 30 minutes about the 2019 outage
> postmortem he'd written. Then I asked what he wanted to be doing
> in 3 years. He said, 'Honestly, I don't know — I keep getting
> pulled into Kafka work because nobody else can do it, and I've
> stopped growing.'*
>
> *What I did: I split the migration into 2 tracks. I owned the
> new streaming pipeline (where his Kafka expertise was an asset).
> I asked him to own the legacy decommission AND the runbook for
> the new system — both areas where he could grow from
> 'implementer' to 'operator-of-record.' I paired him with our
> junior engineer on the first 3 tickets so the knowledge transfer
> was explicit. 6 months later he'd written the internal training
> on the new stack and was presenting it to 3 other teams. He got
> promoted to E6 within a year. He told me later that the
> conversation about identity was the unlock — 'nobody had asked
> me what I wanted before.'*

**What makes this land:** Names a specific person with a specific
identity crisis. The manager's move is diagnosis-first — asking
what they're proud of, what they want — before prescribing. The
outcome is growth on multiple axes (technical, scope, promo). The
final quote is the rubric beat: people grow when they feel seen.

### Story 2: The engineer who wouldn't listen to feedback

**Canonical source:** People Management — *"Tell me about a time you
had to deliver hard feedback."* + Behavioral — *"Tell me about a
time when you had a conflict with someone on your team."*

**Category:** Managing down
**Rubric row:** Performance management

> *"I had an E5 on my team, call her Priya, who'd been a strong
> IC for 2 years but was struggling in her new tech-lead role. Her
> code reviews were getting longer, she was blocking 2 junior
> engineers with nitpicks, and her peers were starting to avoid her
> in design reviews.*
>
> *First conversation: I told her what I'd observed — not 'you're
> being a bottleneck' but 'the last 3 PRs you've reviewed have
> taken 4 days each, and 2 engineers have told me they've stopped
> sending you the early drafts.' She got defensive. I held the
> line — 'I'm not saying you're wrong to want high quality, I'm
> saying the cost is too high.' We agreed on a behavior change:
> review within 24 hours, escalate to me if the PR needs a bigger
> conversation rather than blocking.*
>
> *2 weeks later, the pattern hadn't shifted. Second conversation:
> I named the pattern, asked what was getting in the way, and
> listened. She broke down and told me she'd been given the tech-lead
> role without a clear scope, and she was over-reviewing because
> she didn't trust her own judgment on what was in-scope. The
> fix wasn't more feedback — it was a 1-page scope doc we wrote
> together.*
>
> *6 weeks in, her review turnaround dropped to under 24 hours,
> the 2 junior engineers were unblocked, and 2 of them
> specifically told me they were learning more from her reviews
> now. She got promoted to E6 within a year and is one of the best
> tech leads I've worked with. The lesson: when someone's
> performance dips, ask what's behind it before assuming it's a
> motivation problem."*

**What makes this land:** Names the misdiagnosis explicitly — the
fix wasn't feedback, it was a scope document. That's the EM beat:
you can see your own behavior change in real time. The outcome is
specific and measurable. The takeaway is a transferable lesson.

### Story 3: Disagreement with my director on a reorg

**Canonical source:** People Management — *"Tell me about a time
when you had a disagreement with your manager."* + Behavioral —
*"Tell me about a time when you had to push back on a decision."*

**Category:** Managing up
**Rubric row:** Influence, Self-awareness

> *"My director wanted to fold my 6-person platform team into a
> larger 18-person 'data org' led by a peer EM. I disagreed — the
> peer EM's team had a fundamentally different culture (more
> feature-focused, less platform-focused), and I thought the
> integration would lose the 3 engineers who'd specifically joined
> for the platform work.*
>
> *I didn't push back in the meeting — I could tell he'd already
> aligned with his VP. After the meeting I sent a 1-page doc:
> the 3 specific risks, the 3 specific engineers I was worried
> about losing, and a 2-track alternative (light integration on
> tooling, separate roadmaps and on-call rotations).*
>
> *He replied within 4 hours and agreed to the 2-track alternative.
> The reorg happened on the lighter integration path. None of the
> 3 engineers quit. 18 months later, the platform team is still
> distinct within the broader org, and the integration has been
> smoother than the original plan would have been. The pattern:
> flag the disagreement in writing, with a specific alternative,
> after the meeting rather than during it. My director told me
> later the doc was the reason he changed his mind."*

**What makes this land:** Doesn't blame the director. Chooses the
medium (writing) for tactical reasons, not avoidance. Provides a
specific alternative, not just disagreement. Names the 3 specific
engineers and the specific risk (quit). Pattern propagated.

### Story 4: PM who kept changing scope

**Canonical source:** Behavioral — *"Tell me about a time you had to
work with a difficult stakeholder."* + *"How would you respond if
your team disagreed with your ideas?"*

**Category:** Managing sideways
**Rubric row:** Influence, Collaboration

> *"I was tech-leading a 6-month data quality project. The PM
> changed scope 4 times in the first 8 weeks — each change was
> small, but they accumulated to a 30% scope expansion. My team
> was getting demoralized because no matter how much they shipped,
> the finish line kept moving.*
>
> *What I did: I scheduled a working session with the PM and
> asked her to walk me through *why* each scope change had
> happened. Three of the four were legitimate — new stakeholder
> requirements that came in after the project started. One was
> the PM changing her mind about what success looked like.*
>
> *I proposed: (1) any future scope change goes through a
> 30-minute 'cost of change' conversation with both of us, where
> we estimate the timeline impact before agreeing; (2) we adopt
> a 1-page 'definition of done' that we both sign at project
> start; (3) any change to that doc is treated as a new project,
> not a scope tweak. She agreed to all three.*
>
> *Over the remaining 4 months, scope changed twice — both times
> through the new process, with explicit timeline renegotiation.
> We shipped 2 weeks late on the original timeline but within
> 10% of the renegotiated one. The 'cost of change' pattern
> became the team's default for the next 3 projects."*

**What makes this land:** Diagnoses the pattern (4 small changes =
30% expansion) before prescribing. Names the legitimate reasons for
the changes (not all blame on the PM). Proposes a specific process,
not a complaint. The "1-page definition of done" pattern propagated.
Outcome is measurable and slightly imperfect (2 weeks late) — that's
the senior move, not claiming victory.

### Story 5: The on-call rotation that broke

**Canonical source:** People Management — *"Tell me about a time
when you had to make an unpopular call as a manager."* + Behavioral
— *"Tell me about a time you had to manage a crisis."*

**Category:** Crisis / ambiguity
**Rubric row:** Execution, Judgment

> *"We had a 4-person on-call rotation. One of the engineers had
> been on call for 6 weekends in a row — way over his share —
> because the rotation kept falling back on him. I noticed when
> his responses in Slack started getting shorter and his PR
> throughput dropped 40% week-over-week.*
>
> *I pulled him into a 1:1. He told me he hadn't said anything
> because he didn't want to be 'not a team player,' but he was
> burning out and his partner had asked him to consider leaving
> the team.*
>
> *What I did: I redesigned the rotation that week — explicit
> handoffs, no one person could carry more than 1 in 4 weekends,
> a 'secondary' shadow slot for new joiners, and a hard 'no
> on-call the week after a Sev1' rule. I announced it on the
> team channel before the next rotation. The senior engineer
> who'd been over-carrying sent me a private message that
> afternoon: 'thanks for seeing it.'*
>
> *Retention outcome: he didn't quit. He was promoted to E6
> within 18 months. The new rotation has held for 2 years with
> no further retention saves needed. The lesson: when you see
> the pattern, name it before the engineer has to."*

**What makes this land:** Diagnoses via signal (Slack brevity, PR
throughput) before the engineer names it. Names the systemic cause
(uneven rotation), not just the symptom (one engineer burning out).
Proposes a specific structural fix, not just a band-aid. The "thanks
for seeing it" quote is the rubric beat: the engineer felt seen.

## Try it

Start your people dump. 60 minutes, no filter, just write. Don't
worry about quality. Just get the raw material.

If 60 minutes feels too long, do 30 minutes and stop. You can do
the second pass tomorrow. The point is to start.

After the dump, do Pass 2: tag each person with category, question,
number, quality. You'll have a coverage matrix by the end of the
day.

---

## Action item

Block 3-4 hours this week for Pass 3: write 12-15 STAR stories in
the format above. For each, time yourself telling it out loud —
target 90-120 seconds. Stories that run long are usually missing
the patience. Stories that run short are usually missing the
specifics.

When you're done, you have the raw material for the next 19 lessons.
Every lesson in Modules 02-05 will reference stories from this
bank.
