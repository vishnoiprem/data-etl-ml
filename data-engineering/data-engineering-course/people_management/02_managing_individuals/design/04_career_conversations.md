# 04 — Career Conversations: Levels and Promotions

> **Lesson 4 of 21 — Managing Individuals** · ~25 min

The engineering ladder (E3-E7 / L3-L7), how promo packets are
written, and the 4 conversations you have to have in any
promotion cycle. The career conversation is the most important
1:1 topic you'll cover — it's where the engineer either sees a
future at the company or starts interviewing.

---

## 1. The engineering ladder

Every company phrases the ladder slightly differently, but the
shape is the same. Here's the synthesis, calibrated to Meta and
Google but recognizable across Stripe, Airbnb, the AI labs, the
banks, and most public-company engineering orgs.

| Level | Years of experience (typical) | What they're expected to do |
|---|---|---|
| **E3 / L3** (SWE) | 0-2 | Owns small features with guidance. Learning the codebase and the craft. |
| **E4 / L4** (SWE) | 2-5 | Owns medium-sized projects end-to-end. Reliable execution on ambiguous tasks. |
| **E5 / L5** (Senior) | 5-8 | Owns large projects, often cross-team. Sets direction within their area. |
| **E6 / L6** (Staff) | 8-12 | Owns org-level technical direction. Multiplies the impact of other senior engineers. |
| **E7 / L7** (Senior Staff / Principal) | 12+ | Owns company-level direction. Defines the technical strategy for a major area. |
| **E8 / L8+** (Distinguished / Fellow) | 15+ | Industry-level impact. Cross-company influence. |

Above L7 the ladder diverges by company. At Meta, L8 is "Distinguished
Engineer" (a small cohort). At Google, L9 is "Google Fellow"
(fewer than 20 in the company). At smaller companies, the ladder
collapses — what would be an L7 at Google is "the staff engineer"
at a 200-person startup.

The EM's job at the L5+ levels is to **grow the engineer's scope,
not just their output**. An E5 who ships 3x the average E5's
output is a strong E5, not an E6. An E5 who grows 2 E4s and ships
2x the average is on the E6 track.

## 2. The 4 promotion conversations

Every promotion cycle has 4 distinct conversations. Most EMs run
them in the wrong order. The order matters.

### Conversation 1: Where are they now? (start of cycle, 6-9 months
before promo)

Run this in a 1:1, not in a formal review. The point is to get
the engineer an honest read on where they are relative to the
next level, before they've done the work.

Useful questions:
- "What do you think it would take to be ready for [next level]
  by the next promo cycle?"
- "Looking at the [level] bar, where do you think your gaps are?"
- "What would I observe about your work in the next 6 months that
  would tell me you're ready?"

The senior move is to be honest about the gaps early. Most EMs
sandbag the early conversation ("you'll be ready when the time
comes") and then have a hard conversation at the end of the cycle
("you're not ready yet"). That sequence is the single biggest
source of promo-cycle disappointment.

### Conversation 2: What does the evidence look like? (mid-cycle,
3-4 months before promo)

This is the "show me the work" conversation. The engineer needs
to be able to point to specific projects, specific decisions,
specific scope that demonstrates the next level. Not "I led the
migration" — "I led the migration, here are the 3 specific
decisions only an E6 would have made, here's the impact, here's
what I'd do differently next time."

Useful questions:
- "Walk me through your last 6 months. Which projects best
  demonstrate [next level]?"
- "If you were writing your own promo packet, which 3 pieces of
  evidence would you lead with?"
- "What gaps in that evidence are you most worried about?"

### Conversation 3: The packet (1-2 months before promo)

The promo packet is the document the promo committee reads. The
EM writes it (or co-writes it with the engineer), and it's the
single most important piece of writing the EM does all cycle.

A good packet has:
- **A clear "this is an E6" statement in the first paragraph.**
  Don't make the committee infer the level.
- **3-5 specific examples of [next level] work.** Each with a
  decision, a tradeoff, an outcome, and a number.
- **A "what the engineer did that an E5 wouldn't have" section.**
  This is the discriminator. The committee is asking: *is this
  person doing E6 work, or doing exceptional E5 work?*
- **A list of the engineer's growth areas.** Promo packets that
  pretend the engineer is perfect get distrusted. Honest packets
  get trusted.

A useful template:

```
PROMO PACKET — [Name] — [Current Level] → [Next Level]

ONE-SENTENCE STATEMENT:
[Name] has been operating at the [Next Level] bar for the last
[N] months, demonstrated by [N] specific examples below.

EXAMPLE 1: [Project name]
- Context: [1-2 sentences on the situation]
- Decision: [The specific call they made, including the
  alternative they considered and rejected]
- Impact: [Number, with timeframe]
- Why this is [Next Level] work: [1-2 sentences on what an
  engineer at [Current Level] would have done differently]

EXAMPLE 2-5: [Same structure]

DISCRIMINATORS:
- [Specific ways the engineer's work exceeded the [Current
  Level] bar — e.g., scope, ambiguity, influence, mentorship]

GROWTH AREAS:
- [Honest, specific — the committee trusts this section more
  when the rest of the packet is strong]

QUOTES:
- [2-3 specific quotes from peers / cross-functional partners
  / skips that corroborate the bar]
```

### Conversation 4: The committee and the outcome (after the
packet is submitted)

The EM's job in this conversation is to **advocate**. The packet
goes to a calibration committee, which is a room of 5-10 EMs and
senior ICs who decide if the packet is strong enough. The EM
presents the packet (typically 5-10 minutes), then fields
questions.

The senior move is to **know the rubrics better than the
committee does**. If a committee member asks "why is this E6 and
not strong E5?" the EM has a specific answer with a specific
example. If the EM can't answer, the promo dies.

After the committee, the EM has a 1:1 with the engineer about
the outcome. If it's a yes, the conversation is "congratulations,
here's what I observed about the work that got you there, here's
what E6 work looks like in your first 6 months at the new
level." If it's a no, the conversation is harder — see Module 03
on underperformance and on the "not yet, here's the gap" cycle.

## 3. The "not yet" conversation

Most EMs avoid this. They hope the engineer figures it out from
the outcome, or they give a vague "it was close, try again next
cycle." Both moves are corrosive.

The senior move is to be specific about the gap, with a specific
plan for closing it. The conversation is:

> *"The committee's feedback was that your [technical scope /
> cross-functional impact / project leadership] is at the [next
> level] bar, but your [specific gap] isn't yet. Here's what
> 'yet' looks like: [specific projects, specific decisions,
> specific timeframes]. If you do those things over the next 6
> months, the next cycle's conversation is a different one. Let's
> pick 2-3 of them and write them into your goals."*

The worst version: "you weren't ready this cycle, work harder."
The best version: "here's the specific gap, here's the specific
work that closes it, here's the timeline, I'll be in your 1:1s
every 2 weeks to track it."

## 4. A worked example: the E5→E6 packet for Priya

Priya (from the GROW 1:1 example) has been operating at the
senior IC level for 18 months. Sam is writing her E5→E6 packet.

> **PROMO PACKET — Priya — E5 → E6**
>
> **ONE-SENTENCE STATEMENT:** Priya has been operating at the E6
> bar for the last 18 months, demonstrated by her work leading
> the data quality initiative, the schema migration, and the
> cross-team on-call redesign.
>
> **EXAMPLE 1: Data quality initiative (6 months)**
> - **Context:** Our top customer was churning because of bad
>   data in the dashboard. The team had been treating it as a
>   series of one-off bugs for 18 months.
> - **Decision:** Priya reframed the problem as a systemic
>   quality issue, proposed a 6-month project to add end-to-end
>   data validation, and negotiated a 2-month timeline extension
>   with the PM to do it right. She considered and rejected the
>   alternative ("just fix the top 10 bugs and move on") because
>   she was certain they'd recur.
> - **Impact:** Dashboard error rate dropped from 8% to 0.4%.
>   Top customer renewed at $4.2M/year. The validation framework
>   is now used by 3 other teams.
> - **Why this is E6 work:** An E5 would have fixed the top 10
>   bugs and shipped. Priya identified the systemic cause,
>   proposed a 6-month project, and negotiated the timeline with
>   cross-functional stakeholders. She also mentored 2 junior
>   engineers through the implementation, both of whom she
>   subsequently nominated for E4 promo.
>
> **EXAMPLE 2: Schema migration (4 months)**
> [...same structure, with a different decision, a different
> stakeholder, a different number, and a different E5-vs-E6
> discriminator...]
>
> **DISCRIMINATORS:**
> - Scope: Priya's projects consistently span 2-3 teams and
>   multiple stakeholder groups.
> - Ambiguity: She's comfortable taking a problem with no
>   definition and producing both a definition and a path
>   forward.
> - Mentorship: She's grown 2 E4s to the point where they're
>   ready for promo. Both credit her explicitly.
> - Cross-functional: She's the de facto tech lead for the
>   data-PM relationship — the PM team seeks her out before
>   scoping data projects.
>
> **GROWTH AREAS:**
> - Long-term technical strategy. Priya is excellent at the
>   project level; she's less practiced at the multi-quarter
>   roadmap level. Specific plan: she's co-authoring the 2026
>   data platform roadmap with me, due in 6 weeks.
> - Written communication for senior stakeholders. Her PR
>   descriptions are excellent; her design docs are
>   inconsistent. Specific plan: she and I will co-write her
>   next 2 design docs, and I'll give her feedback on the
>   senior-stakeholder version.
>
> **QUOTES:**
> - "Priya's data quality project is the model I use for what
>   'E6 judgment' looks like. She saw the systemic cause when
>   the rest of us were still arguing about individual bugs."
>   — Aarav, E6 (peer)
> - "I'd hire Priya onto my team in a heartbeat. She's the
>   engineer I go to when I want someone who will see around
>   corners." — Director of Data (cross-functional partner)

**What makes this land:** The "E5 vs E6" discriminator is the
committee's actual question, and the packet answers it
explicitly. The growth areas are honest but specific. The quotes
are from people the committee will recognize.

## Try it

Pick one of your directs. Write a promo packet for them at the
next level, even if you're not in a promo cycle. Use the
template above. Notice which sections feel easy and which feel
hard — the hard sections are your coaching priorities for that
person.

If you're an IC interviewing for an EM role, the equivalent
exercise is: pick a person you've mentored, write the
equivalent of the packet for their growth, and identify the
specific decisions you made as their mentor that contributed.

---

## Action item

Schedule the 4 promotion conversations for your next cycle. Put
them on the calendar now — Conversation 1 (where are they now?)
is the highest-leverage and the most-skipped.
