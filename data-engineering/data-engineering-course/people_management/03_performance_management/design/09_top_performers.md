# 09 — Recognizing and Retaining Top Performers

> **Lesson 9 of 21 — Performance Management** · ~25 min

Retention tactics, special projects, comp adjustments, and the 5
frameworks for keeping your best people from leaving. Top
performers are the engine of the team — losing one is a 6-12
month setback; losing 2 in the same quarter is a reorg.

This is the most important lesson in the module per the spec.
The 5 retention frameworks below are the part to internalize.

---

## 1. The 5 retention frameworks

Most EMs wait until a top performer has one foot out the door
before they act. The senior move is to run 5 retention frameworks
continuously, so the conversation never becomes a "please stay"
panic.

### Framework 1: The career trajectory conversation

**When to run it:** Every 1:1, but formally once a quarter.

**What it is:** A specific conversation about where the engineer
is going. Not "are you happy?" — "where do you want to be in 12
months? What's the gap between here and there? What would close
the gap?"

The script:

> *"I want to check in on your trajectory. Where do you see
> yourself in 12 months? What would have to be true for you to
> be excited about being here 2 years from now?"*

The senior move: **listen for the gap between current state and
desired state.** Most retention problems are gap problems — the
engineer wants to grow in a direction the current role doesn't
support, and the EM hasn't noticed.

The fix: if the gap is closeable (e.g., the engineer wants
staff-managing experience and there's a stretch project coming
up), close it. If the gap isn't closeable (e.g., the engineer
wants to be a director and the org is flat), be honest about
that and have the "is this the right place for you" conversation
before they make it for you.

### Framework 2: The stretch project

**When to run it:** Quarterly, with each top performer.

**What it is:** A project that is genuinely above the engineer's
current level, with appropriate support. The point isn't to set
them up to fail — it's to give them a target that requires
growth.

The pattern:

- Identify a project that has 1-2 dimensions above the engineer's
  current scope (cross-team? multi-quarter? high-stakes
  stakeholder?).
- Propose it explicitly: "I want to give you [project] because
  it's the next-level work, and I think you're ready. Here's the
  support: [weekly check-ins, design-doc reviews, a sponsor from
  the partner team]."
- Coach through the project. Stretch projects are where
  promotions are made.
- After the project, debrief specifically on what the engineer
  learned, what they'd do differently, and what's next.

The mistake: giving top performers "more of the same." The
engineer who was the team's best E4 doesn't want another E4-
shaped project. They want the E5 work.

### Framework 3: The comp conversation

**When to run it:** At least annually, in the comp cycle. More
often if the engineer is at retention risk.

**What it is:** A specific conversation about compensation. Most
EMs avoid this. The senior move is to be transparent about the
comp range, the comp philosophy, and the specific levers.

The script:

> *"I want to walk you through how your comp is set. Your base
> is in the [X] percentile of the [level] range at the company.
> Your bonus is [Y]% of base, which is the standard for the
> level. Your equity refresh is [Z], which is on the higher end
> for the level. Here's the comp philosophy: we pay at the [60th
> / 75th / 90th] percentile of market, we refresh equity
> annually, we adjust for retention cases out of cycle. If your
> comp is a concern, I want to hear it — either now or any time
> in the next 6 months."*

The senior move: **don't wait for the engineer to ask**. The
engineer who's underpaid and hasn't said anything is the one
most at risk. Bringing it up first signals that you take their
comp seriously.

When you do adjust: be specific about the new number, the new
equity, the new title. The engineer should leave the conversation
with a clear picture of what changed and why.

### Framework 4: The "stay interview"

**When to run it:** Annually, for each top performer. More often
if signals suggest risk.

**What it is:** A reverse exit interview. The questions an exit
interview asks ("why are you leaving?") reframed for someone
who's staying ("what would make you leave?").

The script:

> *"I want to do a 'stay interview' with you. 5 questions. (1)
> What do you look forward to when you wake up for work? (2) What
> do you dread? (3) What's the one thing you'd change about the
> team if you could? (4) What would make you leave? (5) What
> would make you stay for another 3 years? Take your time on
> these. I'll follow up in 2 weeks with what I heard and what
> I'll do about it."*

The senior move: **follow up in writing**. The engineer gives
you signal in the stay interview. The signal is a gift. If you
don't act on it, the next stay interview is shorter and the
next exit interview is sooner.

### Framework 5: The retention save (when the engineer is already
interviewing)

**When to run it:** When you hear (directly or indirectly) that
the engineer is interviewing.

**What it is:** A specific 1:1 conversation designed to surface
the real reason and, if possible, close the gap. Most EMs do
this wrong — they either panic ("what can I do to keep you?")
or pretend they didn't hear ("I heard you're interviewing, let's
not talk about it").

The script:

> *"I want to have an honest conversation. I've heard you might
> be looking around. I'm not going to ask you to confirm or deny.
> I am going to ask: is there anything I can do, or that the
> company can do, that would make you want to stay? And if the
> answer is no, I want to make sure your last 3-6 months here
> are good ones, and that you leave with a clean reference and
> an open door."*

The senior move: **mean it**. The "clean reference and open
door" line is what gets you a graceful exit instead of a
disgruntled one. And sometimes the engineer stays, and the
honesty is what made them willing to stay.

If the engineer does stay, the next 90 days matter most. The
EM has to deliver on whatever was promised in the retention
conversation, in writing, in 1:1s. The retention save that
doesn't get followed through on is the next exit interview.

## 2. The "high performer with friction" problem

The hardest retention case is the top performer who creates
friction. This is canonical question #4 in the People
Management bank: *"How would you handle an engineer who creates
conflict with team members but is still performing well?"*

The 3-step pattern:

1. **Name the pattern explicitly.** The top performer is
   probably aware of the friction. They've gotten away with it
   because the work is good. The senior move is to say: "Your
   technical work is excellent. The way you work with [X team /
   Y peer] is creating real cost. We need to address the
   second part, not because the first part isn't good, but
   because the second part is limiting your scope."

2. **Make the friction as concrete as the work.** The top
   performer can argue about vague feedback ("I'm just direct").
   They can't argue with "3 of your last 4 design reviews had
   specific feedback from the partner team that the conversation
   felt dismissive. Here are the 3 incidents, with dates and
   quotes."

3. **Set a specific, time-bound expectation.** Same structure
   as the performance plan in Lesson 08, but framed as a growth
   plan, not a PIP. "In the next 60 days, I want to see 2
   cross-functional design reviews with positive written
   feedback. If the pattern doesn't shift, the consequence is
   that the next promo packet won't include the
   cross-functional-impact evidence we need."

The senior move: **don't let "high performer" exempt the
engineer from the same expectations as everyone else**. The
team sees the exemption. The high performer who never gets
feedback is the reason the next high performer leaves.

## 3. The 3 signals your top performer is at risk

You won't always have the explicit "I'm interviewing" signal.
The 3 indirect signals:

- **Slack brevity.** Short responses, slow replies, less emoji.
  Top performers who are engaged are verbose and warm in chat.
- **1:1 prep drop.** The engineer who used to bring a 1:1 doc
  with 3-4 topics now brings 0-1.
- **Scope reduction.** The engineer who used to volunteer for
  the hard project now declines. The "I'm heads-down on what I
  have" excuse is a flag.

When you see 2 of 3, run the stay interview (Framework 4) that
week. Don't wait for the explicit signal.

## 4. A worked example: retaining the senior engineer who
"isn't being challenged"

Aarav (from Lesson 04's promo packet) has been an E6 for 3
years. His 1:1 prep has dropped. He's been declining the
harder projects. Sam runs the stay interview.

> **Sam:** "I want to do a stay interview. 5 questions, take
> your time. (1) What do you look forward to at work? (2) What
> do you dread? (3) What would you change about the team? (4)
> What would make you leave? (5) What would make you stay
> another 3 years?"
>
> **Aarav:** "(1) The design work, when I get to do it. (2) The
> ops work. I spend 30% of my time on incidents and on-call
> handoffs and I don't grow from any of it. (3) I'd redistribute
> the ops work — the seniors carry the most because we're the
> most reliable, but that means the juniors don't grow into it.
> (4) If I spent another year doing 30% ops with no end in
> sight, I'd interview. (5) If the next 18 months had a clear
> E7 trajectory — bigger scope, less ops, mentoring 2 engineers
> to take the ops work — I'd be here for 5 more years."
>
> **Sam:** "What I heard: the ops distribution is the problem,
> not the work itself. Here's what I'll commit to in the next
> 2 weeks: a rotation proposal that moves seniors to 15% ops
> and grows 2 mid-level engineers into the senior ops slots.
> And a 12-month trajectory doc for you that names the E7 work
> and the E7 timeline. I'll follow up in writing by [date]."

**What makes this land:** Aarav's answers are specific. Sam's
commitment is specific. The follow-up is in writing, with a
date. The retention plan addresses the structural problem
(ops distribution) and the personal problem (E7 trajectory) at
the same time. Aarav isn't being retained with comp — he's
being retained with a path.

## Try it

Pick your strongest performer who's been on the team for 2+
years. Run Framework 4 (the stay interview) in your next 1:1.
Write down their answers. The answers will tell you either (a)
you have a retention problem you didn't know about, or (b)
you have signal that the engineer is happy and you can use it
to make the case for their next promo.

Either way, you have information you didn't have before.

---

## Action item

This quarter, run Framework 1 (career trajectory conversation)
with each of your top 3 performers. Write down the gap between
where they are and where they want to be. Then design a
specific project, conversation, or comp change that closes the
gap. Top performers don't stay by accident — they stay because
their EM is closing the gap deliberately.
