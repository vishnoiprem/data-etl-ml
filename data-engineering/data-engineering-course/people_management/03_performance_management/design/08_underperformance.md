# 08 — Handling Underperformance (PIPs and Beyond)

> **Lesson 8 of 21 — Performance Management** · ~30 min

The PIP, performance plans, termination, and the legal/HR
checklist. The hardest conversation in the EM job. Most senior
ICs have never done this work — this lesson is the framework
for the first time, with a checklist for the second-through-tenth
time.

This is the longest lesson in the module. Plan two passes.

---

## 1. The 3 stages of underperformance

Most underperformance doesn't go straight to termination. It
moves through 3 stages, and the senior move is to **handle each
stage explicitly, with documentation, before moving to the
next**.

| Stage | What it looks like | The EM's move |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1: Feedback** | The engineer is missing expectations in 1-2 specific, observable areas. | Coaching 1:1s, growth plan, regular check-ins. No formal documentation. |
| **Stage 2: Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)** | The engineer has had 2-3 cycles of feedback, hasn't improved, and the gaps are material to the current level. | Formal PIP, written expectations, weekly check-ins, 30/60/90 milestones, HR involved. |
| **Stage 3: Termination** | The engineer has had a PIP, hasn't met the milestones, and the team or business is materially affected. | Termination conversation, separation agreement, HR runs the process. |

The mistake new EMs make: jumping from Stage 1 (informal
feedback) straight to Stage 3 (termination) because the
feedback conversation is uncomfortable. That's the
**constructive dismissal** pattern, and it's both legally
exposed and corrosive to the rest of the team.

The other mistake: staying at Stage 1 forever, hoping the
engineer figures it out, and never moving to Stage 2 because
the PIP is also uncomfortable. That's the **failing to act**
pattern, and it corrodes the rest of the team in a different
way — the high performers see that underperformance has no
consequences, and they leave.

The senior move: **each stage has a specific trigger, a
specific document, and a specific conversation**. When the
trigger is met, you move to the next stage.

## 2. The performance plan (Stage 1→2 transition)

A performance plan is the formal document that transitions an
engineer from informal feedback to a PIP. It's written by the
EM, reviewed by HR, and shared with the engineer. The
structure:

```
PERFORMANCE PLAN — [Name] — [Level] — [Date]

WHY WE'RE HERE:
[2-3 sentences on the specific gaps. Reference earlier
conversations. Don't surprise the engineer with new
feedback — this doc should be a formalization of what
they've already heard.]

THE GAPS (3-5, specific and observable):
- [Gap 1] — [Specific example, with timeframe]
- [Gap 2] — [Specific example, with timeframe]
- [Gap 3] — [Specific example, with timeframe]

WHAT "MEETING EXPECTATIONS" LOOKS LIKE:
- [Specific, measurable, time-bound expectation 1]
- [Specific, measurable, time-bound expectation 2]
- [Specific, measurable, time-bound expectation 3]

THE SUPPORT WE'LL PROVIDE:
- [Specific support 1: e.g., weekly 1:1s, pairing with
  a senior engineer, a written design-doc review
  cadence]
- [Specific support 2]
- [Specific support 3]

THE TIMELINE:
- 30 days: [Specific milestone]
- 60 days: [Specific milestone]
- 90 days: [Final review — meets or doesn't meet]

WHAT HAPPENS IF THE PLAN ISN'T MET:
[Honest: "If the plan isn't met, we'll have a
conversation about whether this role is the right
fit." Don't name termination explicitly — HR will
guide the language here.]
```

The "what meeting expectations looks like" section is the
most important. Each expectation is **observable, measurable,
and time-bound**. "Improve cross-functional collaboration"
is not an expectation. "Lead 2 cross-functional design
reviews by day 60, with positive feedback from the partner
team" is.

## 3. The PIP conversation

The PIP conversation is the hardest conversation in the EM
job. The structure:

1. **Set the frame (3-5 min).** "I want to walk you through
   a performance plan we've been working on with HR. The
   plan formalizes the conversations we've been having about
   [gap 1, gap 2, gap 3]. I'll share the doc and walk you
   through it. Then I want to hear your reaction."

2. **Walk through the doc (15-20 min).** Read it together.
   Be specific. Cite the examples. The engineer should not
   be hearing any of this for the first time, but they may
   be hearing the *consequence* for the first time.

3. **Hear the reaction (10-15 min).** The engineer may be
   angry, sad, surprised, or all three. Listen. Don't
   defend. If the engineer has new evidence, thank them and
   say you'll factor it in. If the engineer wants to
   involve HR or a skip-level, support that — the process
   is designed to be transparent.

4. **Land on the next step (5-10 min).** "Here's what
   happens next: weekly 1:1s starting [date], 30-day
   milestone is [date], we'll review together at each
   milestone. If you have questions or concerns between
   now and then, my door is open."

The mistakes to avoid:

- **Apologizing.** "I'm sorry we have to do this." The
  apology signals that the process is wrong, not that the
  engineer is wrong. Don't apologize for the process; the
  process is designed to help the engineer improve.
- **Sugarcoating.** "This is just a formality." It's not.
  The PIP is a real thing with real consequences.
- **Promising outcomes you can't deliver.** "If you do
  this, I promise you'll be fine." You can't promise that.
  The plan is the plan; the outcome is the outcome.

## 4. The termination conversation (Stage 3)

The termination conversation is the one nobody wants to have.
The structure is different from the PIP conversation because
the outcome is already decided.

**The setup:** HR runs the process. The EM does not run the
termination alone. The conversation has the EM, an HR
partner, and the engineer. The engineer may have a
representative (lawyer, union rep, or coworker). The
conversation is 15-30 minutes.

**The script (HR will guide the exact language, but the shape
is):**

> **EM:** "Thank you for being here. [HR partner] is joining
> us. The purpose of this conversation is to talk about the
> end of your employment at [company], effective today.
> We've worked through the performance plan, and despite
> the support we provided, the gaps we identified haven't
> been closed to the level we need. We've decided to end
> the employment relationship. [HR partner] will walk you
> through the details of the separation."
>
> **HR:** [Walks through the separation agreement, last
> paycheck, benefits continuation, references, non-disparagement
> clauses, timeline for returning equipment.]

**The EM's role:** the EM says the first 90 seconds and then
defers to HR. The EM is not there to argue, defend, or
re-decide. The decision was made before the meeting.

**The mistakes:**

- **The EM is alone.** Always have HR.
- **The EM debates the decision.** Don't. The decision is
  made.
- **The EM says "I wish it had gone differently."** It's
  tempting, but it's a guilt-relief move that doesn't
  help the engineer. The senior move is to be present,
  human, and brief.
- **The EM doesn't follow up.** The day after termination,
  the EM should have a 15-minute check-in with the rest of
  the team (without naming details) acknowledging the change
  and inviting questions. The silence is corrosive.

## 5. The legal/HR checklist

This is the part of the lesson that doesn't get taught in
interview prep but matters most in the actual job. Run
through this checklist **before** every PIP or termination:

- [ ] **HR is involved from Stage 1.** Not Stage 2. The moment
      you start having a hard feedback conversation that
      *might* lead to a PIP, loop in HR. They will tell you
      what documentation you need, what to say and not say,
      and what the company-specific process is.
- [ ] **The performance issues are documented in 1:1s.** Every
      hard feedback conversation should be in the 1:1 doc
      with a date and a specific example. If the 1:1 doc
      doesn't have the feedback, it didn't happen.
- [ ] **The feedback is consistent across reviewers.** If
      you're the only one seeing the gap, that's a flag.
      Talk to your skip-level, peer EMs, and key
      cross-functional partners before formalizing.
- [ ] **The feedback is about the work, not the person.** "You
      missed 3 of the last 4 deadlines" is feedback about the
      work. "You're not detail-oriented" is feedback about
      the person and is legally exposed.
- [ ] **The engineer has had a chance to improve.** The PIP
      can't come as a surprise. The 1:1s should have laid
      the groundwork for months.
- [ ] **The performance plan is reviewed by HR before the
      conversation.** HR will catch language that sounds
      discriminatory, vague, or retaliatory.
- [ ] **The separation agreement is reviewed by the company's
      legal team.** Never sign a separation agreement
      without legal review.
- [ ] **The engineer has been treated consistently with
      peers.** If you're PIP-ing one engineer for
      underperformance but tolerating the same behavior in
      another, that's discrimination exposure.

## 6. A worked example: the PIP for Jordan

Jordan (from the Lesson 07 perf review) has had 2 cycles of
feedback on the same growth areas. The performance has not
improved. Sam is moving to Stage 2.

> **PERFORMANCE PLAN — Jordan — E4 — Effective [date]**
>
> **WHY WE'RE HERE:** This plan formalizes the feedback
> we've been discussing in our 1:1s since [date 6 months
> ago]. The growth areas from your H1 2025 review
> (cross-functional collaboration and project estimation)
> remain material, and we need to see them close in the
> next 90 days for the plan to be considered met.
>
> **THE GAPS:**
> 1. **Cross-functional collaboration.** In our last 1:1
>    cycle, 2 specific incidents came up: the data-science
>    design review on [date] where feedback from attendees
>    was that the conversation was dismissive, and the
>    on-call handoff on [date] where missing context
>    caused a 4-hour incident debug.
> 2. **Project estimation.** The legacy decommission went
>    from 6 to 9 weeks (50% slip), the consumer migration
>    went from 4 to 6 weeks (50% slip). Both slips were
>    traced to under-counted cross-team dependencies.
>
> **WHAT "MEETING EXPECTATIONS" LOOKS LIKE:**
> - Lead 2 cross-functional design reviews by day 60, with
>   positive feedback (in writing, from the partner team)
>   on the collaboration.
> - Ship the next 2 projects on or before the committed
>   timeline, with explicit cross-team dependency
>   estimation in the project plan.
> - Co-author a 1-page "how I work with cross-functional
>   partners" doc by day 30, to be reviewed with me.
>
> **THE SUPPORT WE'LL PROVIDE:**
> - Weekly 1:1s (vs. biweekly previously) for the duration
>   of the plan.
> - Pairing with [senior engineer] on the first design
>   review.
> - Review of the project plan before the kickoff of each
>   of the next 2 projects.
> - Access to the company's cross-functional communication
>   workshop.
>
> **THE TIMELINE:**
> - Day 30 (date): Co-authored "how I work with partners"
>   doc, signed off by both of us.
> - Day 60 (date): 2 design reviews completed, with
>   written feedback from the partner team.
> - Day 90 (date): Final review of all 3 expectations. If
>   met, the plan is closed and we return to standard
>   1:1 cadence. If not, we'll have a conversation about
>   next steps.
>
> **WHAT HAPPENS IF THE PLAN ISN'T MET:** We'll have a
> conversation at day 90 about whether this role is the
> right fit. [HR will guide the exact language here.]

**What makes this land:** Each expectation is observable
and time-bound. The support is specific. The timeline is
explicit. The "what happens if the plan isn't met" section
is honest without being threatening. The doc is the formal
record of a pattern the engineer has been hearing for
months.

## Try it

If you've ever had a hard feedback conversation that didn't
go well, write a 90-second answer about it using the
structure in this lesson. Be honest about what you would do
differently. The senior move in the interview is not "I've
always handled this perfectly" — it's "I handled it the way
I knew how at the time, and here's what I learned."

If you haven't had a hard feedback conversation, write a
90-second answer about a hard conversation you *did* have
(personal, peer, customer) and reflect on which parts of the
PIP framework would have applied.

## Action item

If you have a direct who is in the "feedback" stage right
now (Stage 1 of the underperformance framework), do 2 things
this week:

1. Document the feedback in your 1:1 doc with a specific
   example and a date.
2. Schedule a 1:1 specifically to walk through the feedback,
   even if you've raised it informally. The formality of the
   1:1 is what creates the audit trail.

If you don't have a direct in Stage 1, do the equivalent for
yourself: pick one area where you've been giving informal
feedback without formalizing, and schedule the 1:1.
