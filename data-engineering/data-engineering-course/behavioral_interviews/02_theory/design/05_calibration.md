# 05 — Calibration: Why the Same Answer Ranks Differently

> **Lesson 5 of 7 — Theory** · ~10 min

Two candidates can tell essentially the same story and walk out
with different scores. This lesson is about why, and what you can
do about it.

---

## 1. The variability is real

I have personally watched two candidates tell near-identical
"led a migration" stories to the same interviewer in the same
day, and one got a "strong hire" while the other got a "no
hire." The stories were structurally the same. The projects
were similarly scoped. The numbers were in the same ballpark.

The difference was in the **delivery and the framing**, not the
content. This is a real and intentional feature of senior
interviewing — the interviewer is calibrating not just *what*
you did, but *how* you describe it, because the latter is a
proxy for how you'll be in a 1:1 with them when things are
hard.

This lesson is about the variables you can actually control.

---

## 2. The 5 calibration variables

### Variable 1: Which story you pick

Same question, two stories. One is "I led a migration." The
other is "I mentored a junior engineer through a hard
project." Both are valid. One of them is the *better* answer
to the question you were asked.

**What you control:** your story bank. The fix is to have
multiple stories per category so you can pick the one that
fits the specific question.

### Variable 2: How you open

A 90-second answer that opens with *"I'll tell you about a
time I led a streaming migration with a hard deadline..."* is
memorable. One that opens with *"So basically I was working
on this project..."* is not.

**What you control:** the first sentence. Practice a clean,
headline-style opening for each story in your bank.

### Variable 3: How you frame the tension

The same project can be framed as "it was hard because the
deadline was tight" or "it was hard because the PM and I
disagreed about scope." The first framing makes the project
the protagonist. The second makes *you* the protagonist.

**What you control:** the framing. The senior move is to make
the *decision* (not the project) the protagonist. The
interviewer wants to know how you think, not what you worked
on.

### Variable 4: How you handle the follow-up

The interviewer will almost always ask a follow-up. The
follow-up is the real calibration moment. The first answer
was rehearsed. The follow-up is improvisation.

**What you control:** your breadth of story. If the follow-up
probes a different angle ("what would you do differently?")
and you have a ready answer, you score high. If you freeze
or repeat the same beat, you score low.

### Variable 5: How you land

The last 10 seconds of an answer is what the interviewer
remembers. *"Yeah, so that's the project"* is a fade. *"The
pattern has since become our default for schema changes on
the team"* is a beat.

**What you control:** the closing sentence. Write the closing
sentence in advance for every story in your bank. Practice
ending on it.

---

## 3. The "calibration ceiling" effect

Senior interviewers — Staff+, Principals, EM Directors — have
seen *thousands* of stories. They are saturated. The thing that
makes a candidate memorable is not the project. It's one of:

- An **unusual** angle ("the surprising thing was that the
  hardest part wasn't technical, it was the conversation with
  the partner's CTO about the schema")
- A **specific** anchor that the interviewer can visualize
  ("we had a wall in the team room with red and green sticky
  notes, and the green pile grew for the first 3 weeks")
- A **clean** takeaway that maps to a rubric row ("the pattern
  I took from this is X, and I now use it for Y")
- An **honest** moment of self-doubt that resolves into action
  ("I was genuinely unsure whether this would work, and what
  convinced me was...")

The project doesn't have to be impressive. The **angle** has
to be interesting.

---

## 4. Why "great candidates get rejected" happens

Most of the time it's not the candidate's fault. It's one of:

1. **The interviewer was calibrated wrong** — the interviewer
   was looking for a different signal and you demonstrated a
   different one.
2. **You told the wrong story** — the story you picked didn't
   fit the question as the interviewer heard it.
3. **You had a weak follow-up** — your first answer was good,
   the follow-up fell apart.
4. **The interviewer had 7 interviews that day and was tired**
   — calibration drifts. The first interview of the day is
   usually the most carefully calibrated.
5. **The interviewer was primed against you** — sometimes a
   prior round left a bad signal, and the interviewer is
   unconsciously looking for confirmation.

You can do very little about #4 and #5. You can do a lot about
#1, #2, and #3. The story bank, the per-question story mapping,
and the follow-up practice are all aimed at #2 and #3.

---

## 5. How to debias yourself

If you're running practice interviews with a friend, beware
the **halo effect**: one really good story in a 6-question
round will inflate the scores on the other 5. Train your
practice partner to score each answer independently and not
to "carry" the strong answer into the weak ones.

Similarly, beware the **anchoring effect**: the first answer
sets an anchor. If the first answer was strong, the second
answer (which might be a 3/4) will feel like a 4/4 by
comparison. The fix is to score each answer in isolation.

If you have access to a senior engineer who can run practice
interviews, **ask them to use the actual company rubric** (most
are public on engineering blogs). It will make your practice
much more calibrated to the real round.

---

## Try it

Find a friend and run a 3-question practice interview. Score
each answer independently (don't share scores until the end).
Then share. Notice the discrepancies — that's your calibration
data.
