# 04 — Signal vs. Noise: What Senior+ Interviewers Listen For

> **Lesson 4 of 7 — Theory** · ~15 min

The most important lesson in this module. The 5 specific signals
that separate "above bar" from "at bar" in the mind of a senior
interviewer. If you only read one lesson in Module 02, read this.

---

## 1. The mental model

A senior interviewer (Staff+, EM Director, principal engineer) is
running your 45-minute conversation through a filter that is
*much* noisier than yours. They are listening for **specific
acoustic signatures** in your answers — patterns that their years
of experience have taught them correlate with "this person will
be a good senior engineer."

The signatures are not the things candidates *think* they're
being graded on. They're not:
- "How impressive is your project?"
- "Did you use the right framework?"
- "Did you mention the right technologies?"

They're listening for:
1. **Decision under ambiguity** — did you name a call you made?
2. **Cost acknowledgment** — did you mention what was hard?
3. **Other-people awareness** — did you describe what others did?
4. **Specificity under pressure** — did you stay concrete when
   the question was abstract?
5. **Self-correction in real time** — did you adjust when
   challenged?

Each of these is a *signal* that the candidate has the cognitive
style of a senior engineer. None of them are about the project
itself.

---

## 2. The 5 signals in detail

### Signal 1: Decision under ambiguity

**What it sounds like:** *"The choice I made was..."* / *"I
decided to..."* / *"I called the meeting and proposed..."*

**What it tells the interviewer:** you make calls, you don't
just react. You're comfortable saying "I owned this decision."

**What the absence of it sounds like:** the candidate describes
work that happened, without ever naming a moment where they
chose. The work drifts through the story like it happened by
itself.

**Why it matters for senior+:** a senior engineer's job is
*primarily* to make calls that other people can't or won't make.
The interviewer's question is essentially: "Will this person
make the calls I would have to make, that I would want a senior
to make?"

**How to demonstrate:** every story should have at least one
sentence with the word "decided," "chose," "proposed," "pushed
for," or "called." If your story doesn't have one, your story
isn't a senior story.

### Signal 2: Cost acknowledgment

**What it sounds like:** *"The thing that was hard was..."* /
*"What I gave up was..."* / *"The hardest part was..."* / *"I
made a tradeoff against..."*

**What it tells the interviewer:** you understand that decisions
have costs, and you don't pretend they don't.

**What the absence of it sounds like:** the candidate's stories
are uniformly positive. Everything worked out. Nothing was hard.
Every project was a win.

**Why it matters for senior+:** a senior engineer is constantly
making tradeoffs (speed vs quality, scope vs timeline, technical
debt vs feature velocity). The interviewer wants to know you
*see* the tradeoffs, even if you don't talk about them in every
story.

**How to demonstrate:** every story should contain at least one
sentence acknowledging what was hard, what you gave up, or what
the cost was. Even a happy-ending story should have a beat of
"this was hard because X."

### Signal 3: Other-people awareness

**What it sounds like:** *"The PM was concerned about..."* /
*"My peer pointed out..."* / *"My manager pushed back on..."* /
*"The team disagreed on..."*

**What it tells the interviewer:** you see other people as
agents with their own views, not as obstacles or scenery.

**What the absence of it sounds like:** the candidate's stories
are populated by NPCs. "The team" did things. "Stakeholders"
were aligned. No individual ever had a name or a specific view.

**Why it matters for senior+:** senior engineers spend most of
their time on the people side — alignment, negotiation,
mentoring, feedback. The interviewer wants to know that you
*see* people, not just tasks.

**How to demonstrate:** in every story, name at least one
specific person (not "the team," but "Dana, the senior PM")
and describe one specific view they had that mattered.

### Signal 4: Specificity under pressure

**What it sounds like:** the candidate stays concrete even when
the question is abstract. "How do you handle ambiguity?" gets a
specific instance, not a philosophy lecture.

**What it tells the interviewer:** you think concretely, not
abstractly. You can ground a principle in an example on demand.

**What the absence of it sounds like:** the candidate gives
abstract answers to concrete questions and concrete answers to
abstract questions. The wrong way around. When asked for a
story, they give a principle. When asked for a principle, they
give a story.

**Why it matters for senior+:** the senior's job is to
*operationalize* — to take a vague mandate ("make our data
quality better") and turn it into a concrete plan. The
interviewer is testing whether you can do that translation
under pressure.

**How to demonstrate:** when the question is "how do you
handle X," answer with "I handle X by doing Y, which I learned
when Z happened." Not "I think X is important because..."
Always end with a story.

### Signal 5: Self-correction in real time

**What it sounds like:** when the interviewer pushes back, the
candidate *adjusts* their answer rather than defending it. "Oh,
that's a good point — let me reframe..." or "Actually, the way
I'd describe it now is..."

**What it tells the interviewer:** you can be wrong, gracefully.
You learn in real time. You don't dig in.

**What the absence of it sounds like:** when the interviewer
probes a weak spot, the candidate doubles down. "But no, that's
not what happened. Actually, what I meant was..."

**Why it matters for senior+:** a senior engineer gets things
wrong *constantly* — wrong estimates, wrong designs, wrong
assumptions. The thing that separates a good senior from a
bad one is the ability to update when the evidence changes.
The interview is testing whether you can do that *in this
room, right now*.

**How to demonstrate:** when the interviewer says "but isn't
that just X?", your first instinct should be to consider whether
they're right. If they are, say so. If they aren't, say so
*gently* and explain. Never dig in.

---

## 3. How to listen for these in your own answers

The hard part of this lesson is that you can't *consciously*
demonstrate these signals while you're also doing the work of
answering. The cognitive load is too high.

The fix: **build them into your story bank in advance.** When
you write a story, scan it for the 5 signals. If a signal is
missing, ask: *how would I tell this story differently to
include that signal?* Then practice the new version.

After enough practice, the signals become part of your default
delivery. You don't think about them. They just show up because
your stories are *built* to show them.

---

## 4. The interviewer's negative signals

It's also useful to know what **disqualifies** a candidate in
the first 30 seconds of an answer:

| Negative signal | What it tells the interviewer |
|---|---|
| Blame ("my manager didn't support me") | "This person will be hard to work with." |
| Generic ("I improved the system") | "This person can't get specific under pressure." |
| Hedging ("I think I might have done X") | "This person didn't own the outcome." |
| Name-dropping without substance ("we worked with the VP") | "This person is performing, not reflecting." |
| Self-aggrandizement ("I single-handedly...") | "This person overstates their contribution." |
| Long pauses with no follow-through | "This person is unprepared." |

Two of these in a single answer is a hard downlevel. Three is a
"no hire" regardless of what else the candidate says.

---

## Try it

Take your 4 strongest stories. For each one, score it 0-5 on the
5 signals (1 = missing, 5 = strongly present). Total score is
out of 25.

If a story is below 15, you have work to do on it. Either find a
new story, or rewrite the existing one to demonstrate the missing
signals.

The target: every story in your bank scores 18+ out of 25.
