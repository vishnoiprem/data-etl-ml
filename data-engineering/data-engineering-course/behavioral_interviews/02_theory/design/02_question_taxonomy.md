# 02 — Behavioral Question Taxonomy

> **Lesson 2 of 7 — Theory** · ~12 min

The 7 categories of behavioral questions, with 5 examples of each
and which rubric row each category tests. Knowing the taxonomy lets
you anticipate 80% of the questions before the interviewer asks.

---

## 1. Why the taxonomy matters

Most candidates walk into an interview with 3-4 stories and hope
the questions line up. This is a mistake. A 6-question round
covers 4-5 distinct categories, and if you have only 4 stories,
you'll either repeat a story (visible) or stretch a story to fit
a question it doesn't really answer (worse).

The fix: have **at least one story per category**, plus 2-3
"spare" stories that can flex. That's 10-12 stories total, which
is what Module 03 helps you build.

---

## 2. The 7 categories

### Category 1: Leadership / Influence

**What it tests:** can you drive outcomes through other people
who don't report to you?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you led a project without formal authority.
2. Tell me about a time you had to influence someone who disagreed
   with you.
3. Tell me about the most senior person you've had to convince of
   something.
4. Describe a time you had to get buy-in for a change that was
   unpopular.
5. Tell me about a time you had to motivate a team through a
   difficult period.

**Rubric row:** *Influence, Collaboration, Communication.*

**The senior move:** the story has a *named* person you
influenced, a *specific* tactic, and a *measurable* outcome.
"I convinced them by talking it through" is E4. "I wrote a 1-page
doc, sat with them for 30 min, and we landed on option C" is E5.

---

### Category 2: Conflict / Disagreement

**What it tests:** can you disagree without being disagreeable?
Can you hold your position under pressure?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a coworker.
2. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.
3. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a decision.
4. Describe a situation where you and a peer had very different
   approaches.
5. Tell me about a time you had to deliver feedback that was hard
   to hear.

**Rubric row:** *Collaboration, Growth, Judgment.*

**The senior move:** you describe the other person's position
*charitably* and accurately before describing your own. You
describe your *tactic* (not just the outcome). You don't claim
total victory — even if you were right.

---

### Category 3: Failure / Mistake

**What it tests:** can you own a failure honestly? Do you learn
and propagate the learning?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you failed.
2. Tell me about a project that didn't go as planned.
3. Describe a time you made a decision you later regretted.
4. Tell me about a time you missed a deadline.
5. What's a professional accomplishment you're *not* proud of?

**Rubric row:** *Growth, Ownership, Self-awareness.*

**The senior move:** the failure has a *specific* cause (not
"the requirements changed" or "I was busy"), a *specific*
change you made, and a *measurable* follow-up. See the
failure-story analysis in `01_fast_track/design/08_mock_senior_part2.md`.

---

### Category 4: Ambiguity / Incomplete Information

**What it tests:** can you make progress when you don't have
the full picture? Do you freeze or do you act?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete
   information.
2. How do you handle ambiguity?
3. Tell me about a project where the requirements were unclear.
4. Describe a time you had to figure out the problem before you
   could solve it.
5. Tell me about a time you didn't know what success looked like.

**Rubric row:** *Judgment, Ownership, Comfort with uncertainty.*

**The senior move:** you describe the *specific* signal you used
to cut through the ambiguity. "I asked the top 3 consumers what
they wished was different" is a tactic. "I figured it out" is not.

---

### Category 5: Technical Depth / Hard Problem

**What it tests:** can you actually do the work? How do you
reason about hard technical problems?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about the hardest technical problem you've solved in
   the last year.
2. Tell me about a time you had to debug a production issue under
   time pressure.
3. Describe a system you designed from scratch.
4. Tell me about a time you made a technical decision you weren't
   sure was right.
5. Tell me about a project where you had to learn a new technology
   quickly.

**Rubric row:** *Technical depth, Judgment, Learning velocity.*

**The senior move:** you describe the *alternatives you considered*
and rejected, not just the choice you made. You also describe the
*non-obvious* part of the problem — the thing that made it hard,
not the surface complexity.

---

### Category 6: Ownership / Going Beyond

**What it tests:** do you finish what you start? Do you take
on more than your job description?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you went above and beyond.
2. Describe a time you had to take ownership of something that
   wasn't your responsibility.
3. Tell me about the most impactful project you've worked on.
4. Tell me about a time you saw a problem nobody else was
   addressing.
5. Tell me about a time you had to step up when others didn't.

**Rubric row:** *Ownership, Initiative, Scope.*

**The senior move:** the story has a *cost* — you had to give
up something else to do this, and you made that trade
deliberately. "I had a quiet quarter and I picked it up" is E4.
"I was already at capacity and I took this on anyway because it
was the most important thing" is E5.

---

### Category 7: Helping / Developing Others

**What it tests:** can you make the people around you better?

**Sample questions:**
1. Tell me about a time you mentored someone.
2. Describe a time you helped a coworker who was struggling.
3. Tell me about a time you onboarded a new team member.
4. Describe a time you shared knowledge with the broader team.
5. Tell me about a time you gave someone feedback that helped them
   grow.

**Rubric row:** *Collaboration, Growth, Influence.*

**The senior move:** the story has a *specific* thing the other
person learned or did differently afterward. "I helped them
learn the codebase" is E4. "Six months later they were
independently shipping features to that area" is E5.

---

## 3. How to use the taxonomy

Step 1: build a 2-axis matrix.

```
                Rubric rows:  Ownership Judgment Collab Growth Comm
Leadership                   [story]    [story] [story] [...]
Conflict                     [...]      [...]   [...]
Failure                      [...]      [...]   [...]
Ambiguity                    [...]      [...]   [...]
Technical depth              [...]      [...]   [...]
Ownership                    [...]      [...]   [...]
Helping                      [...]      [...]   [...]
```

Step 2: for each cell, write down whether you have a story that
fits. The goal is at least one strong story per row, with overlap
(good stories cover 2-3 cells).

Step 3: for the cells where you have *no* story, write down either
(a) a story you can build, or (b) a different story you can use
to flex into that rubric row.

The interview-prep version of this matrix is the single most
useful artifact you'll produce in this course. Bring it to every
prep session.

---

## Try it

Build the matrix for your own career. Don't worry about having
a perfect story for every cell — just see which cells are empty.

The empty cells are your prep priorities. Spend 30 minutes
brainstorming for each empty cell. The Workshop in
`05_workshops/01_story_mining.md` gives you a structured way to
do this.
