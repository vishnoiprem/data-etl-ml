# 07 — Common Pitfalls Senior Engineers Make

> **Lesson 7 of 7 — Theory** · ~10 min

The 8 specific failure modes at the senior+ level. Most of these
are *only* seen at the senior+ bar — junior candidates make
different mistakes. Read this before any interview.

---

## 1. Why senior+ is different

Junior candidates get downleveled for obvious things: rambling,
no structure, no specific stories, blaming the manager.

Senior+ candidates have already cleared that bar. The
downleveling at senior+ is for *subtler* mistakes: over-hedging,
excessive abstraction, false humility, scope inflation. The
mistakes are smaller but the cost is the same.

---

## 2. The 8 pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Over-hedging

Senior engineers have learned to be careful with claims. They
soften everything: *"I think maybe we kind of did X, possibly
with Y impact, sort of."* This is fine in a design doc. In an
interview, it's a downlevel signal. The interviewer reads it
as "this person doesn't have conviction."

**Fix:** own your claims. *"I led the migration, chose the
dual-write approach, and the result was 14 caught mismatches
and zero production incidents."* If you don't believe it
enough to say it without hedging, you don't have a strong
story.

### Pitfall 2: Excessive abstraction

*"I think the most important thing in engineering is
communication, and what I try to do is build bridges between
disciplines..."* This is philosophy, not an answer. The
interviewer is looking for the specific instance, not the
abstract principle.

**Fix:** the rule of "principle, then instance." Name the
principle in one sentence, then spend 60+ seconds on the
specific story that demonstrates it. Never give the
principle without the instance.

### Pitfall 3: False humility

*"I was just lucky to be on the team. The real credit goes to
my manager and my peers. I didn't really do much."* This sounds
humble but the interviewer reads it as: either you didn't
actually do the work, or you don't have the self-awareness to
recognize what you did. Both are bad.

**Fix:** credit your team *and* own your specific contribution.
*"My team did the heavy lifting on the technical work. My
specific contribution was the schema negotiation with the data
science team, which unblocked the migration."*

### Pitfall 4: Scope inflation

*"I led the company's cloud migration, which touched 1,200
engineers across 8 orgs."* If your title was Senior Engineer
and you owned one of the 50 workstreams, that's scope
inflation. The interviewer will probe and the story will
collapse.

**Fix:** be honest about your scope and make the *impact*
the impressive part, not the *number of people*. "I owned
one of the 50 workstreams, specifically the data layer for
the analytics team. We migrated 8 TB of data with zero
incidents over 3 weekends, and the playbook I wrote
became the template for the next 6 teams."

### Pitfall 5: Vague takeaways

*"I learned a lot about communication and leadership."* This
is a non-takeaway. The interviewer reads it as "this person
didn't actually internalize a specific change."

**Fix:** the takeaway has to be a *specific behavior* you'll
do next time, not a generic value. *"The specific thing I now
do differently: at the start of every cross-team project, I
write a 1-page 'definition of done' doc with my PM counterpart
before I write any code. I haven't missed an estimate since."*

### Pitfall 6: Recency bias

You tell 5 stories, all from your last 6 months. The
interviewer is now wondering: is this person a one-project
wonder, or do they have a sustained track record? The 6
question round is a sample, and you want the sample to span
your whole career, not just your last quarter.

**Fix:** plan your 6 answers to span 2-3 years. If your
strongest stories are all from one role, sprinkle in a
weaker-but-genuine story from an earlier role to show
breadth.

### Pitfall 7: Mistaking activity for impact

*"I worked 80-hour weeks, I was on three projects, I mentored
two interns, I wrote 12 design docs, I ran 30 meetings..."*
This is a busy log, not a story. The interviewer is grading
*outcomes*, not *effort*.

**Fix:** every story should answer "what *changed*?" — not
"what did you *do*?" If your story is dominated by verbs of
action (built, wrote, ran, led) without a corresponding
outcome verb (improved, reduced, increased, enabled), it's
not landing.

### Pitfall 8: Failing the "would I work with this person" test

This isn't a single mistake — it's a vibe. The interviewer
walks out of the room and asks themselves: *"If this person
joined my team tomorrow, would I be happy? Relieved? Neutral?
Concerned?"* Anything below "happy" is a problem.

**Fix:** the practical version of this is to be **pleasant
to talk to**. Smile when appropriate. Don't interrupt.
Listen to the question. Say "that's a great question" if it
is. Don't be defensive. Don't be arrogant. Don't be glib.
Don't make jokes about the company. Don't make jokes about
the previous company. Be the person the interviewer would
want to grab coffee with.

---

## 3. The meta-pattern

Most of these pitfalls share a single underlying cause: **the
candidate is performing, not reflecting.** The interview sounds
rehearsed, generic, hedged, inflated, or glib because the
candidate is trying to *seem* like a senior engineer, not
*being* one in the room.

The fix is the one that's been true since lesson 1 of
Module 01: **specific beats general, honest beats polished,
numbers beat adjectives.** When in doubt, be specific and
honest. The senior signals will follow.

---

## Try it

Read through your 5 strongest stories one more time. For each,
ask: which of the 8 pitfalls is closest to my version of this
story? Pick one. Rewrite the story to avoid that specific
pitfall.

The pitfall you keep returning to is your most common failure
mode. Knowing it by name is half the fix.
