# 06 — Habits of High-Performing Candidates

> **Lesson 6 of 8 — Fast Track** · ~12 min

Eight specific habits that separate "good answer" from "gets the
offer." None of these are about what you say — they're about what
you do in the 30 seconds before, during, and after the answer.

---

## 1. The habit list

After hundreds of mock interviews, the candidates who consistently
get the offer share these eight habits. They are learnable. They
are not charisma.

### Habit 1: Pause before answering

The worst answers start with "So..." and run for 90 seconds.
The best answers start with a 2-second pause while the candidate
thinks, then a clean, structured delivery.

The pause is information. It tells the interviewer: *"I'm taking
this seriously, I'm picking the right story, and I'm about to
give you a structured answer."* A senior engineer who pauses to
think is signaling thoughtfulness. A junior engineer who pauses
is signaling nervousness. Same pause, different signal. **Earn
the right to pause by having prepared.**

### Habit 2: Anchor with one sentence, then drill in

A 90-second answer that starts with *"I'll tell you about a time
I led a migration of our analytics pipeline from batch to
streaming..."* is 10× easier to follow than one that starts with
*"So basically I was working on this thing..."*

**First sentence = the headline.** It tells the interviewer
what the story is about so they can listen for structure instead
of trying to figure it out. *"I'll tell you about a time I led
a migration with a hard deadline and had to negotiate scope
with our partner."* Now the interviewer knows: a project, a
constraint, a negotiation. They can place each subsequent beat.

### Habit 3: Use the question's words back

If the interviewer asked "tell me about a time you **influenced
without authority**," your answer should contain the phrase
"influence without authority" or "without formal authority."
This is **anchoring**. It tells the interviewer you understood
the question, and it makes it easy for them to score you on the
rubric row that asked for that exact thing.

Most candidates answer *adjacent* to the question. Top candidates
answer *on* the question.

### Habit 4: Name the moment of decision

Every good story has a moment where the protagonist (you) made
a call. The senior move is to **highlight that moment** in the
narration: *"That's when I decided to..."* / *"The choice I made
was..."* / *"I called the meeting and proposed..."*

Most candidates drift through the story without highlighting
the decision. The interviewer is left wondering *"so what did
you actually do?"* The fix is one sentence: **explicitly name
the moment you decided.**

### Habit 5: Answer for 90-120 seconds, not 4 minutes

A 4-minute answer is a downlevel signal. It says: *"I don't know
what's important in this story, so I'm going to tell you all of
it."* The senior move is to **edit ruthlessly** — cut the setup,
cut the aftermath, cut the parts that don't earn their place.

The discipline of "90-120 seconds" forces you to choose the
single most important beat and tell only that. If the interviewer
wants more, they'll ask. And the follow-up is where the real
calibration happens anyway.

### Habit 6: End with a beat, not a fade

A weak answer ends: *"...and then we shipped it. Yeah. So. Yeah."*
A strong answer ends with **a sentence the interviewer remembers**.
The options:

- **The takeaway:** *"The lesson I took from this is..."*
- **The compounding:** *"The pattern has since become the team's
  default for..."*
- **The what-I'd-do-differently:** *"What I'd do differently is..."*

Pick one. End on it. Stop talking.

### Habit 7: Ask "is there a follow-up you'd like?" after the answer

After a 2-minute answer, say: *"I'm happy to go deeper on any
specific part — is there a follow-up that would be useful?"* This
is a senior move for three reasons:

1. It signals confidence — you know your story well enough to
   pick a follow-up.
2. It surfaces what the interviewer was actually probing (often
   something you didn't say).
3. It gives the interviewer an easy way to push you on the
   specific rubric row they care about.

### Habit 8: Take notes on the interviewer

Bring a notebook (paper, not laptop). After each answer, jot down
the question. After the interview, you'll have a list of what
you were asked. Over multiple interviews, you'll see the patterns
in your weak areas.

Senior candidates treat every interview as a data-gathering
exercise, even when they're also trying to get the job. Junior
candidates treat every interview as a one-shot performance. The
senior move compounds.

## 2. The interview day routine

The morning of the interview:

- [ ] Re-read your top 5 stories (5 min)
- [ ] Re-read the job description (3 min)
- [ ] Re-read the 3-5 reverse-interview questions you plan to ask
      (3 min)
- [ ] No new prep. Your stories are baked. Trust them.

Right before the round:

- [ ] Bathroom. Water. Phone off.
- [ ] Open the notebook. Re-skim your top 3 stories.
- [ ] 30 seconds of box breathing. Slow exhale.

In the room:

- [ ] Slow down. Speak at 90% of your normal speed.
- [ ] Pause 2 seconds before answering.
- [ ] Answer for 90-120 seconds. Stop. Ask if they want a follow-up.
- [ ] Take notes on what they ask.

After:

- [ ] Within 1 hour, write down: which stories landed, which
  didn't, what questions surprised you. (See Habit 8.)

---

## 3. The compound effect

Habit 8 is the one that makes the other seven reliable. If you
take notes after every interview — even the ones you bomb — you
will, in 4-5 interviews, have a precise picture of your weak
spots. Most candidates discover these in interview #20. The
disciplined candidates discover them in interview #3.

The note format I recommend:

```
Date: 2026-10-09
Company: Acme
Role: Senior Data Engineer
Question: "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority."
Story I told: [the schema migration one]
What worked: specific, had a number
What didn't: I drifted at 3:00, no clear takeaway
Question I didn't expect: "what would your manager say about
  how you handled the conflict?"
Follow-up: ask more about delivery timing
```

Five entries like this and you'll see patterns. The patterns are
where the work is.

---

## Try it

Pick one of the habits above that you *don't* currently do
(pause-before-answer, end-with-a-beat, take-notes-after, etc.)
and commit to practicing it for the next 3 days. After 3 days,
pick another.

By the end of two weeks you'll have internalized 6-7 of them,
which is enough to be visibly different from the average
candidate in the room.
