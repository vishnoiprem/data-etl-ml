# 01 — The 60-Second Self-Introduction

> **Lesson 1 of 5 — Practice** · ~10 min

The single most-rehearsed 60 seconds in the entire interview loop.
If you only have 5 minutes to prepare for a behavioral round,
spend them on this.

---

## 1. Why this is the most important 60 seconds

"Tell me about yourself" comes at the start of *every* interview
round — behavioral, coding, system design, hiring manager. It
sets the tone for the next 45 minutes.

A weak intro:
- Sounds generic
- Doesn't differentiate you
- Wastes the interviewer's attention on the wrong things
- Forces the interviewer to *find* your interesting angles

A strong intro:
- Lands your 3 most distinctive signals in 60 seconds
- Pre-loads the follow-up questions the interviewer will ask
- Sets you up as a senior engineer *from the first sentence*
- Gives the interviewer a frame for everything that follows

---

## 2. The 4-part structure

A senior-engineer 60-second intro has 4 parts:

| Part | Length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The headline** | 10 sec | "I'm a [X] with [Y] years of experience, currently [Z]." |
| **2. The signature work** | 20 sec | 1 specific project/area you're known for. |
| **3. The 3 signals** | 20 sec | 3 specific things you want the interviewer to know about you. |
| **4. The why-here** | 10 sec | Why you're talking to *this* company. |

Total: 60 seconds. Time it.

---

## 3. The signals

The 3 signals are the most important part. They are the *anchors*
the interviewer will use to remember you 5 minutes from now, and
the *probes* they'll reach for in follow-ups.

Good signals are:
- **Specific** (not "good communicator" but "I write design docs
  before any non-trivial work")
- **Distinguishing** (not every senior engineer can claim them)
- **Relevant** (to the role you're interviewing for)
- **Verifiable** (you have a story for each one in your bank)

Examples of strong signals (pick 3):

- "I tend to over-index on stakeholder alignment before I start
  building."
- "I've gotten good at estimating work that has a lot of moving
  parts."
- "I care a lot about observability — I'd rather have a good
  dashboard than a clever algorithm."
- "I have a habit of writing postmortems for things that
  *didn't* go wrong, to capture the near-misses."
- "I do well in ambiguous situations where the right answer has
  to be invented."
- "I tend to push back on big-bang launches in favor of staged
  rollouts."

Examples of weak signals (avoid):
- "I'm a hard worker" (everyone says this)
- "I'm a team player" (meaningless)
- "I'm passionate about technology" (no specific)
- "I'm a fast learner" (no evidence)

---

## 4. Worked example: Senior Data Engineer

> *"I'm a data engineer with 6 years of experience, currently
> at [Company] where I lead the data platform team.*
>
> *The work I'm proudest of is the streaming migration I led
> last year — we moved our analytics pipeline from nightly
> batches to sub-minute streaming, which changed how the whole
> org makes decisions.*
>
> *Three things I'd want you to know about me. First, I
> over-index on stakeholder alignment — before any non-trivial
> project, I write a 1-page doc and circulate it for review.
> Second, I've gotten pretty good at debugging production
> systems under time pressure — the production incident last
> quarter that lasted 47 minutes, I was the lead, and we cut
> the next one to 6 minutes by changing our detection
> strategy. Third, I care a lot about observability — I'd
> rather have a great dashboard than a clever algorithm.*
>
> *I'm talking to [Target Company] because your data platform
> is doing exactly the kind of work I want to do at larger
> scale, and the team seems to value the same things I do —
> the way you wrote about observability in your engineering
> blog resonated with me."*

**Why this works:**
- Headline (10 sec): clear, anchored, with a number
- Signature work (20 sec): specific, with an outcome
- 3 signals (20 sec): each is a specific behavior, each is
  provable with a story
- Why-here (10 sec): specific to the company, references their
  public content, signals you've done your homework

## 5. The variations

The 60-second intro has 3 common variations you should also
have ready:

### Variation 1: The career-changer intro

If you're switching specialties (e.g. backend to data, or
IC to EM), the structure changes:

> *"[X] years as a [current role]. The work I'm proudest of
> is [specific project]. The thing I realized is that I
> want to spend more of my time on [new area], which is why
> I'm talking to you about [target role]."*

### Variation 2: The senior-to-staff intro

At Staff+, the signature work should be *org-level*, not
team-level:

> *"...the work I'm proudest of is the cross-org platform
> initiative I led, which touched 4 teams and reduced
> infrastructure cost by $400k/year."*

### Variation 3: The returning-to-work intro

If you've been on a break (parental leave, sabbatical, etc.),
acknowledge it briefly and move on:

> *"...I spent the last [N] months on parental leave, which
> was great. Before that, I was at [Company] where I [work]."*

---

## 6. The don'ts

A few things to *never* do in the 60-second intro:

- **Don't recite your resume.** The interviewer has it. They
  want the *story* of you, not the timeline.
- **Don't start with personal info** ("I grew up in Ohio...").
  Personal context is fine in social settings, not in
  interviews.
- **Don't apologize** ("I know I don't have the most impressive
  background, but..."). The interviewer has decided you're
  credible enough to interview. Own it.
- **Don't go over 90 seconds.** If you can't say it in 90
  seconds, you don't know what your story is yet.
- **Don't end on the why-here** unless you can make it specific.
  Generic "your company seems great" endings are wasted air
  time.

---

## 7. The practice loop

The 60-second intro is a *muscle memory* skill. Here's the
practice loop:

1. Write out your intro (use the template above).
2. Time it. Adjust until it's 55-65 seconds.
3. Record yourself saying it. Listen back.
4. Note 2 things to fix.
5. Rewrite.
6. Repeat 5 times over 3 days.
7. By day 3, you can deliver it without thinking.

The senior version of the intro has 3-4 variants (general,
career-change, staff+, returning). Have all of them ready.

---

## Try it

Write your 60-second intro using the 4-part structure. Time
it. Record yourself. Listen back. Notice:
- Did you use any of the "weak signals" from Section 3?
- Did the "why here" feel specific or generic?
- Did the whole thing feel like *you*, or did it feel
  performed?

If it feels performed, rewrite until it doesn't. Authenticity
is a senior signal.
