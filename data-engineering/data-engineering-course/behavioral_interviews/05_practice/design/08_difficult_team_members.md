## Why this lesson

This is the **highest-leverage trap question** in the 40-question
behavioral bank. Interviewers ask it not because they want your
roast of difficult colleagues — they ask it because the answer
reveals whether you can:

1. Self-reflect (the people you find difficult are usually the ones
   who *expose your own gaps*).
2. Take responsibility (every "difficult person" story has a moment
   where *you* made it harder).
3. Differentiate between a *type of person* and a *type of situation*.

The wrong answer — "people who don't deliver" / "people who talk too
much in meetings" / "people who won't commit" — is the same wrong
answer 80% of candidates give. It's wrong because:

- It **dehumanizes** the colleague.
- It **frames the problem as theirs**, not the situation's.
- It **fails the reversibility test** — if you can't imagine a
  different colleague making the same dynamic work, the problem is
  in you, not them.

## The framework — name a *type of situation*, not a type of person

| Step | What you say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Reframe | "I find *situations* difficult, not types. Specifically: I find it hard when expectations are unspoken." | Reframes the question. |
| 2. Own it | "And looking back, the times I labeled someone difficult, the common thread was that *I* had been vague about what I needed." | Ownership. The most senior-grade sentence. |
| 3. Anchor in a story | Tell a 2-3 minute story where you misread a teammate, recovered, and what you changed. | The artifact. |

## Worked example — ride-share team

> **What I used to call a difficult person.** I had a teammate, R,
> who would push back hard in design reviews. The other engineers
> found her "negative." I included her in every review anyway.
>
> **What I missed.** The pattern was me. I had been running reviews
> where I presented a finished design and asked for *ratification*,
> not for input. R was the only one who would not ratify. She forced
> the room to confront gaps. The other engineers agreed with her in
> the chat afterwards.
>
> **The change.** I changed three things. (1) I moved the design
> review to *before* I had committed to a direction. (2) I named the
> decision I was trying to make before the meeting. (3) I followed
> up privately with R after every meeting. None of those changes
> were about R. They were about the *situation* I had created.
>
> **What I learned.** The "difficult" people in your career are
> usually the most precise mirror you have. If you find a pattern,
> the pattern is probably in *you*, not them.

## Google — Interview101 2026 — the Googleyness signal

The "Googleyness & Leadership" pillar called **collaboration**
includes this:

> "Candidates who visibly recalibrate and engage the new constraint
> constructively score higher than those who defend their original
> answer."

This question is *exactly* that test. The wrong answer defends a
worldview ("I find lazy people difficult"). The right answer
recalibrates ("I found *myself* difficult in this situation, and
here is what I changed").

## Meta — Aced 2026 — Core Values

The relevant value is **"Be Open"**:

> "Openness to different perspectives, willingness to change your
> mind based on new information."

The instinct-question cousin. The wrong answer implicitly says "I
am right and they are wrong." The right answer says "the dynamic
had two authors."

## The 4 failure modes

1. **Naming a type of person** — "people who don't deliver," "people
   who talk too much," "people who are too quiet." All wrong.
2. **Implicit blame** — "I had a coworker who…" centers the
   colleague. Center the situation.
3. **No self-reflection** — every answer needs a "looking back, the
   pattern was me" beat.
4. **Anecdote-only** — the story must end with what you *changed*,
   not what they did.

## The 30-second version (for Meta — stories capped at 5 min total)

"The situations I find difficult are where expectations are unspoken.
The person I found most difficult turned out to be the most precise
mirror I had — I had been running design reviews as ratification,
not input. I changed the meeting structure, the dynamic flipped, and
R is now the person I most want in the room."

## Try it — your turn

Write a 30-second answer and a 2-minute answer to the same
question. The 30-second version is for the warm-up round; the
2-minute version is for the deep-dive. Compare the two — most
candidates discover their "30 seconds" reveals what they actually
believe, while the 2-minute version is what they wish they believed.
