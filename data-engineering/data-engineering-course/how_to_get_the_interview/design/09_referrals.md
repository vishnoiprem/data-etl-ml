# 09 — Referrals

> **Lesson 9 of 9** · ~15 min

The 3 types of referrals, the 30-60-90 day rule for asking
favors, 3 sample messages, and why a bad referral is worse
than no referral.

---

## 1. The 3 types of referrals

Not all referrals are equal. The data on conversion rate:

| Type | Source | Conversion to screen | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peer referral** | Ex-coworker at the same level as you, now at the target company | 30-50% | Their judgment about you is the most credible signal. "I worked with this person for 2 years, they're great" is high-trust. |
| **Internal referral** | Senior+ employee (manager, staff+, principal) at the target company, ideally in the data org | 50-70% | Their reach inside the company is bigger. Their recommendation is harder to get, so it's more valuable. |
| **Alumni referral** | Someone who went to your school, your bootcamp, your meetup group — now at the target company | 20-40% | Lower than peer because the alumni relationship is weaker than a working relationship. But it's a credible signal. |

The hierarchy is clear: **internal > peer > alumni**. A senior+
internal referral at the target company is the gold standard.
A peer referral is the most common achievable version. An
alumni referral is the entry-level version, and is the one
most candidates should start with.

---

## 2. The 30-60-90 day rule

The single most common referral mistake: **asking a stranger
for a favor the first time you message them.** It doesn't work.
It feels transactional. It gets ignored.

The 30-60-90 day rule:

- **0-30 days after first contact:** no asks. Just talk. Reply
  to their posts. Engage with their content. Send 1-2
  substantive messages (not "hi," not "I'd love to pick your
  brain" — actual *substance*).
- **30-60 days:** light asks. "What was your interview loop
  like at [Company]?" "Who would you recommend I talk to
  about the data platform team?" These are asks for *information*,
  not for action.
- **60-90 days:** the referral ask. By now you have a
  relationship. The ask is reasonable. The person knows
  enough about you to say yes (or to say no honestly).

Most candidates try to compress this into 1 message. They get
ignored. The 30-60-90 day rule is what works.

**Exception:** the 30-60-90 day rule is for *cold* relationships
— people you just met. For people you already have a
relationship with (ex-coworkers, college friends, ex-managers),
the rule is "ask when you actually need the referral, and be
specific about why them." There's no warm-up period for people
who already know you.

## 3. The 3 sample referral messages

### Message 1: LinkedIn DM to a senior+ employee you don't know

```
Hi [Name] — I came across your post on [TOPIC] and your
take on [SPECIFIC POINT] is the clearest version of that
argument I've read. I work on similar problems at
[YOUR COMPANY] — built a [YOUR WORK] — and would love to
compare notes.

No agenda, just a fellow data engineer who finds your
writing useful. If you're open to it, I'd love a 15-min
chat. If not, no worries at all — keep the posts coming.

[Jordan]
```

**Why it works:**

- **Specific reference** to their actual content (signals
  research)
- **No "I'd love to pick your brain"** (the worst possible
  opener)
- **A genuine offer of reciprocity** ("compare notes")
- **A small, bounded ask** (15 min, not "a coffee")
- **A graceful no** ("If not, no worries at all")

This is the *first* message in a 30-60-90 day relationship.
The referral ask comes later, if the relationship develops.

### Message 2: Email to a friend-of-friend for a referral

```
Subject: Intro: [Friend's Name] suggested I reach out

Hi [Name] — [MUTUAL FRIEND] mentioned you work on the data
platform team at [COMPANY] and thought we should connect.
I'm a senior data engineer with 5 years on Python + Kafka
+ AWS, currently at [YOUR COMPANY], and I'm exploring my
next role.

[COMPANY] is at the top of my list — your team's work on
[SPECIFIC THING] is exactly the kind of problem I want to
spend the next few years on. I'm planning to apply to
[ROLE TITLE] (linked below). If you'd be open to a
referral, I'd really appreciate it; if not, I'm grateful
for the connection regardless.

[LINK TO JD]
[LINK TO RESUME]

[Jordan]
```

**Why it works:**

- **Names the mutual friend** (the introduction is socially
  validated)
- **Names the specific role** (the referrer knows exactly
  what you're asking for)
- **Includes the resume and the JD** (the referrer doesn't
  have to ask for either)
- **A graceful no** ("if not, I'm grateful regardless")
- **Bounded ask** (one specific role, not "anything you
  have open")

### Message 3: Slack DM to a college alumni at the target company

```
hey [Name] — random one — saw you're at [COMPANY] now,
congrats! I'm a data engineer at [YOUR COMPANY], also ex-[SCHOOL],
also ex-[CLUB/MAJOR if relevant], and I'm applying to the
[ROLE TITLE] role on your team (or a similar one).

If you have 5 min to chat about the team / the role / the
interview loop, I'd be grateful. If you can refer me
internally, even better — but no pressure either way.

[Jordan]
```

**Why it works:**

- **Alumni framing** (shared school, shared context, lower
  social cost to reply)
- **Casual tone matching the medium** (Slack DMs are
  different from LinkedIn DMs)
- **Bounded ask** (5 min, not "an exploratory call")
- **Two-step ask** (chat first, then referral) — gives the
  alumni an out if they can't refer but can still help

## 4. Why a bad referral is worse than no referral

The unspoken rule of referrals: **your reputation is now
attached to the referrer's**. If they refer you and you bomb
the interview, the referrer looks bad. This is a real cost to
them, and most senior+ employees won't pay it for someone
they don't know well.

What "bad referral" looks like in practice:

- A peer refers you. You get a screen. You don't prepare. You
  ramble. You ask about salary in the first 5 minutes. The
  recruiter tells the peer.
- A senior+ employee refers you. The hiring manager pulls
  your resume. It doesn't match the JD. The senior+ employee
  gets a "why did you refer this person" note.

Both of these are *worse* than not getting referred at all,
because:

- The peer who referred you now has a slightly tarnished
  reputation, and they're less likely to refer anyone again
  (including you, in the future).
- The senior+ employee who referred you is now associated
  with a bad candidate, which can affect their own standing
  in the company.

**The rule:** don't ask for a referral unless:

- You have a *real* relationship with the referrer (not a
  5-minute LinkedIn exchange).
- Your resume is *ready* for the role (targeted, not generic).
- You are *committed* to interviewing well if you get the
  screen.

If any of those is missing, wait. The cost of a bad referral
is higher than the cost of a slower search.

## 5. The "ask" — the actual message that gets the referral

Once you have a relationship (or once you're reaching out to
a friend-of-friend for the specific purpose of a referral),
the ask itself is straightforward. The structure:

1. **Re-establish the relationship** (1 sentence)
2. **Name the specific role** (1 sentence)
3. **Make the ask** (1 sentence)
4. **Provide the assets** (1-2 sentences — resume + JD link)
5. **A graceful out** (1 sentence)

**Sample ask (the actual referral request):**

> *Hey [Name] — thanks again for the chat last week about
> [TOPIC]. It was really useful.*
>
> *I'm planning to apply to the [ROLE TITLE] role on your
> team at [COMPANY] (linked below). The work you described
> — [SPECIFIC THING] — is exactly what I want to do next.*
>
> *Would you be open to referring me internally? If so,
> here's my resume and the JD. If not, no worries at all —
> I appreciate your time regardless.*
>
> *[JD LINK]*
> *[RESUME LINK]*

The ask is direct, specific, and includes the assets. The
referrer doesn't have to do work to find the role or your
resume. They just have to click a button in their internal
ATS.

**What if they say no?** Be graceful. Most "no"s are "I'd
rather not put my name on this one" — not personal, just
risk-averse. The right response is: *"Totally understand,
no problem. Thanks for the chat, I'll keep you posted."* Don't
push, don't guilt, don't argue.

## 6. Worked example: the 30-60-90 day plan for one target company

**Target:** Senior DE role at Stripe. You have no current
connection to anyone at Stripe.

**Day 1-30: Build the relationship.**

- Identify 3-5 senior+ data engineers / EMs at Stripe on
  LinkedIn.
- Engage with their posts (substantive comments, not "Great
  post!").
- Send 1-2 connection requests per week, with a short
  personalized note referencing their work.
- Goal: 2-3 connections accepted, 1-2 substantive DMs exchanged.

**Day 31-60: Light asks.**

- Send 1-2 messages asking for *information*: "What's the
  interview loop like for senior DEs on the data platform
  team?" "Who else on the team would you recommend I talk to?"
- Share a relevant article or your own work if it's
  appropriate.
- Goal: 1 real conversation, 1 additional intro.

**Day 61-90: The ask.**

- After 1-2 substantive conversations with the same person,
  make the referral ask. (See Section 5.)
- If they say no, you have a relationship for *future*
  applications, or for an informational interview that
  becomes a warm intro to someone else.
- Goal: 1 referral submitted, 1 application with referral
  in flight.

The 90-day plan is for *one* target company. You can run
2-3 of these in parallel if you have the bandwidth, but more
than that and the per-company quality drops. Better to do 2-3
targeted 90-day plans than 10 half-hearted ones.

## 7. The "no referral available" case

If you have no peer connections, no alumni connections, and
no current or former coworkers at the target company, the
30-60-90 day plan is your only path. The question is whether
to invest the 90 days.

The honest answer: **it depends on whether the role is worth
it**. For a top-3 target company, yes. For a generic "good
company," use the cold DM + apply path from Lesson 08 instead.
The 90-day plan is high-leverage for dream roles and a waste
of time for backup options.

**Alternative paths when you have no network:**

- **Cold DM the hiring manager** directly (Lesson 08, Section 3).
  This works without any prior relationship.
- **Engage publicly with the team's content** (Twitter,
  LinkedIn, conference talks). This builds familiarity
  without a 1:1 relationship.
- **Apply through the company's career page** with a strong
  targeted resume + cover letter (Lessons 04, 06). The
  conversion rate is lower than a referral, but it's
  non-zero.

Most senior+ candidates *do* have a network — they just don't
realize it. The exercise below helps you find yours.

---

## Try it

1. **List every company you've worked at** in the last 7 years.
   For each, list 3-5 former coworkers you could message today.
2. **For each former coworker, note:** (a) where they work now,
   (b) what role they're in, (c) when you last spoke.
3. **Pick your top 3 target companies.** For each, list any
   1st-degree connections you have.
4. **Pick 1 person** from steps 1-3. Send them a message
   this week — either a re-engagement message (for old
   connections) or the cold-DM template (for new ones).

The first message is the hardest. After that, the cadence is
self-sustaining.
