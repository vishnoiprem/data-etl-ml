# 10 — The Recruiter Screen: How to Get Past the Gate

> **Lesson 10 of 9** · ~15 min

The 15-30 minute call that decides whether you get to the
loop. It is not technical. It is not a "real" interview.
It is a filtering call — and most strong engineers
under-prepare for it because they assume their technical
record will speak for itself.

It won't. The recruiter screen is a **5-question
gating conversation**, and the candidates who pass it
cleanly are the candidates who practiced the 5 questions
out loud.

This lesson teaches the questions, the bad answers, the
good answers, and the comp-negotiation scripts.

---

## 1. What the recruiter screen is (and isn't)

A 15-30 minute phone or video call with an in-house
recruiter or sourcer. It is **not** a technical screen.
There is no SQL, no system design, no STAR story
expected. The recruiter is rarely the hiring manager,
and even when they are, this call is not the place to
prove depth.

It **is** a filtering call. The recruiter is assessing:

- **Communication** — can you speak clearly and concisely?
- **Motivation** — do you actually want this role, or are
  you spamming applications?
- **Comp expectations** — are you in the band, or are you
  going to waste everyone's time at the offer stage?
- **Logistics** — work authorization, location, start
  date, willingness to relocate.
- **Signal** — does your background match the role, or
  are you a mismatch that needs to be filtered out?

A "yes" on all 5 → the recruiter schedules you for the
technical phone screen. A "no" or "uncertain" on any →
you're done, often with a polite email two weeks later.

> **The recruiter's job is to filter, not to advocate for
> you.** Treat them as a respected gatekeeper, not a
> friend. Be warm, be specific, be honest — but don't
> vent, don't overshare, and don't badmouth your current
> employer. The recruiter is mentally checking boxes
> against a rubric, and the rubric is unforgiving.

---

## 2. The 5 questions every recruiter asks

These 5 questions account for ~80% of the call. The
remaining 20% is logistics (start date, location, work
auth). If you can answer all 5 cleanly in under 90
seconds each, you will pass almost any recruiter screen.

1. **"Walk me through your background."**
2. **"Why are you looking?"** (the trap)
3. **"Why this company / this role?"** (the homework test)
4. **"What are your compensation expectations?"**
5. **"Are you interviewing elsewhere? Any offers?"**

Below: a bad answer and a good answer for each, with the
reasoning.

---

## 3. Question 1: "Walk me through your background"

**What they're testing:** 90 seconds. Can you summarize
your career cleanly, with the relevant 2-3 roles front
and center?

**Bad answer (rambling, 4 minutes):**

> "So I started at Infosys in 2018, did some Java work,
> then moved to a startup in 2019 where I did a bunch of
> stuff — Python, some AWS, I think we used Airflow
> maybe? Then I went to a bigger company and did more
> data stuff, and now I'm at my current place doing —
> actually, let me back up, I forgot to mention I did
> a 6-month internship in 2017..."

**What's wrong:** too long, no numbers, no narrative
arc, the recruiter has to do the work of identifying
what's relevant.

**Good answer (75 seconds):**

> "Sure. Six years of data engineering. I started at
> [BigCo] on a generic data team, then moved to
> [MidCo] where I owned the streaming pipeline for the
> fraud team — Kafka, Flink, Snowflake, 4B events/day.
> I spent the last 2.5 years at [CurrentCo] as a
> senior DE on the personalization platform — built
> the feature store from scratch, 200+ features, used
> by 4 ML models. I'm targeting senior or staff
> platform roles, which is why I'm excited about
> this role."

**Why it works:** structured (early career → growth →
current), names the relevant companies and skills,
front-loads the technical depth, ends with the
positioning. The recruiter can copy-paste the
highlights into the hiring-manager brief.

> **The formula:** years of experience → first relevant
> role → second relevant role → current role + 1-2
> specific skills → why this role.

---

## 4. Question 2: "Why are you looking?" (the trap)

**What they're testing:** honesty, professionalism, and
whether you're a flight risk. The trap is that the
*true* answer ("my manager is a nightmare," "I was
laid off," "I hate the tech stack") will get you
filtered.

**Bad answer (overshare):**

> "Honestly, my current manager micromanages
> everything, and the team is in chaos after the
> reorg. I haven't gotten a raise in 18 months
> and the project I'm on was deprioritized last
> quarter. I just need to get out."

**What's wrong:** every red flag in the book. The
recruiter will mentally note "might be a flight risk,"
"might badmouth us in 6 months," and "salary-driven."

**Bad answer (the opposite, vague):**

> "Just looking for new opportunities. Open to
> anything interesting."

**What's wrong:** no signal, no motivation, the
recruiter can't tell if you've thought about your
next move or you're just bored.

**Good answer (60 seconds):**

> "Two reasons. First, the platform I've been
> building is mature — the team is in maintenance
> mode, and I miss the greenfield work. Second,
> I want to move into a role with more
> cross-functional influence — partner with ML
> and product on data strategy, not just deliver
> pipelines. This role is a strong fit on both
> counts because [specific reason 1] and
> [specific reason 2]."

**Why it works:** framed as **pull** (what you want),
not **push** (what you're running from). Names 1-2
specific reasons. Ties back to the role. The recruiter
can write "motivated, specific, low flight risk" in
their notes.

> **Rule of thumb:** if the real reason is compensation,
> say it as "comp is part of it, but not the primary
> driver — the work is." If the real reason is a bad
> manager, **never** mention the manager by name. Frame
> it as "I want more influence over technical direction"
> or "I want to work on problems at a larger scale."

## 5. Question 3: "Why this company / this role?"

**What they're testing:** did you do the homework?
A recruiter can tell in 10 seconds if you've read
the job description. They can tell in 30 seconds
if you've done any external research.

**Bad answer (generic):**

> "Your company is a leader in the space and I've
> always wanted to work somewhere with strong
> engineering culture. The role looks like a great
> fit for my background."

**What's wrong:** could be sent to any company. Zero
specifics. The recruiter has heard this 50 times
today.

**Good answer (75 seconds):**

> "Three things. First, the JD mentions migrating
> from batch to streaming CDC — I led exactly that
> migration at [CurrentCo], 4B events/day, 40-second
> p95 freshness, and I want to do it again at
> larger scale. Second, I saw the engineering blog
> post on your schema-evolution framework from
> last quarter, and the design has some real
> parallels to what I built — I'd love to dig into
> that. Third, the team's scope — owning the entire
> data platform for [product line] — is exactly
> the level of ownership I'm targeting at this
> point in my career."

**Why it works:** specific (names 1-2 things from
the JD, 1-2 from external research), ties back to
the candidate's own work, ends with why the role
is a **match**, not just why the company is
**good**.

> **The prep you should do before every recruiter
> call:** read the JD twice, read the company's
> engineering blog, read the hiring manager's
> LinkedIn (last 3 months of posts), check
> Glassdoor for 2-3 recent reviews. 30 minutes of
> research is the difference between a
> passable and a strong answer.

## 6. Question 4: "What are your compensation expectations?"

**What they're testing:** are you in the band?
Recruiters get paid to filter out candidates who
will be impossible to close at the offer stage.
This is the highest-stakes 60 seconds of the call.

**The two schools of comp conversation:**

**School A: Give a range.** Useful if you know
the market rate for the role and you're confident
your number is in the band. The risk: if your
number is above the band's top, the recruiter
will politely end the call.

**School B: Deflect to "what's the range for
this role?"** Useful if you're unsure, or if
the recruiter contacted you first (in which case
they should be the first to name a number).
The risk: some recruiters interpret a full
deflection as a red flag.

**The hybrid (recommended):**

> "I'm flexible on comp — it depends on the
> overall package, the scope of the role, and
> the team. To make sure we're in the same
> ballpark: I'm targeting the senior band for
> this role and level, which based on my
> research is in the [$X–$Y] range for base.
> Is that aligned with what you have budgeted
> for this role?"

This does 3 things: signals you've done research,
gives a range (so the recruiter can check the
band), and **flips** the question back so they
have to engage.

**When they push back ("we need a number to
move forward"):**

> "I understand. I'd rather not anchor on a
> number until I understand the full scope of
> the role and the leveling — but if I had to
> give a range today, it would be $X to $Y
> total comp, with the understanding that we
> can revisit after the loop."

**What you should never do:**

- Never give a single number. Always a range.
- Never name your current salary (in the US,
  this is increasingly illegal for them to ask,
  but they may try).
- Never say "I'll take anything" — you'll be
  lowballed at the offer.
- Never name a number above the band you
  suspect they're at without signaling
  flexibility.

> **The prep you should do before every
> recruiter call:** know the market rate for
> the role, level, and location. Use
> levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and 2-3 conversations
> with peers. Have a range, not a number.
> Have a "walk-away" number in your head that
> you do not share with the recruiter.

## 7. Question 5: "Are you interviewing elsewhere? Any offers?"

**What they're testing:** leverage, timeline, and
seriousness. Recruiters use this to prioritize
their pipeline. A candidate with 2 active loops
and no offers gets fast-tracked. A candidate
with no other loops gets deprioritized.

**Bad answer (lying):**

> "No, this is the only place I'm interviewing."

**What's wrong:** the recruiter will find out
(eventually) and lose trust. Also, this signals
you have no other options, which weakens your
hand.

**Bad answer (overshare):**

> "Yeah, I have 4 loops in progress, 2 final
> rounds next week, and one offer at $300k
> base. I'm probably going to take that one
> unless you can beat it."

**What's wrong:** reads as a negotiation play
during a screen call. The recruiter will feel
pressured and the call will end awkwardly.

**Good answer (60 seconds):**

> "Yes — I'm in early stages with 2-3 other
> companies in the data platform space. None
> of them are at the offer stage yet. I'm
> being thoughtful about which one I want to
> prioritize for the loop, and this role is
> near the top of my list because [reason]."

**Why it works:** honest (you do have other
loops), specific (2-3 is a believable number),
signals seriousness, and ends with why this
role is a top choice — which is the **flattery
the recruiter wants to hear**, because it makes
their job easier.

> **The subtext the recruiter hears:** "This
> candidate is in demand but still interested
> in us." That is the optimal signal. Anything
> stronger (lots of competing offers) reads as
> flight risk. Anything weaker (no other loops)
> reads as low signal.

## 8. The signals recruiters send

Decoding what the recruiter says at the end of
the call:

| What they say | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| **"Let's get you scheduled for the technical phone screen — when's your availability this week and next?"** | Strong yes. Calendar invite within 24 hours. |
| **"I'll follow up next week with next steps."** | Mild yes, but they need to check with the HM. No calendar invite means no urgency. |
| **"We're interviewing a few other candidates. I'll be in touch."** | Soft no. They're keeping you as a backup. Don't wait. |
| **"It doesn't sound like the role is the right fit for what you're looking for."** | Hard no. Polite, but a no. |
| **"Can you send me your latest resume and a writing sample?"** | Sometimes a real ask, sometimes a stalling tactic. Send within 24 hours either way. |
| **"Let's circle back in 6 months when we have more headcount."** | No, but they're being nice. Move on. |
| **No response after the call** | Either ghosted or deprioritized. Follow up once after 5 business days, then move on. |

## 9. Comp negotiation cheat sheet

The 4 sentences that handle 80% of comp
negotiation at the screen stage:

**When you don't want to give a number:**

> "I'm flexible on comp. What's the range you've
> budgeted for this role and level?"

**When they push:**

> "I'd love to see the range before I anchor. I
> can share my expectations once I know we're in
> the same ballpark."

**When you want to give a number:**

> "Based on my research and experience, I'm
> targeting $X to $Y for base, with the
> understanding that total comp including equity
> and bonus is what I'm optimizing for."

**When the range they give is below your floor:**

> "Thanks for the range. That's a bit below what
> I'd be targeting for this level — can you tell
> me more about the leveling and the growth
> path? I'd rather understand the full picture
> than anchor on a number now."

## 10. Logistics questions you'll get

These are 30-second answers, but have them ready:

- **"What's your work authorization status?"** —
  Have your visa status (if any) ready in one
  sentence. Don't oversell or undersell.
- **"Are you open to relocation?"** — Yes/no,
  with one sentence of context.
- **"What's your earliest start date?"** — Have
  a real answer. "Immediately" if you're not
  employed. "Two weeks after I give notice, so
  roughly [date]" if you are.
- **"Are you willing to come onsite X days a
  week?"** — Yes/no, honest. Don't lie and
  try to renegotiate later.
- **"Do you have any other constraints we should
  know about?"** — This is where you flag
  things like "I can't start before [date]" or
  "I need visa sponsorship." Don't hide
  constraints; they'll come out in the loop.

## 11. Common failure modes

**The 4-minute background.** Rambling past 90
seconds on the first question. The recruiter
mentally checks out by minute 2.

**Badmouthing the current employer.** Even
mild. "My manager is okay but the company
has lost its way" is a yellow flag. Say
nothing negative, or frame everything as
"looking for" rather than "running from."

**Asking about comp before the company has
made its case.** "What's the salary?" as
question 1 is a deal-breaker. Get the
recruiter to want you first; the comp
conversation is easier on the other side.

**Lying about other offers.** Recruiters
talk. If you say you have 3 other loops and
the next recruiter hears you said 0, you
lose credibility across the market.

**Being unprepared on the company.** A
recruiter can tell in 5 seconds if you read
the JD. They will mentally downgrade you
to "low motivation."

**Over-asking questions.** "What's the
on-call rotation? What's the WLB? What's
the attrition rate? What's the comp? What's
the leveling philosophy?" — five questions
in a row signals anxiety, not curiosity.
Ask 1-2 genuine questions and end the
call.

## Try it

Pick a real role you're targeting. Set a
30-minute timer.

**Step 1 (10 min):** Write out your answer to
each of the 5 questions, timed at 90 seconds
each. Read them out loud, alone, with a timer.

**Step 2 (10 min):** Have a friend play the
recruiter. Run the full screen. They should
interrupt you if you go over 90 seconds on
any answer. Record the call.

**Step 3 (10 min):** Listen back. Write down
3 things: (a) where you rambled, (b) where
you got a question you didn't have a good
answer for, (c) where your comp answer was
unclear.

Do this for **2 different roles** in the
same week, and you will pass any recruiter
screen.
