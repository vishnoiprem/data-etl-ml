# 07 — How to Transition Internally

> **Lesson 7 of 9** · ~12 min

The internal mobility playbook: IC → senior, IC → staff, IC →
ML platform, IC → manager. The FAANG programs, the manager
conversation script, and the 3 things that get a transfer
approved.

---

## 1. Why internal transitions are the highest-leverage move

If you already work at a company with a data team, an internal
transfer is **3-5x easier to land than an external hire at the
same level**. The reasons:

- **No ATS.** Your resume is already in the system. The hiring
  manager can pull it up, and your internal profile already has
  your YoE, your current team, and your manager's name.
- **The hiring manager already trusts your context.** They've
  heard of your team. They might know your manager. They have
  insider information about your work.
- **You can interview in a less adversarial setting.** Internal
  loops are typically 2-3 rounds (not 5-6), the bar-raiser is
  usually more lenient, and the questions lean toward "could
  you do this job" rather than "prove you belong here."
- **The compensation is already calibrated.** No "we can only
  offer you X" anchor — your current comp is the starting point.

The downside: **your reputation precedes you**, in both directions.
If you're known as the person who doesn't ship, the internal
interview will surface that. If you're known as a senior+ IC
with a track record, the same. The internal loop is a *more
honest* signal than the external one.

---

## 2. The 4 transition paths

| Path | Difficulty | What makes it work |
|---|---|---|
| **IC → Senior IC** | Low | Same team, different scope. A promotion pitch, not a transfer. |
| **IC → Staff IC** | Medium | Cross-team scope, technical leadership, often an org-level impact story |
| **IC → Manager** | Medium-High | Different role type; requires evidence of people-leadership, not just tech leadership |
| **IC → ML / Analytics / Platform** | Medium | Same title family, different domain. Leverages your existing infra skills. |

The mechanics differ for each, but the *first conversation* —
with your current manager — is the same in all 4 cases. Get that
right and the rest is execution.

---

## 3. The internal mobility programs (FAANG)

Most large companies have a formal internal mobility program.
The four you should know:

| Company | Program | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Meta** | **Move** | Open to all employees after 12 months in current role. Apply to internal reqs, interview, get matched. Manager approval needed for transfer. |
| **Google** | **Transfer** | Apply to internal reqs via the internal job board. Interviews, calibration, leveling. Hiring manager needs your current manager to "release" you. |
| **Amazon** | **Jobfinder** | Internal job board. Apply, interview, get an offer. Manager can block for "business need" but most don't. |
| **Microsoft** | **Internal Marketplace** | Internal reqs only. Manager approval needed; usually given for strong performers. |
| **Netflix** | No formal program | Senior+ only. Hiring manager needs to vet you directly. Manager has little blocking power. |

The general pattern: **you apply to internal reqs, interview,
and your current manager has to release you**. The release is
the hard part. Most managers will release you if (a) you've
been at the company 18+ months, (b) you're a strong performer,
and (c) you've given them time to backfill. If any of those
three is missing, the release is harder.

## 4. The manager conversation script

The single most important conversation in an internal
transition is the one with your *current* manager. Get it right
and the rest of the process is execution. Get it wrong and
your manager will block, delay, or quietly sabotage the move.

**The wrong way (most candidates do this):**

> *"Hey, I'm thinking about exploring other opportunities on
> other teams. Not because of anything here, I just want to
> grow in a different direction. What do you think?"*

The problem: vague, no specific team, no specific role,
signals you're about to leave. The manager has no reason to
help you and every reason to worry.

**The right way (the script that works):**

> *"I've been thinking about my next step, and I want to talk
> to you about it before I do anything. Specifically, I want
> to explore [TEAM X] for [REASON]. Here's why, here's what
> I'd want to do for the team, and here's what I'd want to do
> to make sure my current work here is wrapped up before any
> transition.*
>
> *Some context: I've been here [N] years, my last promo was
> [TIME] ago, and I think [TEAM X] is the right place for me
> to [GROWTH GOAL]. I want to be transparent with you about
> this because I'd rather have you as an advocate than have
> you hear it from someone else.*
>
> *What I want from you: an honest read on whether you think
> this is a good move, and if so, what you'd want from me
> in terms of timing and transition plan."*

The right way has 4 parts:

1. **Specific team, specific role.** Not "I want to explore
   options" — "I want to explore the Lakehouse Platform team."
2. **A reason that doesn't indict the current team.** "Growth
   opportunity," "different problem space," "the team's
   roadmap aligns with what I want to do next." Never
   "I'm bored here" or "I want to leave this team."
3. **Acknowledgment of the current work and the transition
   plan.** "I want to wrap up X, hand off Y, and document Z
   before any move." This is what managers actually care about.
4. **An ask for the manager's input.** "What do you think?"
   turns it from a unilateral decision into a conversation. The
   manager gets to be a stakeholder, not a victim.

The conversation is 30 minutes. The script above gets you most
of the way. The remaining 10 minutes are Q&A — the manager
will have questions, and you answer them honestly.

## 5. The 3 things that get a transfer approved

After the manager conversation, the actual transfer has to be
approved by a hiring manager, a recruiter, and (sometimes) a
calibration committee. The 3 things that get it approved:

1. **A clear "why this team, why now" story.** Not "I want to
   try something new." Instead: "I've been working on X for N
   years, and the team working on Y is the natural next step
   for me. Here's the specific problem I'd work on, and here's
   why I think I'd be good at it."

2. **Evidence of senior+ scope in your current role.** A
   promotion or transfer is not the time to start building
   your case for senior+ scope. It should already be in your
   current role: a project you led, a system you owned, a
   cross-team initiative, mentorship of juniors. The interview
   will probe these.

3. **A clean transition plan for your current work.** "I'll
   wrap up X by date Y, hand off Z to person P, and document
   the rest in our runbook." Managers block transfers when
   they're worried about their team's continuity. The clean
   transition plan answers that concern.

## 6. Worked example: IC → Staff data platform

**The candidate:** Senior DE, 4 years at a mid-size tech
company, currently on the analytics team, targeting the data
platform team.

**The script (paraphrased):**

> *"I want to talk to you about my next step. I've been on
> the analytics team for 3 years and I've grown a lot — led
> the dbt migration, owned the data quality work, mentored 2
> juniors to mid-level. I'm thinking about what's next, and
> I want to be transparent with you about it.*
>
> *Specifically, I'm interested in the data platform team.
> The work they're doing on the streaming infrastructure is
> the kind of problem I want to spend the next few years on,
> and I think I have the right background — Kafka, Spark,
> production on-call — to be useful there.*
>
> *I want to ask: what do you think? Is this a good move? And
> if so, what would you want from me in terms of timing and
> transition plan? I'd rather have you as an advocate than
> have you find out from someone else."*

**What happens next (in the good case):**

- Manager says: "Let me think about it for a week."
- A week later, manager says: "I think it makes sense. Let's
  talk about a transition plan."
- Hiring manager on data platform team hears about you
  through your manager or your skip-level.
- Internal interview loop (2-3 rounds, lighter than external).
- Offer at the staff level (or senior, depending on scope).

**The bad case (and how to avoid it):**

- Manager says: "I can't release you, we have too much going
  on." This is a *signal*, not a no. The signal is: your
  current work is in a critical state, or your manager doesn't
  see you as senior+. The fix is to wait 3-6 months, build
  the senior+ signal, and try again.
- Manager says: "I can release you but not for 6 months."
  This is *fine*. 6 months is normal. Use the 6 months to
  ship the senior+ work that you'll need for the staff-level
  loop.

## 7. The "internal vs. external" decision

The internal transition is easier, but not always better. The
trade-offs:

| Factor | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| **Difficulty** | 3-5x easier | Baseline |
| **Comp upside** | Modest (within band) | Larger (new band) |
| **Scope change** | Smaller (same company) | Larger (new company) |
| **Reputation risk** | Higher (you live with these people) | Lower (clean slate) |
| **Speed** | 1-3 months | 3-6 months |

The right answer depends on your goals. If your goal is *level
+ scope* at a company you already like, internal is the move.
If your goal is *comp + scope* at a new company, external. If
your goal is *level + comp + scope*, external.

The most common senior+ mistake: doing internal transitions
when the right move is external. After 2-3 internal moves,
the comp and scope are both capped by the company's leveling.
External is the only way to break out.

## Try it

If you're currently employed at a company with multiple data
teams, do the following this week:

1. **Identify 1 specific team** you'd consider transferring to.
2. **Read the team's recent work** (blog posts, internal
   repos, manager's all-hands decks).
3. **Write a 3-sentence "why this team" pitch.** This is your
   private draft — you don't share it yet.
4. **Schedule a 30-min conversation with your manager** using
   the script in Section 4. If the manager conversation goes
   well, you have a transfer in flight. If it doesn't, you
   have information about your current situation.

If you're not currently employed or your company doesn't have
mobility programs, this lesson still applies — the same
script works for "I want to move from my current contract role
to a full-time role" or "I want to move from a non-data team
to a data team." The mechanics are the same.
