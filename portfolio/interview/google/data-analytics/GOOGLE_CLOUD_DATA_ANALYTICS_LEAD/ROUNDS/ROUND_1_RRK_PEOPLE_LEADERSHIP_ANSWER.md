# RRK: People Leadership of Senior Talent

**Question:** One senior consultant is missing deadlines and the customer is unhappy. Another strong performer wants a promotion. The team is burnt out. How do you lead?
**Follow-up:** A senior says your design is wrong in front of the customer.
**Time:** about 5 minutes, then 1 minute for the follow-up.
**Pass signal:** coaching, clear objectives, accountability, and a link between the three problems (they may share a cause).

---

## 1. Short answer (the version that was supplied, kept for reference)

Underperformer: private 1:1, specific examples, ask about the blocker (skill, workload, motivation), 30-day plan with measurable goals, weekly check, mentor pairing, formal process if no improvement. Strong performer: stretch (lead a workstream, pre-sales exposure, certification path), promote in 9 months. Burnout: rotation, clear objectives, no 100% utilisation. Philosophy: stay technically credible, hands-on, code reviews, whiteboard, get Google Cloud certified early. Follow-up: acknowledge, park, discuss privately with evidence, decide, present a united front.

Good skeleton. Fixes needed:
- **"Promote in 9 months" is a promise you can't keep.** Promotion is decided by evidence against the level criteria and a calibration panel. Say "here are the criteria and the gap; we review in [3 to 6] months", not a date.
- **The three problems are probably connected** (burnout may be why deadlines are slipping). A Lead says so.
- **The customer comes first.** Protect them before the coaching plan starts.
- **Certification needs a date.** "Early" is not a plan.

---

## 2. Improved answer

**Restate (10 s):** "Three people issues at once: a senior missing deadlines with an unhappy customer, a strong performer asking for promotion, and a burnt-out team. My first hypothesis is that they're linked, so I'll diagnose before I act."

### Step 0. Protect the customer (day 1)
Talk to the customer sponsor, own the problem, and agree a short recovery commitment. Re-balance the work so a deadline doesn't depend on one person. Customer trust is the first fire, and it must not wait for a coaching plan.

### Step 1. Diagnose the whole team (week 1)
Check utilisation, hours worked and after-hours load by person, work allocation (who is carrying what), and delivery data. I'm asking: is the missed-deadline consultant **overloaded**, **under-skilled for the task**, **disengaged**, or affected by **something outside work**? Burnout and underperformance often look the same from outside.

### Step 2. The underperforming senior
- **Private 1:1,** early, direct and kind: "Here's what I'm seeing, with these specific examples. What's your view? What's in the way?"
- **Diagnose:** skill, will or situation, and listen for blockers I own (unclear scope, too many projects, an unreasonable customer).
- **Plan:** 30/60/90 days, a few measurable goals (e.g. deliverables on date, design reviews passed, customer feedback), a weekly check-in, a mentor or pairing, and written notes after each meeting. I remove the blockers I can remove.
- **Accountability:** if there is no improvement despite real support, move to a formal process with HR. Fair, documented, dignified, with no surprises. Protect the customer and the team meanwhile.
- **If the cause turns out to be overload,** the fix is workload, not performance management. I'd say so.

### Step 3. The strong performer who wants promotion
- Thank them and take the ambition seriously.
- Map their work against the **next level's written criteria** (scope, influence, design ownership, coaching). Show two or three specific gaps with evidence.
- Design a **stretch** that exercises exactly those gaps: lead a workstream, run a customer design review, support a pre-sales scoping, mentor a junior, take the Google Cloud certification with a booked date.
- Name a sponsor, agree a review in [3 to 6] months against the criteria, and **don't promise a date or outcome**. If they're a flight risk, address recognition and scope now.

### Step 4. Burnout
- **Immediate:** cap utilisation (no sustained 100%), clear weekend and after-hours expectations, protect leave, and take the most draining work off the most stretched people.
- **Structural:** rotation between repetitive and design work, backfill or partner capacity, saying no to or re-scoping work I can't staff, and a simple health signal (hours worked, check-ins, anonymous pulse).
- **Model it:** I take leave and don't send messages at midnight.

### Step 5. Link them
"If the underperformer and the burnt-out team share a cause, such as too many engagements and too little capacity, then fixing capacity and prioritisation does more than any performance plan. I'd review my own planning first."

### Philosophy (3 sentences, in your own words)
1. Set a clear bar and context, then get out of the way.
2. Stay hands-on enough to be credible: review designs and code, draw on the whiteboard, hold the architecture line.
3. Measure myself by what the team can do without me. **Name the Google Cloud certification and a booked date.**

---

## 3. Follow-up: "A senior says your design is wrong in front of the customer."

**Answer:** "In the moment, I stay calm, and I **don't defend**. I say: 'That's a fair challenge; let's test it.' If their point has merit, I say so in the room. If it's unclear, I park it: 'Let's validate and come back to you tomorrow.' That protects the customer's confidence by showing we challenge ourselves. Afterwards, privately, I go through their evidence. If they're right, I change the design and **credit them publicly**. If I'm right, I explain with evidence, and I ask them to raise concerns before customer sessions next time. Either way, we leave with one position and we present it united. I'd also treat it as a signal: if a senior felt they had to say it in front of the customer, I need a better internal design review and a safer place to disagree."

## 4. Pass checklist

- [ ] Customer protected first
- [ ] Diagnose before acting; see the link between the three problems
- [ ] Underperformer: specific examples, blockers (skill, will, situation), measurable plan, support, a formal path if needed
- [ ] Promotion: criteria, gap, stretch, sponsor, **no promised date**
- [ ] Burnout: concrete utilisation cap, rotation, capacity, and leading by example
- [ ] Philosophy with a dated certification plan
- [ ] Follow-up: no defending, no humiliating, credit if right, united front
- [ ] One real example from your history (STAR), including something you would do differently

## 5. Likely probes

| Probe | One-line answer |
|---|---|
| "What if they say they're fine but the work says otherwise?" | I stay with facts: examples, impact, expectations, and a plan with checkpoints. |
| "What if the strong performer leaves anyway?" | I make sure it isn't because I ignored them; I'd have a succession plan and an honest exit conversation. |
| "How do you avoid favouring the strong performer?" | Written criteria, calibration, and the same process for everyone. |
| "When do you decide to part ways?" | When the gap persists after clear expectations and real support, and the customer or team is at risk. |
| "How do you stay technically credible?" | I review designs and code, run whiteboard sessions, and hold a dated Google Cloud certification plan. |
