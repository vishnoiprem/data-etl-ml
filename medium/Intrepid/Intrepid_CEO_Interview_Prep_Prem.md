## 🎯 What a CEO Round Actually Tests
Not architecture depth — that's for the tech round. A CEO is asking four silent questions:
1. **Will this person move the business?** (revenue, margin, efficiency — not dashboards)
2. **Can they think strategically, not just execute?**
3. **Can they lead and hire in a fast, ambiguous, scale-up environment?**
4. **Do I want them in my leadership team?** (culture, energy, judgment)

**Golden rule: translate every technical answer into money or speed.** GMV uplift, marketing efficiency, cost reduction, automation rate — the JD literally names these metrics. Use their language.

# SECTION 1 — VISION & STRATEGY QUESTIONS

## Q1: "What's your vision for our data and AI platform?"
> "I'd frame it as four stages, and I'd be honest that most companies stall at stage two.
>
> **Reporting** — dashboards people pull. Necessary, but passive.
> **Insights** — the system tells you what changed and why, without being asked. Anomaly detection, automated narratives.
> **Actions** — recommendations pushed to the person who can act, in their workflow. Not a dashboard they must remember to open.
> **Autonomy** — the system optimizes within guardrails: pricing, bidding, inventory.
>
> The mistake I'd avoid is jumping to stage four for the headline. Autonomous decisions require trust, and trust requires a governed semantic layer where 'GMV' means one thing company-wide. So my first 90 days would be about the foundation and one or two visible wins — not a two-year platform rebuild nobody sees.
>
> For Intrepid specifically, the highest-value early moves I'd explore: anomaly detection on campaign and marketplace performance so account teams find problems before clients do, and NL2SQL self-serve so business teams stop queuing behind the data team."

## Q2: "How do you decide what to build first?"
> "Two axes: business value and time-to-proof. I want early wins that are visible to the business and build credibility for the bigger platform work.
>
> Concretely, I'd spend the first weeks with commercial and client-facing teams asking what decisions they make weekly and what data they wish they had. The answers usually cluster fast. Then I'd pick something with clear money attached — say, ROAS optimization or inventory forecasting — where I can show a measurable delta in one quarter.
>
> I did this at Makro: the month-end close automation was chosen deliberately because it was painful, visible, and quantifiable. Cutting five days to two hours bought me the political capital to fund the broader platform."

## Q3: "Where does AI genuinely create advantage for a business like ours?"
> "In ecommerce and social commerce, I'd point at four:
>
> **Speed of reaction** — anomaly detection on campaigns and listings. Catching a broken listing or a collapsing ROAS in hours instead of at month-end is direct margin.
> **Scale of judgment** — you manage many brands across six countries and multiple platforms. Humans can't watch everything; models can. That's leverage without linear headcount.
> **Self-serve** — NL2SQL and copilots remove the data team as a bottleneck. I've built exactly this.
> **Client-facing differentiation** — this is the one I'd push hardest. Intelligence you can expose *to clients* — predictive insights, scenario planning — isn't just internal efficiency, it's a reason brands choose you over a competitor and pay more.
>
> That last one turns the data team from a cost centre into part of the product."

## Q4: "What would you do in your first 90 days?"
> "**Days 1–30 — listen and diagnose.** Meet business, product, and engineering leaders. Understand what decisions get made and where data fails them. Audit the current stack — BigQuery, StarRocks, Airflow, DBT, Cube, Superset — for quality, cost, and trust. Find out which numbers people argue about, because that tells you where the semantic layer is broken.
>
> **Days 30–60 — sequence and commit.** Publish a roadmap with a clear stage-by-stage story, agree the impact metrics with you and the business, and start one quick win in parallel.
>
> **Days 60–90 — deliver something visible.** Ship the first win, get the semantic layer standardised on core metrics, and lay foundations for the AI layer.
>
> I'd rather show one real result in 90 days than a beautiful architecture diagram."

# SECTION 2 — BUSINESS IMPACT QUESTIONS

## Q5: "How do you measure the data team's success?"
> "I'm allergic to vanity metrics — number of dashboards built, tickets closed. Those measure activity, not value.
>
> I'd measure three things:
> **Business outcomes** — GMV uplift, marketing efficiency, cost reduction, automation rate. The JD names these, and I'd want them agreed with the business so we're accountable to the same number.
> **Adoption** — is anyone actually using what we built? A model nobody acts on is worth zero. I learned this the hard way.
> **Platform health** — cost per query, pipeline reliability, data freshness. Unsexy but it's what stops trust eroding.
>
> At Makro I deliberately set my team's KPIs against business outcomes rather than engineering output. It changes behaviour — engineers start asking 'will anyone use this?' before building."

## Q6: "Data teams are expensive. How do you justify the investment?"
> "By tying every major initiative to a number the business already cares about, before we build it.
>
> I've owned a $3 million budget, so I'm used to defending spend. Three levers: cost efficiency on the platform itself — query optimization, storage tiering, right-sizing, which is very real on BigQuery where costs scale with scanned data; automation that removes manual effort — the finance close I automated was five days of people's time every month; and revenue-side impact — pricing, forecasting, ROAS.
>
> I'd also be honest that not everything is directly attributable. Some foundational work is an enabler. My approach is to fund foundations *through* visible wins rather than asking for a blank cheque up front."

## Q7: "How do you translate data work for non-technical stakeholders?"
> "I don't talk about pipelines, I talk about decisions.
>
> Example: I don't say 'we built a real-time streaming platform on Kafka and Flink.' I say 'operations can now see where every delivery is and reroute it, instead of finding out yesterday's problem tomorrow.' Same system, but one of those sentences a commercial leader can act on.
>
> I also write publicly on Medium about data architecture and leadership, which forces the discipline of explaining complex things simply."

# SECTION 3 — LEADERSHIP & CULTURE QUESTIONS

## Q8: "How do you build and lead a data team?"
> "I've scaled an organisation from 8 to 30 people across four domain squads — Finance, Supply Chain, O2O, and Platform. Structure matters: I organise by business domain rather than by technology, so each squad owns outcomes for a part of the business and builds real domain knowledge, with a platform squad underneath so they're not each reinventing infrastructure.
>
> On hiring, in a scale-up I'd rather hire fewer, stronger, versatile people than many specialists. And I look for people who ask 'why' about the business, not just 'what' about the ticket.
>
> On leading — I set direction and outcomes, then delegate the how. I stay technical enough to review architecture and challenge decisions, but I'm not the bottleneck."

## Q9: "This is a fast-paced, ambiguous, high-growth environment. How do you operate in that?"
> "I've spent my career in it — Lazada during Alibaba's SEA expansion was about as fast and ambiguous as it gets, and at Makro I was building the data function while simultaneously running it.
>
> My approach in ambiguity: get to a decision quickly with 70-80% information, make it reversible where possible, and be willing to be wrong publicly. The bigger risk in a fast business isn't a wrong decision, it's no decision.
>
> And I'd expect the roadmap to change. I'd rather commit to outcomes for the quarter and stay flexible on how we get there."

## Q10: "What kind of leader are you? / How would your team describe you?"
> "Hands-on but not controlling. I'll sit with an engineer on a hard architecture problem, but I don't want to be the person every decision routes through.
>
> I think my team would say I'm demanding about standards but clear about why — and that I care whether what we build actually gets used. I set KPIs tied to business outcomes precisely so the team sees the point of the work.
>
> I've also grown people — that's the part I find most satisfying. Scaling 8 to 30 wasn't just hiring, it was developing leads who could run their own squads."

## Q11: "Tell me about a failure."
> "I led a forecasting model that tested well and underperformed in production. Two reasons, both mine. The historical data had quality gaps my curated test set hid. And more importantly, I built it without deeply involving the business users who had to act on it — so when its recommendations conflicted with their intuition, they ignored it. A model nobody uses is worth nothing.
>
> I changed permanently after that. Now I validate data readiness first, and I involve the business owner from problem framing, not handover. I applied that directly to the dynamic pricing model at Makro — framed it with the commercial team from day one, drove adoption with merchandising as part of the project. That one landed and influenced pricing across the catalogue.
>
> The general lesson: in data and AI, adoption risk is bigger than technical risk, and it's the one people forget to plan for."

# SECTION 4 — LIKELY CURVEBALLS

## Q12: "Your background is retail and logistics, not agency/ecommerce services. Why does that transfer?"
> "The domain differs but the shape is the same. At Makro I ran O2O — online and offline commerce merged, unified customer data, campaign activation, marketplace-style catalogue problems. At Lazada I was inside one of SEA's largest ecommerce businesses, dealing with marketplace scale and logistics. I built item matching across online and offline channels, which is exactly the catalogue-intelligence problem you have across marketplaces.
>
> What's new for me is the agency and multi-client dimension — serving brands rather than a single P&L. That's a real difference and I'd want to learn it fast. But multi-tenancy, multi-country, multi-platform data is a problem I know well — I've built for three countries at Makro and multiple markets at Lazada."

## Q13: "We use BigQuery, StarRocks, DBT, Cube. You've used Databricks, Spark, Alibaba Cloud. Concern?"
> "Not really, and I'd say that carefully. The tools differ; the principles don't. I've worked multi-cloud — AWS, Azure, Alibaba — and the hard problems are always the same: modelling, governance, cost, trust, and adoption.
>
> BigQuery I'd get productive on quickly — it's a columnar warehouse with the same cost dynamics I manage today, where scanned data drives spend. DBT and Airflow I know conceptually and would be hands-on within weeks. Cube as a semantic layer is interesting to me because I built a semantic layer for the Text-to-SQL platform at Makro — I understand *why* it matters, which is harder than learning the tool.
>
> Where I'd add value immediately is the AI layer, which is where you're heading, not where the stack is today."

## Q14: "How would you approach agentic AI here — realistically?"
> "Carefully, and with guardrails, because I've built this.
>
> My Text-to-SQL platform taught me the hard part isn't the model, it's everything around it — a semantic layer so the agent knows what your metrics mean, validation so a wrong query doesn't reach production data, and clear limits on what it's allowed to do.
>
> So for agentic here, I'd start narrow and high-value: an agent that monitors campaign and marketplace performance, detects anomalies, explains the likely cause, and *recommends* an action — with a human approving it. Then, once we've measured that its recommendations are right often enough, widen the guardrails to let it act autonomously in bounded areas like bid adjustments.
>
> Autonomy is earned through demonstrated accuracy, not switched on because it's exciting."

## Q15: "Why do you want to leave Makro / why this role?"
> "I've built what I set out to build at Makro — scaled the org from 8 to 30, modernised the platform, delivered the AI roadmap. It's in a strong place.
>
> What draws me here is that Intrepid is explicitly trying to make the leap I care most about — from reporting to autonomous intelligence — in ecommerce, at regional scale, with data across marketplaces, creators, and marketing that's genuinely rich. And I'd be building the intelligence backbone of the business rather than a supporting function. That's a bigger swing than optimizing what I've already built."
