# PART 2 — SUNEY'S QUESTIONS (Technical)

### S1. "Bank on Oracle and Teradata. Board wants GenAI. Walk me through that conversation."
"First I'd slow it down, because the board's ask and the real problem are different things.

I'd ask four diagnostic questions: how many copies of your data exist today; how long from a business question to an answer; who governs row and column level access; and when does the Oracle renewal land — because that's the urgency lever.

Then I'd name the failure mode honestly: GenAI dies without a data foundation. I've seen it. The model is the easy part.

Then I'd sequence it, not big-bang. Land the data in S3 as open Iceberg tables. Unify lake and warehouse with SageMaker Lakehouse so there's one governed copy. Lake Formation for row and column governance — non-negotiable for a bank. Zero-ETL to cut the pipeline sprawl. Then Bedrock and Amazon Q on top of a foundation that can actually answer.

On migration: Teradata to Redshift, Oracle to Aurora PostgreSQL — that kills the licensing without throwing away their SQL skills. And parallel-run, never a big-bang cutover for a bank.

Finally I'd build the CFO's case: licence savings plus time-to-insight, not just a tech story."


Sure. Here it is. First, I'd focus on the business need, not on migration. I'd ask what GenAI use case they're after, who the users are, what data is needed, and what outcome they're expecting. Only then would I talk about the current state on Teradata and what data is actually required. Then I'd propose a phased approach. For example, land the GenAI dataset into S3 using open table formats like Iceberg, build the GenAI app with Bedrock. and keep the core stable while modernizing over time. That gives them quick wins and a longer-term transformation path. The key message is that you're solving their business problem first, delivering value now, then building a trusted path forward. That's how I'd frame it. Gentle, clear, customer first.



### S2. "What GenAI have you personally shipped, and what broke?"
"I built an internal GenAI assistant so engineering and business users could query enterprise knowledge in natural language.

I designed the ingestion pipeline, built the document processing, implemented chunking and embedding generation, created the vector index, and integrated the retrieval layer with the model. I also owned CI/CD, monitoring and deployment.

The hard part wasn't the LLM — it was the data. Users got inconsistent answers because documents were duplicated, outdated, or had no metadata, so retrieval returned irrelevant chunks.

I fixed it at the data layer: document versioning, metadata tagging, better chunking, hybrid search combining vector and keyword, and automated quality checks before indexing. I added prompt templates, citations and a feedback loop so users could verify answers.

The second issue was latency and cost. I tuned retrieval to return only the most relevant context and cached common queries.

That build was on Azure OpenAI — the AWS equivalent maps cleanly: Bedrock for the model layer, OpenSearch Serverless or Aurora pgvector for the vector store, S3 for documents, Textract for extraction. Same architecture, and the retrieval and guardrail problems are identical either way.

The outcome: business users started self-serving questions that used to come to my team as tickets — the ticket volume largely dried up. And the lesson was that GenAI succeeds on data foundations and production engineering, not on the model."

### S3. "Customer's standardised on Databricks, team loves it, won't move. 20 minutes with the CDO."
"First, I'm not walking in there to move them off Databricks. That's the fastest way to lose a CDO's trust — and Databricks runs on AWS anyway, so they're already our customer.

So I'd mostly ask. Three questions: where does your data actually live, what are you paying to store it and move copies around, and where do you feel locked in? Because the pain is almost never the compute engine their team loves. It's the cost of copying data everywhere and being tied to one proprietary format.

Then one idea, not a rebuild: keep Databricks, but put the data in open Iceberg tables on S3. One governed copy that Databricks, Athena and SageMaker can all read. Lower storage cost, no lock-in, future-proofed — without touching the tool the team loves.

And I'd land it exactly like that: I'm not here to replace what's working, I'm here to make it cost less and lock you in less."

### S4. "Explain the AWS data stack as you'd explain it to a CDO."
"Four phases, and I'd draw it.

**Ingest** — Kinesis or MSK for streaming, and Zero-ETL to replicate from operational databases automatically. That one gives engineering headcount back.

**Store** — S3 as the foundation, in open formats: Parquet and Iceberg. S3 Tables when you need Iceberg with ACID. Redshift as the warehouse — and the natural Teradata replacement. SageMaker Lakehouse is the piece I'd emphasise: it unifies lake and warehouse into one governed copy, open Iceberg underneath, serving both analytics and AI.

**Process and govern** — Glue for ETL and the catalog, EMR for heavy Spark, and Lake Formation for row, column and cell-level governance. In financial services, Lake Formation is the door-opener.

**Consume** — Athena for serverless ad-hoc SQL, pay per scan. Redshift for high-concurrency BI. QuickSight for dashboards. SageMaker for ML. Bedrock for GenAI and RAG. Amazon Q for natural-language querying.

The point I'd make to a CDO: one copy, open format, governed once, and every consumption pattern reads from it."

### S5. "What's the hardest technical problem you've solved?"
"AML at Standard Chartered. I built entity resolution and fuzzy matching across more than fifteen countries under MAS compliance.

The hard part was that the same customer appeared differently in every system and every country — name transliterations, address formats, no common key. And in AML a false negative is a regulatory event, while too many false positives buries the investigations team.

I built fuzzy matching and entity resolution to link identities across those systems, with a tuned threshold and an audit trail for every decision, because a regulator will ask you to justify any single match.

It taught me something I use constantly: in regulated data, governance and explainability are first-order design constraints, not features you bolt on later. That's why I lead with Lake Formation when I talk to a bank."

### S8. "Batch vs real-time — when do you actually recommend streaming?"

I'd tell a customer the same: streaming costs more to build and operate, so justify it with a decision that can't wait. Most 'real-time' requirements are actually 'fresher than daily'."
